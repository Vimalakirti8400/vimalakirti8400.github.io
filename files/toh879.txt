The Hundred and Eight Names of Noble Mañjuśrī Kumārabhūta, together with the Dhāraṇī-Mantra, known in Tibetan as Pakpa Jampal Shönnur Gyurpé Tsen Gyatsa Gyepa Zungngak Dang Chepa and corresponding to a Sanskrit title along the lines of Ārya Mañjuśrī Kumārabhūta Aṣṭottaraśataka Nāma Dhāraṇī Mantra Sahita, is a short devotional and ritual text centered on the bodhisattva of wisdom, Mañjuśrī, here called Kumārabhūta, "the Youthful," and Mañjughoṣa, "Gentle Voice." It opens with homages to the fully awakened buddhas of the ten directions, whose pure intelligence pervades all that can be known, and to Mañjuśrī himself, whose stainless mind and radiant qualities draw the reverence of gods, humans, asuras, nāgas, and vidyādharas, and who is praised as one who destroys rebirth in the lower realms. It then presents a Sanskrit dhāraṇī-mantra that invokes him through a long series of epithets, celebrating him as jewel-adorned, as a liberator of other beings, and as the holder of the great treasury of the Tathāgata's teaching. The work is Toh 879 in the Kangyur, where it belongs among the dhāraṇī texts, the collection of incantations and name-recitations prized for their protective and purifying power.

This vidyā-mantra that accomplishes every aim,
At once, for sentient beings,
Birth and aging came to an end.
All the lower destinies were pacified,

All the gods rejoiced exceedingly,

The very moment this dhāraṇī

Countless beings, in hundreds of millions,

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The verse of praise continues into the next piece. "Prajāpati" ("Lord of Creatures") is an epithet applied here to Mañjughoṣa. The line rendered "Knowing restraint, guarding well, knowing shame" might alternatively be parsed as "knowing restraint, guarding wisdom, knowing shame."  In the line rendered "Attended at his feet by hosts of worshippers," the Tibetan reads rjed pa ("forgetting"), which makes no sense here. It is translated as if it were a word for reverence or worship, matching the later line about offerings gathered at his feet. The phrase rendered "in gentle recitation" (Tibetan 'jam pa'i klog) may be a slip for 'jam pa'i dbyangs. The instrumental particle would support that reading, and the line would then say "proclaimed by Mañjughoṣa." The line "Yogin who surely conquers great fervor" renders an obscure compound (tshan chen), and its sense is uncertain.
