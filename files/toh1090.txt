Praise of the Mistress of Diseases

The Praise of the Mistress of Diseases, known in Tibetan as Nad kyi bdag mo la bstod pa, is a short devotional hymn addressed to a fierce goddess who presides over illness. Her iconography closely recalls the Indian smallpox goddess often called Śītalā, though no Sanskrit title is securely attested here. In vivid verse, the hymn describes her anointed with sesame oil, wearing a single braid and earrings of lead, naked and riding a donkey through the world. It also shows her as a terrifying, skull-bearing Black One who swallows all beings at the dissolution of saṃsāra and who delights in charnel grounds as the domain of accomplished yogins. Yet it praises her as both peaceful and terrible, a deity who crushes the deceitful and dispels the fear of disease and death, so that the poem serves as a protective invocation as well as an expression of awe. The work is Toh 1090 in the Kangyur, where it belongs among the brief hymns, dhāraṇīs, and related ritual texts gathered within the tantric portion of the collection.

Praise of the Mistress of Diseases.

Anointed with sesame oil, wearing a single braid, charming with earrings of lead, you bear every adornment.
As an ornament, a single fetter made of iron makes your own foot beautiful.
Naked, riding a donkey, you roam through this world; you yourself, O goddess,
know yourself to be beautiful, but who else could know the meaning of your conduct, vast as a great ocean?

When saṃsāra is destroyed, when blood and fat drip from human bones and smoke billows forth,
O goddess of unbearable fangs, all beings in a single instant enter into your mouth.

Black One holding a human skull, your domain is that of accomplished yogins, and you delight in charnel grounds.
You crush the deceitful and dispel the fear of disease and death; peaceful yet terrible, you also sound the bell.

You bear a garland of heroes' heads, finely adorned with crowns, severed by weapons in battle;
dancing, your supple hands outstretched, broad of hips, with lords of the nāgas strung together
you bind your girdle; holding spear and sword in your hands, drunk on the wine of blood,
your eyes bloodshot, going by night to the charnel ground, you are seen by the great hosts of bhūtas.

With a garment of fresh hide, rolled and stretched, half your lower body is covered, and half is wrapped in coarse cloth.
With the hollow tubes of fresh human entrails you adorn the moon of your face, and bells swing at your cheeks.
Your body is freshly smeared with grease, and dotted in clusters with drops of dripping blood and fat.
You who gnash and champ with teeth on human corpses, bearer of the skull cup, may you protect me!

Fierce one among the fierce, terrible, holding human flesh in your mouth, unlike anything before: when they see you,
even the mātṛkās take fright, and in their haste, awestruck, with darting eyes, they bow to you.

Black One, Bhadrā, dark of complexion and hard to bear: to you even Śakra and the others
offer praise day after day, and Yama himself, trembling, ever bows to you.

On one ear, as an ornament, you have placed the resting place of Viṣṇu,
the thousand-hooded one that goes upon its breast, who humbles humans and all beings.
On the other you hold a peerless lion, and you press down the earth encircled by the ocean.
The thousand-rayed sun that travels overhead you have placed at the region of your navel.

You are the fire blazing from the ocean's depths,
able to dry up the waters.
For the sake of pacifying all women,
you dwell in half the body of Mahādeva.

Escort, Durgā, Mahārātri,
you to whom gods and asuras bow,
you know whatever human diseases there are;
from them there will be no fear.

Sister of Yama, mother of Māra,
sovereign lady of the desire realm,
mistress of all diseases:
the eighth Praise of the Great Goddess, composed by the brahmin Supreme Longing, is complete.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The author's name is given in Tibetan as "mchog sred." I could not identify the Sanskrit form, so I have translated its meaning as "Supreme Longing." In the closing verse, "rdzongs" is unclear as an epithet, and I have rendered it literally as "Escort." "Nag mo bzang mo" may stand for the single name Bhadrakālī. "The resting place of Viṣṇu," the thousand-hooded serpent, is presumably Śeṣa (Ananta). Mahādeva dwelling in half the body refers to the goddess as the half-female form of Śiva (Ardhanārīśvara). "Mahārātri," the Great Night, is a known epithet of the Goddess. "Bstod pa brgyad pa" is taken as an ordinal: this hymn is the eighth in a series of praises.
