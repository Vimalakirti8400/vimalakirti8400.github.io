The True Names of Mañjuśrī, the Wisdom Being, in Their Ultimate Sense — a Song of Praise (Mañjuśrījñānasattvasya paramārthanāmasaṃgīti)

Toh 360 in the Kangyur is the Mañjuśrī-jñānasattvasya-paramārthanāma-saṃgīti, known in Tibetan as the Jampal Yeshe Sempe Döndam Tshen Yangdakpar Jöpa, and rendered in English as "The True Names of Mañjuśrī, the Wisdom Being, in Their Ultimate Sense — a Song of Praise." It is a celebrated tantric praise-hymn, or nāmasaṃgīti, devoted to the bodhisattva Mañjuśrī in his aspect as the embodiment of non-dual wisdom, and it recounts his names and attributes in verse as a means of conveying the deepest nature of awakened mind. The text opens with the great Vajrapāṇi, lord of secret mantra, addressing the Buddha and requesting the teaching, establishing the work firmly within the Vajrayāna liturgical and doctrinal tradition. Widely regarded as one of the most important texts in Indo-Tibetan tantric Buddhism, it sits within the tantra section of the Kangyur and has generated an exceptionally rich commentarial literature across multiple schools of Tibetan Buddhism.

In the language of India: Mañjuśrī-jñānasattvasya paramārthanāmasaṃgīti. In the language of Tibet: The True Names of Mañjuśrī, the Wisdom Being, in Their Ultimate Sense — a Song of Praise.

Homage to Mañjuśrī, the Ever-Youthful.

Then the glorious Vajradhara,
supreme tamer of the difficult to tame,
the hero victorious over all three worlds,
the lord of vajra power, king of secrets,
with eyes wide-open like full-blown white lotuses,
his face like a fully blossomed lotus,
wielding the supreme vajra in his own hand,
brandishing it again and again,
with his waves of fierce, furrowed-browed wrath and so forth,
Vajrapāṇi, boundless in his qualities,
hero and tamer of the difficult to tame,
heroic one of awesome form,
he whose vajra tips blaze with radiance,
who wields it in his own hand,
supreme among those who accomplish the welfare of beings
through great wisdom, skillful means, and compassion,
endowed with a mind of delight and joy,
bearing the form of a wrathful body,
the protector who performs the enlightened activity of the buddhas —
together with those who had bowed their bodies down,
he prostrated to the protector, the Blessed One, the Tathāgata,
the perfectly awakened buddha,
and having joined his palms together,
sat in his presence and spoke these words:

"O all-pervading Lord, for the sake of healing me,
and out of your loving care for my welfare,
please grant me the perfectly accomplished awakening
of the net of magical display, just as you yourself have attained it.
And in order to heal all sentient beings
whose minds are disturbed by afflictions
and who have sunk into the mire of ignorance,
and so that they may attain the unsurpassed fruit,
may you, the Blessed One, the Teacher, the perfectly awakened buddha,
the supreme guide of beings, please speak.

You who know the great pledge in its reality,
supreme lord who perceives the intentions of sentient beings,
the wisdom body of the Blessed One,
the sovereign of the words of the great uṣṇīṣa,
the wisdom body of Mañjuśrī, the wisdom being,
self-arisen —
profound in meaning, vast in meaning,
supremely great in meaning, incomparable, utterly peaceful,
wholesome at the beginning, the middle, and the end —
the supreme genuine proclaiming of the names:
the buddhas of the past have spoken it,
those of the future too shall speak it,
and the perfectly awakened buddhas of the present
have spoken it again and again, whatever they have said.
In this great tantra, the net of magical causation,
the great Vajradhara, holder of secret mantra,
has with delight spoken it thoroughly and correctly,
countless though they are.

I too shall hold to this
with a mind firm in its resolve and certain in its going-forth.
How shall I, O protector, become the keeper of secrets
of all the perfectly awakened buddhas?
In order to dispel all afflictions without remainder
and to abandon all ignorance without remainder,
please, clearly explain to me in full
the distinctions among the intentions of sentient beings."

Thus did Vajrapāṇi, the lord of secrets, the greatly powerful one,
address the Tathāgata in those words,
having joined his palms together,
and sat with bowed body in his presence.

These were the sixteen verses of supplication.

Then the Blessed One, Śākyamuni,

long and broad —
illuminating all three worlds
and subduing the enemies that are the four māras,
and displaying a smile that purifies the three lower realms (naraka, preta, and animal) of beings,
filling all three worlds with his melodious Brahmā-voice,

the lord of secrets:

The title in Sanskrit is rendered as transliterated in the Tibetan dbu can script: "Mañjuśrī-jñānasattvasya paramārthanāmasaṃgīti," which is the standard Sanskrit title of the Nāmasaṃgīti, a foundational tantric praise text. The Tibetan rendering of the title is translated faithfully as "The True Names of Mañjuśrī, the Wisdom Being, in Their Ultimate Sense."

The phrase rendered "the net of magical display" translates "sgyu 'phrul dra ba," which corresponds to Sanskrit māyājāla, an important tantra cycle connected with Mañjuśrī in which the perfection of awakening is described as accomplished through magical net-like display — a technical reference to the Māyājālatantra.

The phrase "gtsug tor chen po'i tshig gi bdag" is rendered "sovereign of the words of the great uṣṇīṣa," referring to Mañjuśrī as identified with the uṣṇīṣa (the cranial protuberance, a mark of a great being), a standard epithet in the Nāmasaṃgīti tradition.

The closing lines of the passage are incomplete as the text breaks off mid-sentence; no translation of a complete thought was possible beyond what is given.  The mantra passage preserves the Sanskrit dhāraṇī syllables and the root mantra of Mañjuśrī in their original Sanskrit/Sanskrit-derived form, as is standard in canonical translation practice; they are not rendered into English.

The phrase rendered "the great offering of intense desire" and the parallel constructions that follow reflect a tantric exegetical convention in which the five poisons (desire, aversion, delusion, wrath, attachment) are recast as "great offerings" whose very intensity becomes the path; this is a standard Vajrayāna doctrinal point and is preserved literally here.

The term rendered "ushnīsha" (གཙུག་ཏོར་, Skt. uṣṇīṣa) refers to the cranial protuberance, one of the major marks of a great being, here used as a family designation in a tantric classificatory scheme of six families rather than the more common three or five.  The passage contains two distinct sections: a concluding portion of a eulogy in the dharmadhātu wisdom register, then a new section beginning with Vajrabhairava epithets. The colophon-like line "These are the twenty-five deficient-foot verses of the wisdom of the dharmadhātu, entirely pure" marks the end of the first section; the number twenty-five refers to a traditional syllable-count category used in Tibetan poetics.

The term rendered "non-returner" and "once-returner" translates the Tibetan ཕྱིར་མི་ལྡོག་པ and ཕྱིར་མི་འོང་, the standard Tibetan equivalents for the anāgāmin and sakṛdāgāmin respectively, the third and second of the four noble fruits of the śrāvaka path.

The phrase rendered "rhinoceros-like solitary traveler" renders བསེ་རུ་སོར་སོར་འདྲེན, a stock image for the pratyekabuddha (solitary buddha) who, like a rhinoceros, walks alone — an image derived from the Khaggaviṣāṇasutta.  The phrase "mgo skyes rdo rje 'bar ba" rendered as "hair-born blazing vajra" refers to Vajra born from the head or crown, a standard iconographic epithet, though some editions read this as referring to a being born or arising from the crown of the head in a tantric context.

The phrase "yig ge drug pa rdo rje'i sgra" rendered as "The syllable of six, the sound of vajra" refers to the six-syllable mantra Om Mani Padme Hūṃ, which is here identified with vajra-sound, though in context it may also indicate a six-syllabled seed-syllable specific to the deity addressed.

The phrase "gsum mtha' mtha' yas grol gsum pa" is syntactically compressed. It has been rendered as "liberated at the infinite extremes of the three — the third liberation," but the referent of "the three" is ambiguous; it may indicate the three realms, the three vehicles, or the three natures.

The passage ends with a section colophon indicating these ten verses belong to the mirror-like wisdom section, after which a new section begins. The structure of the larger text appears to organize verses according to the five wisdoms; this colophon marks the close of the first such group.  The epithet "glorious calf" rendered from "dpal gyi be'u" is an unusual image; in context it likely conveys the sense of a young radiant source of nourishment and brilliance, possibly echoing solar iconography. The exact import of "be'u" as calf or young animal in this liturgical context may warrant further commentary consultation.

The name "Jinajik" for "rdo rje zab mo dzin dzik" is a transliteration of what appears to be a Sanskrit name preserved in Tibetan phonetics, possibly Gajajit or a similar epithet; it is retained as a proper name pending identification of the Sanskrit original.

The phrase rendered "primordially possessing great attachment" from "chags bral dang po chags pa che" is doctrinally striking — the juxtaposition of freedom from attachment with great attachment likely reflects tantric non-dual language where ultimate non-attachment paradoxically encompasses all experience; this is a recognized rhetorical device in vajrayāna eulogy literature.  The passage belongs to a praise-text or enumeration of qualities, likely from a Vajrayāna context cataloguing the attributes of an enlightened being or a buddha. Several epithets are densely technical and semi-formulaic.

The phrase rendered "pressing the summit of the gate of Brahmā" refers to the brahmarandhra, the crown aperture, a well-attested tantric anatomical term; the Tibetan ཚངས་པའི་སྒོ is a standard rendering of this.

The lines concerning the bindu, letters, and the "half of half of sixteen" are technical Vajrayāna numerical-syllabic formulas. "Half of half of sixteen" yields four, likely referring to the four-dot bindu or a specific syllabic count in tantric esotericism. The precise referent is context-dependent and may vary by lineage.

"The great secret mantra of the three families" refers to the three buddha-families of body, speech, and mind, or alternatively the families of buddha, vajra, and padma, standard in Vajrayāna classification.

"The three meanings" in "the mind free of doubt regarding the three meanings" most likely refers to the three objects of definite knowledge standard in Madhyamaka and Abhidharma contexts, though the precise referent depends on the broader textual tradition.  The line rendered "attachment of buddhas" and "desire of buddhas" translates the Tibetan terms ཆགས་པ and འདོད respectively. In this tantric praise context, these are technical positive epithets signifying the buddha's blissful compassionate engagement and the wish-fulfilling quality of awakening rather than ordinary afflictive desire or attachment. The doctrinal point is intentional and belongs to the Vajrayāna register of this text.

The "three kinds of definite emergence" renders ངེས་འབྱུང་གསུམ, likely referring to the three vehicles and their respective paths of liberation.

The closing prose section shifts from verse into a formal statement by Vajrapāṇi, introducing the purpose of reciting these names. The syntactically open-ended final clause — "this too I have taught" — appears to be an incomplete sentence whose continuation follows in the next passage.  The phrase rendered "closing of all the doors to the lower destinies" translates a compound meaning literally "covering all the doors/gates of the lower rebirths," referring to the blocking of rebirth in hell, the animal realm, and the realm of hungry ghosts. This is a standard technical usage and no ambiguity is involved.

The phrase "freedom from all engagement for all who are perfectly engaged" renders a deliberate paradox in the original: those who are perfectly engaged in practice attain freedom from all compulsive engagement or grasping-attachment. This is doctrinally intentional and not a textual error.  The phrase rendered "perfectly attaining the gnosis body of Mañjuśrī" reflects a standard late-tantric formulation in which the recitation of Mañjuśrī's name confers his jñānakāya; no emendation is needed, but the phrasing is dense and the connection to the five eyes in the following clause follows a standard sequential listing rather than strict logical subordination.  The passage contains a long enumeration of divine beings. "Upendra" renders ཉེ་དབང་པོ, a common epithet for Viṣṇu as "lesser Indra." "Nārāyaṇa" renders སྲེད་མེད་ཀྱི་བུ, literally "son of the uncreated," a standard Tibetan rendering of Nārāyaṇa. "Kumāra" renders ཀུན་འགྱེད་གཞོན་ནུ, the ever-youthful dispenser, associated with Kumāra or Skanda. "Skanda" then renders སྨིན་དྲུག་གི་བུ, literally "son of the Pleiades," a transparent epithet of Skanda-Kārttikeya. "Nandīśvara" renders དགའ་བྱེད་དབང་ཕྱུག, "the lord who gives joy." "Pūtanā" renders ལུས་ངན, and "Hāritī" renders འཕྲོག་མ; the identification of these two is conventional in the Tibetan tradition for this list, though other identifications are possible. The passage ends mid-sentence in the source, indicating the list of activities continues in the next section.  The phrase rendered "the seventh mother-spirits" refers to the seven mātṛkās, a well-attested group of seven female spirit-beings in the Indian tradition; the enumeration here distinguishes them from the general category of mātṛkās mentioned just before.

The phrase "wearing it at the throat" reflects a common dhāraṇī practice of wearing a written text or amulet at the throat; this is a physical ritual instruction, not metaphorical.
