The Noble Hundred and Eight Names of Vajrapāṇi, Together with the Dhāraṇī-Mantra (Ārya Vajrapāṇi Nāmāṣṭaśataka Dhāraṇī Mantra Sahita)

The Noble Hundred and Eight Names of Vajrapāṇi, Together with the Dhāraṇī-Mantra, known in Tibetan as Phakpa Lakna Dorjé Tsen Gya Tsa Gyepa Zungngak Dang Chepa, is a short scripture devoted to the bodhisattva Vajrapāṇi, the fierce protector who wields the vajra. Its Sanskrit title is not securely attested, though it would correspond to something like Ārya Vajrapāṇi Nāma Aṣṭaśataka. The text opens with Vajrapāṇi proclaiming his own heart mantra, at which the earth shakes and obstructing spirits cry out in terror. The Buddha, smiling, gently urges him not to frighten the assembly too much, and Vajrapāṇi responds by praising his dhāraṇī as supremely secret and the highest of all knowledge-mantras. It is Toh 878 in the Kangyur, where it belongs among the dhāraṇī texts associated with the tantra section, works valued for the protective and purifying power of their recited formulas and names.

The Noble Hundred and Eight Names of Vajrapāṇi, Together with the Dhāraṇī-Mantra

Homage to the bodhisattva, the great being Vajrapāṇi.

Then the bodhisattva Vajrapāṇi spoke his own heart mantra:

oṃ vajra paṇḍa sarva duṣṭāṃ ta ka ha na da ha pa ca hūṃ phaṭ

As soon as this dhāraṇī-mantra had been spoken, the great earth shook, and all the obstructors and vināyakas were terrified and let out cries of distress. Everyone gathered in the great maṇḍala also exclaimed, "Ha ha!" The gods and the other great vidyādharas also let fall a great rain of ornaments.

Then the Blessed One, smiling, looked at Vajradhara and said, "Dispeller of the hostile, do not frighten this assembly too much!"

Vajradhara replied, "Blessed One, this dhāraṇī of mine is supremely secret. It is the highest of all vidyā-mantras. It averts all obstructors, dispels all who are hostile, bestows what is sublime, and bestows wealth. It makes the hostile become pleased. If it is heard, it brings benefit; if it is recited, it brings benefit; if it is honored and venerated, it brings benefit. It generates strength, makes one's form beautiful, produces eloquence, causes one to gain sovereignty, makes one wealthy, and brings under control. It crushes the hostile and destroys the hostile. It removes poison, brings victory, gives joy, gives supreme joy, and inspires faith. It conquers pride, defeats opponents, pacifies wrongdoing, purifies offenses that have been committed, and pacifies the afflictions such as desire. It averts the deeds of immediate retribution, pacifies bad dreams, pacifies what is inauspicious, increases glory, and brings good fortune. It delights all sentient beings, brings others under control, grants whatever is wished for, and perfects all good qualities. It thoroughly pacifies excessive rain, the failure of rain, and the harms of the seasons.

"When there is great war, great epidemic, great strife, danger from nonhumans and the like, hostility, curses, great vetāla rites, banishment, a time when one's life is in danger, or when great fear arises, one should rise at dawn, bathe, put on white clothes, face east, and, gazing at the face of the Blessed One Vajradhara, recite it seven times. By merely reciting it, one will be freed from all fears, will also attain the great accomplishment, and will be victorious everywhere. For protecting, guarding, and sheltering sentient beings, there is nothing greater than this great dhāraṇī."

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The line rendered "Clad in robes of willow-green and madder-red" is uncertain: ལྕང་ལོ may mean willow leaves or braided locks, and རྩ་དམར may be madder-red or a red herb; the general sense of a description of his garments is clear, but the exact image is not. In the list of benefits, བརྗོད་ན་དོན་ན་མཆིས་པ has an extra ན, evidently a slip for བརྗོད་ན་དོན་མཆིས་པ, and is translated in parallel with the neighboring phrases.  In the line rendered "Worshiped by the supreme vidyādharas," the Tibetan has rjed ("forget"), which makes no sense here. It has been read as mchod ("worship"). In "wholly freed from all faults," the text's nges pa ("certainty") has been read as nyes pa ("faults"). "Son of the son of Śuddhodana" presents Vajrapāṇi as a spiritual son of the Buddha. The text spells log 'dren as "logs 'dren"; it is taken as vināyaka.
