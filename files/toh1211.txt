The Analysis of the Reality of Hevajra (Hevajratattvavibhaṅga)

The Analysis of the Reality of Hevajra, known in Tibetan as Kye rdo rje'i de kho na nyid rnam par phye ba, is a short tantric work that explains the inner meaning of the iconography of Hevajra, a principal deity of the Highest Yoga Tantra tradition. In verse, it interprets the deity's sixteen hands as the sixteen emptinesses. It also reads the animals held in his skull cups as symbols: the elephant, horse, donkey, ox and camel stand for the afflictions of delusion, aversion, pride, attachment and envy, and are identified with the buddhas Vairocana, Akṣobhya, Ratnasambhava, the Lotus-Born Amitābha and Amoghasiddhi, while further figures such as the human, the śarabha and the cat represent karma, affliction and suffering. By showing how the deity's form embodies the transformation of afflictive states into awakened wisdom, the text serves as a contemplative key to visualizing Hevajra. It is Toh 1211 in the Tengyur, where it belongs among the Indian treatises and commentaries on the tantras, specifically the body of literature associated with the Hevajra cycle.

The Analysis of the Reality of Hevajra

As stated in the Analysis of the Reality of Hevajra:

Likewise, the sixteen emptinesses
Are renowned as the sixteen hands.
Since the elephant is the nature of delusion,
The elephant is declared to be Vairocana.
Since the horse is the nature of aversion,
The horse is proclaimed to be Akṣobhya.
Since the donkey is the nature of pride,
The donkey is declared to be Ratnasambhava.
Since the ox is the nature of attachment,
The ox is declared to be the Lotus-Born.
Since the camel is the nature of envy,
The camel is Amoghasiddhi himself.
For karma, affliction and suffering
There are the human, the śarabha and the cat.
They arise perfectly as the eight emptinesses;
They abide perfectly as the eight skull cups.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The channel names follow the list of thirty-two channels in the Hevajra Tantra. Where the Tibetan clearly renders a known Sanskrit name I give it, followed by the meaning the verse plays on. Three names have no confident Sanskrit match in this text and are translated: "the Loving One" (in the Hevajra list this position is held by Divyā), "the Measure," and the pair "the Delighted" and "the Embracer," whose two lines are obscure.

The two couplets on "subtle" and "form" together explain the name Sūkṣmarūpā, "Subtle Form"; the Tibetan does not state the name there, so it is not added to the translation.

"The Lotus-Born" is literal; in context it stands for the lotus-family buddha, Amitābha.

"The hot, likewise, is the Giver of Life" follows the text's reading; it may be a slip for "Giver of Heat," which would match Uṣmā.

"The threefold" renders the transliterated word tripata, whose sense is uncertain.

"Māradārikā" in the verse on the maiden plays on dārikā, which means both "girl" and "she who rends."

In the Saṃpuṭa passage on hūṃ and oṃ, the word otherwise meaning mātṛkā clearly means prosodic measures (mātrā): short, long and prolonged vowels.

"Carrying the eyes" for Doṣā follows the Tibetan. Some versions of the list may have "liver" here, which is close in Tibetan spelling.

"She of urine" at the anus is read as a slip for Sāmānyā, which sounds similar and fits the list's order. "Petal" in the last verse is read as a slip for Kāminī, which looks similar and completes the list.  This passage maps the thirty-two channels onto the fifteen goddesses of the Hevajra maṇḍala. The goddesses are identified by their usual Sanskrit names: Śabarī for "mountain-dweller", Caṇḍālī for "outcaste woman", Ḍombinī for "Ḍomba woman", Caurī for "thief woman", Bhūcarī for "earth-goer" and Khecarī for "sky-goer". Gaurī appears twice in this text: first for the Loving One and Vāmā, and again as door guardian for Surūpiṇī and Sāmānyā. Both occurrences are translated as the text gives them.
