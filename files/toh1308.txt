The Illumination of Nairātmyā (Nairātmyaprakāśa)

The Illumination of Nairātmyā, known in Sanskrit as the Nairātmyaprakāśa and in Tibetan as Dakmemé raptu salwa, is a prose sādhana with verse opening and closing, setting out the practice of the goddess Nairātmyā, "She Who Is Selflessness," the consort of Hevajra. Composed by the Indian master Advayavajra, who closes by honoring his teacher, the Lord of the Śabaras, and translated into Tibetan by Tsur Jñānākara from the teaching of the Indian preceptor Vajrapāṇi, it moves from the preliminaries and the visualization of Hevajra in front, through the protective circle, the palace and the eight charnel grounds, to the generation of Nairātmyā and her fifteen yoginīs from the vowels, with the symbolism of their ornaments and attributes explained. It then turns to the completion stage, mapping the yoginīs onto the thirty-two channels and four wheels of the vajra body, and ends with the mantras for recitation, torma and blessing. The work is Toh 1308 in the Tengyur, where it belongs among the tantra commentaries as a sādhana of the Hevajra cycle.

In the language of India: nairātma prākāsa. In the language of Tibet: The Illumination of Nairātmyā.

Homage to glorious Hevajra.

You who are declared the dharmakāya, wholly free of all conceptual thought,
The enjoyment body, lovely with incomparable bliss alone,
And the emanation body, become the cause of benefit for the world:
Blessed Vajrasattva, through you may there be virtue!
Wholly clear and yet extensive,
Free of conceptual thought, concise and yet extensive,
In accord with understanding and in accord with scripture,
I shall explain the sādhana of Nairātmyā.

The yogin, in a pleasing place such as a charnel ground, having partaken of the pledge substances such as the nectars, sits down on a comfortable seat. Without clinging and free of doubt, he raises his resolve for the sake of sentient beings and should generate the pride of being Nairātmyā. At the heart, upon a sun maṇḍala, he meditates on a blue syllable hūṃ. Then with its bright light rays he illuminates the three realms and summons those who dwell in Akaniṣṭha: the lord surrounded by eight yoginīs, sixteen-armed and eight-faced, wearing a skull crown beautifully adorned with a garland of skulls, pressing down the four māras evenly with his four feet, blue in color. In the skull cups held by the array of his right hands are an elephant, a horse, an ass, an ox, a camel, a man, a lion and a cat. In the lotus vessels placed in the array of his other hands are the earth goddess, the water god, the wind god, the fire god, the moon, the sun, Yama and the lord of the yakṣas. His principal face is black. His right face is like the moon and the jasmine flower. His left face is deep red. His upper face is like smoke and fiercely bares its fangs. All the other faces are deep black. He is adorned with a garland of fifty skulls. Embraced about the neck by Nairātmyā, he dwells in the center of the sky; one should meditate on him in front of oneself. After that, he should be worshipped by the eight goddesses with the outer, secret and ultimate offerings. Here too there is the picuvajra offering, so as to realize his nature through skillful means and wisdom.

Then, having made prostration, confession of wrongdoing, the vow to refrain from wrongdoing from now on, rejoicing in merit, full dedication of merit, going for refuge to the Three, generating the mind set on awakening, offering one's own person, and supplication, one should meditate on the four abodes of Brahmā.

The essence of the reality of all things, condensed: oṃ śūnyatā jñāna vajra svabhāva ātma ko'haṃ. By meditating on the meaning of the mantra, "All phenomena have as their very nature the vajra of the wisdom of emptiness," one abides in the essence that does not abide anywhere at all.

Then, rising through mindfulness by the force of one's aspiration, one sees in front, from raṃ, a sun maṇḍala. On it one imagines a crossed vajra from the syllable hūṃ. Then the mass of minute particles of light streaming from that crossed vajra binds the vajra fence and the net. Below, one should imagine the ground made of vajra and a moat. Immeasurable light rays come forth from the sun and the crossed vajra, and in this way all of these should be made firm.

After that, in the expanse of space, one should visualize wisdom in the form of a source of phenomena, hollow within, pure white, and wide at the top. Then upon it one should meditate on an eight-petaled lotus of various colors. Then upon it, on a sun maṇḍala arisen from raṃ, is the syllable hūṃ, and from its transformation one should imagine a crossed vajra. In the center of the crossed vajra one sees, each above the last, wind, fire, water and earth: smoke-colored, red, white and green; bow-shaped, triangular, round and square; from the transformation of yaṃ raṃ baṃ laṃ. All of these are regarded as mere wisdom, and from their transformation one should visualize the celestial palace, square, with four doors and four porticoes, beautified by eight pillars and adorned with nets and half-nets.

Then, inside the vajra fence, one should imagine the eight charnel grounds. In the east are Indra, an arura tree, and an elephant-faced one, yellow. In the south are Yama, a cūta tree, and a buffalo-faced one, white in color. In the west are an aśoka tree, Varuṇa, red in color, and a lion-faced one. In the north are the bodhi tree, Kubera, yellow, and a man-faced one. In the southeast are a karañja tree and the fire god, white and goat-faced. In Nairṛti's quarter are a ladajabaḍu tree, a man-faced one, and Nairṛti, white. In the northwest are a kakupa tree, Vāyu, and a deer-faced one, yellow. In Īśāna's quarter are a bhūta and a bull-faced one. One meditates on various banyan trees. All of these hold a skull cup in the left hand and their own various weapons in the right hand. Their upper bodies are shown. Likewise in the eight groups, the east and the rest, in due order, are Ananta, Padma, Vāsuki, Mahāpadma, Takṣaka, Śaṅkhapāla, Karkoṭa and Kulika. The eight clouds are yellow, gray, white, pale yellow, red, yellow, green, and of various colors. The colors of the eight guardians of the directions given in this way are simply those commonly known. The colors of the eight stūpas should also be understood.

Then, within the wheel of the maṇḍala, one imagines a red eight-petaled lotus, and one should see fifteen corpses: inside the lotus on the four petals of the east and so on, and likewise at the four corners of the celestial palace, at the four doors, and above and below. After that, from the transformation of the vowels and consonants pressing on the corpses, one should meditate on the fifteen vowels beginning with ā, placed within the moon and the sun. Then, from the transformation of the syllables beginning with a, from all of them come curved knives marked with the seed syllables. Then, from the transformation of the moon, the sun and the curved knife, one should meditate on the fifteen yoginīs, Nairātmyā and the rest. Here, the moon is endowed with mirrorlike wisdom; the sun is endowed with equality; what is placed within them is discriminating wisdom.

All becoming one is accomplishing activity. The image made complete is the utterly pure dharmadhātu.

Thus, in the center, arisen from the vowel syllable a, sealed by Akṣobhya, having the nature of aversion and of the aggregate of consciousness, is the form that is the essence of skillful means and wisdom, a form embracing the khaṭvāṅga. On the petals of the east and the rest, arisen from the vowels a i ī ū, having the nature of delusion, slander, desire and jealousy, sealed by Vairocana, Ratnasambhava, Amitābha and Amoghasiddhi, having the nature of the aggregates of form, feeling, perception and formations, one should meditate on Vajrā, Gaurī, Vārī and Vajraḍākinī. Then, in the outer ring, in the intermediate quarters beginning with Īśāna's, one should meditate on Pukkasī, Śavarī, Caṇḍālī and Ḍombinī, arisen from the vowels ū ṛ ṝ ḷ and so on, sealed by Akṣobhya, Vairocana, Ratnasambhava and Amitābha, having the nature of earth, water, fire and wind. Then at the doors of the east and the rest one should meditate on Gaurī, Caurī, Vetālī and Ghasmarī, arisen from the vowels ḹ e ai o, sealed with the nature of Pukkasī and the rest, having the nature of form, sound, smell and taste. After that, above and below, one should meditate on Bhūcarī and Khecarī, sealed by delusion and desire, arisen from au aṃ, having the nature of the tangible and of the dharmadhātu, the nature of saṃsāra and nirvāṇa. Here too, after the goddesses have been generated, one should understand that they are empowered together with their own families and sealed by their own families.

These are the fifteen yoginīs. Each has the look of a sixteen-year-old and abides on a moon maṇḍala, indivisible, her color like black collyrium. Her blazing hair, the nature of the awakening mind, stands upright. On the crown of her head she wears a skull fragment four fingers wide, the nature of Vajrasattva, and on her head five dry skulls, the nature of the five buddhas. She has three round red eyes and a face baring its fangs. She is adorned with a garland of fifteen dry heads, the nature of the fifteen mātṛkās. She wears a skirt of tiger skin, sits in the dance of the half-crossed posture mounted on a corpse, and wears the five mudrās. Of these:

Akṣobhya is in form the wheel;
Amitābha is the nature of the earrings;
On the necklace is the Jewel Lord;
The bracelets are said to be Vairocana;
On the girdle abides Amoghasiddhi;
The whole body is like Vajradhara.
Because she bows to the guru, the master and the chosen deity,
She wears the wheel on the crown of her head.
So as not to listen to words that disparage
The guru, the vajra holder,
She wears earrings on her ears.
For reciting mantra, there is the necklace;
Relying on the mudrā is the girdle;
Having given up killing is the bracelets;
And she wears anklets and armlets.
Her heart of loving-kindness: her limbs are black.
Her reddish-yellow hair
Shows great passion,
And stands upright so that she is known as wrathful.

Because body, speech and mind are by nature intensely passionate, her eyes are by nature large and red. Her two arms are the nature of existence and of nirvāṇa. What cuts faults such as pride is the curved knife. The three pieces of the skull are the three realms purified; it is filled with the blood of the four māras, that of the aggregates and the rest, and of all conceptual thought. The three frowning creases are the purity of the dharma, enjoyment and emanation bodies. The fullness of her body is the purity of the single body of essential nature. To show freedom from obscurations, she wears the garment of tiger skin. To show that the three realms are without any object of reference, her feet are bare. To show the nature of one taste, she presses down the earth maṇḍala with one foot.

After that, with five kinds of light rays arising from the seed syllable at the heart, one summons the wisdom being dwelling in Akaniṣṭha to one's own wheel of Nairātmyā, and should make it enter the seed syllable at the heart. Having made the wisdom being and the pledge being one, through having the pride of Nairātmyā one becomes equal to Nairātmyā. Here, in order to establish the six limbs of union, one should meditate in sequence on black, red, yellow, green, blue and white.

Then, as meditation becomes excellent, at first the deity appears like the disc of the moon covered by clouds. From the further perfection of that, it appears like an illusion. After that, from the ripening of that utmost perfection, one who sees nondually, at a time when dream and waking are alike, will attain the great seal. This passage presents the generation stage.

Otherwise, from the union of bola and kakkola, the awakening mind arises, set within the nature of great bliss, the joy free of the supreme. That very awakening mind, having the nature of the fifteen digits, instantly takes the emblems, colors and shapes shown above, and one should see the maṇḍala wheel of the fifteen yoginīs. This, moreover, is the five aggregates, the four elements and the six objects, the nature of body, speech and mind. This is the profound generation stage. One sees in a single instant the maṇḍala wheel consisting of the fifteen yoginīs. This is the completion stage.

The vajra body too has thirty-two channels blessed by the five wisdoms, which drip from the place of great bliss. These too have the nature of the fifteen yoginīs; the body itself is the wheel of Nairātmyā. This is the stage of full completion. Likewise lalanā and rasanā, beginning from the throat and down to the navel, lie on the left and on the other side, and are called moon and sun. Within the navel, in the channel of the place of birth, they are lalanā and rasanā, carrying Akṣobhya and blood. Avadhūtī, abiding within the head, the throat, the navel and the place of birth, carries the awakening mind. These channels are Nairātmyā. Abhedyā and Sūkṣmarūpā abide at the crown of the head and, respectively, carry in pairs the characteristics of nails and teeth, and of head hair and body hair: these are Vajrā.

Divyā, at the right ear, carrying skin and filth, and Vāmā, at the spine of the back, carrying flesh: these are Gaurī. Yāminī and Kūrmajā, abiding at the left ear and between the eyebrows, carrying sinew, bone and filth: these are Vārī. Bhāvakī and Sekā, abiding at the eyes and at the roots of the arms, carrying the lungs and the heart: these are Vajraḍākinī. Doṣāvatī and Mahāniye, abiding at the armpits and the breasts, carrying the eyes and bile: these are Pukkasī. Mātarā and Śarvarī, abiding at the navel and the tip of the nose, carrying the liver, the intestines and filth: these are Śavarī. Śītadā and Uṣmā, abiding at the mouth and the throat, carrying the threads of the flanks and the stomach: these are Caṇḍālī. Pravaṇā carries the heart and feces. Kṛṣṇavarṇā abides at the borders: these are Ḍombinī. Sūkṣmarūpā and Sāmānyā, at the genitals and the anus, carrying phlegm and bile: these are Gaurī. Hetudāyikā and Viyogā, abiding at the thighs and the calves, carrying blood and menstrual fluid: these are Caurī. Premaṇī and Siddhā, at the toes and the backs of the feet, carrying fat and liver: these are Vetālī. Pāvakī and Sumanā abide at the thumbs and the two knees; of these the former carries phlegm and the latter strength and mucus: these are Ghasmarī. On the petals of the east and so on around the center of the lotus at the heart are, in due order, Trivṛttā, Kāminī, Gehā, Caṇḍikā and Māradārikā. Of these, the first two channels are Bhūcarī and the rest are Khecarī. These too, by dripping profusely from the channels, cause increase, cause movement, and then flow a little.

Body, speech and mind, which are the essential nature of the dharma, enjoyment and emanation bodies, are arranged in the body as four wheels. Of these, the wheel of emanation is a lotus of various colors with sixty-four petals below the navel, where a black syllable a abides facing upward. The wheel of Dharma is an eight-petaled lotus at the heart, where a black syllable hūṃ abides. At the throat is the wheel of enjoyment, red, with sixteen petals, on which is the praṇava, a red seed syllable. At the crown of the head, on a white lotus of thirty-two petals, is the white seed syllable haṃ facing downward, from which a stream of nectar flows: this is the wheel of great bliss. Their arrangement according to the joys and the moments should be learned from the guru's instructions. This is the cause of effortlessly making manifest the awakening mind that arises nondually in union. This is the stage of essential nature.

Then, when one is wearied by meditation, one should recite the mantra while holding the pride of Nairātmyā. Here, the first, subtle and innately accomplished, is the mantra beginning with a, the nature of the fifteen vowels, with hūṃ phaṭ at the end. It is this: oṃ a ā i ī u ū ṛ ṝ ḷ ḹ e ai o au aṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā. Holding the pride of the red Nairātmyā, one should recite the mantra for stirring up a city. The mantra here is: oṃ a ka ca ṭa ta pa ya śa svāhā. oṃ akāro mukhaṃ sarba dharmāṇāṃ ādyanutpannatvāta oṃ āḥ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā: this is the torma mantra. oṃ āḥ hūṃ is the mantra for blessing the pledge substances. After that, at the time when the maṇḍala wheel is made manifest, setting aside the breasts, one should make one's own member a bola abiding within the kakkola, and the two cheeks become the bell.

The heart of the treatises on the way of secret mantra,
Known through the efforts of the holy guru,
This Illumination of Nairātmyā
I have composed with a mind of compassion.
As for the certain understanding
Of the profound maṇḍala wheel,
How could embodied beings here
Who have not taken upon their crowns
The dust of the lotus feet of the Lord of the Śabaras
Ever come to it with certainty?
By this virtue gained through this realization,
May the whole world give up the suffering and sorrow of the three realms of existence
And abide firmly in the state of Vajradhara!

The Sādhana of Nairātmyā, composed by the glorious paṇḍita Advayavajra, is complete. Having heard it from the Indian preceptor Vajrapāṇi, the later monk Tsur Jñānākara translated it.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The second verse of the opening is terse; it states that the sādhana will be explained clearly, concisely yet fully, and without needless conceptual elaboration. The deities in the left-hand lotus vessels are rendered by their roles, following the Hevajra parallel (Pṛthivī, Varuṇa, Vāyu, Agni, the moon, the sun, Yama and Vaiśravaṇa); "the moon" renders "head of the night". The tree in Nairṛti's charnel ground is transliterated as the Tibetan spells it, since it cannot be identified with confidence.  "In the intermediate quarters beginning with Īśāna's": the Tibetan has dbang po (Indra, the east), which cannot head a list of intermediate quarters; read as dbang ldan (Īśāna), where Pukkasī is placed elsewhere in this cycle. "Akṣobhya" carried by lalanā follows the Hevajra Tantra, where it denotes semen. "Her feet are bare" renders "feet not cleansed" (zhabs ma sbyangs pa); the sense is uncertain. "Bola and kakkola" are the tantric code terms for the male and female organs.  The channel names follow the list of thirty-two channels in the Hevajra Tantra. Phra ma na is read as Pravaṇā, and Mahāniye is kept as the Tibetan spells it, since it does not match the usual list. The channel called Sūkṣmarūpā here corresponds to Surūpiṇī in the Hevajra list. The closing instruction about setting aside the breasts and making one's member a bola within the kakkola is terse; it seems to describe the practitioner, visualized as the goddess, taking on the male role in union, and "the two cheeks become the bell" is uncertain. The Lord of the Śabaras is the siddha Śabareśvara (Śavaripa), the author's teacher.
