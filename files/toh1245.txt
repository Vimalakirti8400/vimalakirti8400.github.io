The Means of Accomplishment Called Dispelling Delusion (Bhramahārasādhana)

The Means of Accomplishment called Dispelling Delusion, in Sanskrit Bhramahāra Sādhana and in Tibetan Trulpa Pongwa Zhejawé Druppé Tap, is a short Indian tantric practice manual, or sādhana, devoted to the deity Hevajra in his eight-faced, sixteen-armed, four-legged form as Heruka. Composed by the Indian master Ratnākaraśānti, it guides the mantra practitioner through a full day of practice: rising at dawn, protecting the place and oneself, generating the maṇḍala of Hevajra and his surrounding goddesses through a sequence of meditative absorptions, making offerings and praises, reciting the deity's mantra, making aspirations, and carrying the yoga even into sleep and waking until the signs of accomplishment appear. It is Toh 1245 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the section of tantra commentaries among the works connected with the Hevajra cycle.

In the language of India: bhramahāra sādhanaṃ nāma. In the language of Tibet: The Means of Accomplishment called Dispelling Delusion.

Homage to glorious Hevajra.

By the force of his feet pressed down upon the earth, the ocean is set heaving;
like the clouds of the time of terror, his faces roar;
the wind from the forest of his arms shakes the mountain peaks and hurls them down:
the dance of Heruka dispels your fears.

With three eyes on every face,
terrifying with his four legs,
with sixteen arms and eight faces:
I shall explain the sādhana of Hevajra.

Here the mantra practitioner who has gained mastery in meditation and mantra recitation rises early in the morning and imagines the seed syllable upon a sun at the heart. Having washed the face and so on, he protects the place, himself and the yoga with oṃ rakṣa rakṣa hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā. Then he enters a place of meditation made fragrant with scents, flowers and the like, and sits cross-legged on a comfortable seat. Having fully recited the mantra over the welcoming water, with the light rays of the mantra at his heart he invites the Blessed One together with his retinue into the sky in front. Having beheld them, he should make offerings with the eight goddesses, Gaurī and the rest, who arise from the seed syllable at the heart. Of these, Gaurī holds the hare-marked moon, Caurī the sun, Vetālī water, Ghasmarī strength, Pukkasī sandalwood, Śavarī honey, and Caṇḍālī beats a hand drum, while Ḍombī embraces him about the neck as a man would.

Then, in front of that very Blessed One, one should perform the confession of wrongdoing and the rest as follows.
"I confess each and every wrongdoing to the Blessed One. I rejoice in all buddhas, bodhisattvas, noble ones and ordinary beings. I dedicate all my virtue to unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. Until awakening, I go for refuge to the Buddha, supreme among the two-legged. I go for refuge to the Dharma, supreme among the teachings of the Great Vehicle. I go for refuge to the Saṅgha, supreme among the assemblies of irreversible bodhisattvas. How wonderful! For the benefit and happiness of all sentient beings, I shall become fully awakened in unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening, and then establish them in the awakening of a buddha, up to the supreme and final nirvāṇa. To that end, I shall rely on the Vajra Vehicle, the path to unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening."

Then one should cultivate joy, in the form of wishing all sentient beings to dwell never parted from the exalted bliss of the deity, and equanimity, in the form of joining them to the path that is the remedy for the afflictions.

Then, taking all phenomena as the object of the mind, one should examine them: these are mind itself, appearing as its various transformations. As in a dream, there is no object grasped by the mind outside the mind. Because there is no grasped object, the mind too has no existence as a grasper. Therefore all phenomena are the body of the mind, and their emptiness of grasped and grasper is called the ultimate. Having determined this conclusively, one should view the nature of those phenomena, rid of the aspects of all the phenomena that are mistaken signs superimposed by delusion, as solely the nondual characteristic of cognition. This is taught to be the supreme awakening mind, transcending the world, the wisdom of emptiness, free from elaboration and free from conceptual thought.

Then one should bless it with its mantra: oṃ śūnyatājñāna vajra svabhāva ātmako'haṃ. That itself is the Blessed Lady, the Perfection of Wisdom, and that itself is the supreme protection.

Then one should meditate on the protection that is its concordant outcome, endowed with aspects, whose nature is pure worldly wisdom:

In front, from raṃ one should visualize a sun;
from that sun arises hūṃ, which turns into a crossed vajra.
With that very vajra one should visualize
the blazing fence, and the tent too, firmly bound.

The crossed vajra radiates light rays everywhere, unbearable like a terrifying fire, and fills all space. One should then see on the sides a square, blazing vajra fence; above, a vajra tent; and below, a ground of vajra nature reaching down to the foundation beneath the earth. Then the sun and the crossed vajra turn into light rays, which spread out in every direction and fill it, and one should seal the boundary far out beyond. That is the meaning of the verse.

Then one should contemplate one's own five great elements, purified, which are its concordant outcome. Of these, the great element of space is called the source of phenomena. It has the nature of the great Vajradhara, white like the autumn moon, narrow below and broad above, triangular, its inside of the nature of space. Within it, at the heart of a lotus that has sprung up with petals of various colors, rests a great crossed vajra. In its center one should view the four maṇḍalas of the four great elements, one above another, which have the nature of the four goddesses. First, from the syllable laṃ comes the great maṇḍala of Mahendra, square and yellow, marked at its corners with three-pronged vajras. Then from paṃ comes the maṇḍala of water, round and white, marked with a vase. Then from raṃ comes the maṇḍala of fire, triangular and red, marked at its corners with raṃ. Then from yaṃ comes the maṇḍala of wind, bow-shaped and black, marked at its two ends with fluttering streamers. At that time the meditator abides solely as that very transcendent wisdom which pervades it all.

Then, in the center of the hub of the crossed vajra, one should meditate on the great mansion of liberation, whose essence gathers into one the buddha field born from the transformation of the four great elements. It has the nature of Vairocana: a multistoried mansion made of various jewels, encircled by the eight charnel grounds, which show that all world systems are without self.

beautified by eight pillars,
encircled all around by four terraces,
and adorned with four archways.

The four terrifying māras

On the seat at its center, from the fifteen vowels comes a moon maṇḍala, the nature of mirror-like wisdom; above it, from the thirty-four consonants, a sun maṇḍala, the nature of the wisdom of equality. In its center, from the transformation of oṃ and hūṃ, one should imagine a curved knife and a skull cup, each marked in its center with its own seed syllable, having the nature of discriminating wisdom. Then from the two seed syllables the forms of the wheel of yoginīs and all the tathāgatas fully radiate out, gather back again and become one with the seed syllables: this is the wisdom of accomplishing activity. Then, born from the transformation of the moon, sun, emblems and seed syllables, the Blessed One Vajrasattva, with the color, form, emblems and the rest that will be explained, is generated in the form of glorious Heruka, and likewise embraced about the neck by Nairātmyā; one should view this as having the nature of the wisdom of the completely pure sphere of phenomena. These are the five manifest awakenings.

Then, through great vajra passion,
he melts together with the consort.

Then, roused by the songs, he is born from the gathered melted drop. One should ascertain the two syllables aṃ and hūṃ that rest on the sun maṇḍala which has arisen in its center, the deity's emblems, the body and the rest, to be like space and like an illusion. Then one should view the heart where the curved knife and skull cup are joined, whose nature is the samādhi that rests on their transformation, as endowed with the deity's two seed syllables, whose nature is fully knowing things exactly as they are. Then from that very thing, through yoga, one radiates the form of the wheel of yoginīs, whose nature is the infinite tathāgatas. Having ascertained that all of it is of the nature of mind, one gathers it back again through yoga, and knowing all of it to be like an illusion and like space, one should view oneself as Heruka, born from the transformation of the two suns, the emblems and the seed syllables.

At the heart of the Blessed Lady too, upon a moon, is a curved knife, and in its handle, upon a moon, one should imagine aṃ. These same five, Vajrā and the rest, are in order upon the aggregates of form, feeling, perception, formations and consciousness, and likewise upon delusion, avarice, attachment, jealousy and aversion. Upon the sense sources of form, sound, smell, taste, touch and phenomena are the outer Gaurī, Caurī, Vetālī, Ghasmarī, Bhūcarī and Khecarī.

Upon the elements of earth, water, fire and wind are Pukkasī, Śavarī, Caṇḍālī and Ḍombī. Upon body, speech and mind are the roaming goddesses and Nairātmyā. In this way everything has become deity.

His eyes are red with compassion;
his limbs are black, a mind of love;
his legs are the means of gathering;
his sixteen arms are emptiness;
his eight faces are the eight liberations;
the three realities are his three eyes;
the five mudrās are the five Victors;
as the wrathful one he tames the wicked.
At the throat, heart, bhaga and crown
are the four wheels, in order:
those of enjoyment, of Dharma, of emanation,

They are lotuses with sixteen and eight petals,
sixty-four and thirty-two,
adorned at their centers

After that, with hooks of light radiating from the seed syllable at one's own heart, one invites the tathāgatas who dwell in the ten directions and requests them to be seated in the sky. Having made offerings to them with the eight goddesses, one should make the request: "May all the tathāgatas empower me." Then they, having taken the form of glorious Heruka, embodying the five tathāgatas, confer empowerment with vases filled with the five nectars. On the crown of the one so empowered, the Blessed One Akṣobhya arises. Before him a rain of flowers and a rain of saffron fall, and the sound of drums resounds. Rūpavajrā and the others make offerings, and Locanā and the others praise him with vajra songs. This teaches the first samādhi, called the first yoga: the essence body.

Then one should attend to the reality of the deities. Here all phenomena are gathered into body, speech, mind and wisdom. The discernment of the emptiness of duality of these, body and the rest, whose nature is mere cognition, is, as fits each case, the reality of the door guardians. The true wisdom that is that very emptiness, free from elaboration, the undefiled wisdom, the unsurpassed Vajra Vehicle, is, as fits each case, the reality of the four, Pukkasī and the rest. The fruit reached through that very Vajra Vehicle, the state of the great Vajradhara, unsurpassed awakening, is the reality of the lord of the maṇḍala.

Then from the seed syllable at one's own heart one emanates the eight offering goddesses, and as the sky is filled with the clouds of what they emanate, one should make offerings to the maṇḍala together with its lord. Delighting in many and various charms and glances,

displaying many and various great vows.

Through the yoga of all space,
having entered the samaya of space,
all the worlds without exception

Then one should visualize in the sky a vajra arising from hūṃ, and below it a lotus arising from a. In its center one marks the substances with oṃ. From the union of vajra and lotus the fire of great passion blazes up; by it they are boiled, ripened and melted, and they become like the light of the sun of wisdom. Their rays illuminate the forms of all the tathāgatas of the ten directions, and those same rays draw out the nectar of wisdom from their wisdom seeds and make it fall into the substances and mingle with them. Likewise, when these have become nectar, one blesses them with the three syllables. One imagines on the tongue a hūṃ and a supreme vajra, and at the heart the maṇḍala upon a sun, and then one should properly make offering to oneself, who is their very nature, and to the maṇḍala.

In the delightful wheel of the mātṛkās
the bliss free of waves is attained.
Having the nature that is without waves,
such a pervading lord one should meditate upon.

This is the second samādhi, called the Supreme King of the Maṇḍala; from it comes the Enjoyment Body.

Then Gaurī, Caurī and the rest, up to the lord of the maṇḍala, are emanated one by one in sequence, unceasingly, into the ten directions. Having accomplished the welfare of sentient beings, they are gathered back into that very place. This is the maṇḍala that has done its work. The lord of the maṇḍala, too, should be seen as displaying the bliss free of conceptual elaboration. Therein Gaurī establishes sentient beings in the discernment of the reality of body; Caurī, of mind; Vetālī, of speech; and Ghasmarī, of wisdom. Pukkasī establishes them in the perfect wisdom of body; Śavarī, of mind; Caṇḍālī, of speech; and Ḍombī, of wisdom. And the lord of the maṇḍala establishes sentient beings in the state of the great Vajradhara.

Then one should meditate in equipoise through the yoga of six limbs:

Black, red, and then yellow,
green, blue and white, in sequence;
for as long as the innate joy endures,
one should contemplate the circle with its lord at the head.

This is the third samādhi, called the Supreme King of Action, the Emanation Body.

When wearied by meditation, one should recite mantra. Firmly visualizing oneself together with the maṇḍala in the form of the deity, one recites oṃ deva picu vajra hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā. One mentally draws at the heart the thirteen syllables of the mantra, standing upright, blazing like lamps and arranged in a circle, and then recites. One should recite them with vajra words and with wrathful words, imagining that all the faces, and all the faces of the goddesses, are reciting the mantra. By this very practice the deity's own recitation is made a hundredfold greater than the recitation of all the goddesses.

Then one should make an aspiration:

By whatever virtue has been gained,
may the five classes of beings attain
all the wealth of all the buddhas,
the state of the great Vajradhara.
What the mantra practitioner of the awakening vajra
practices for the sake of perfect awakening,
and whatever is the conduct of the perfect buddhas,
may I practice those two.

Then, having properly worshiped the maṇḍala together with its lord as before, one meditates on the goddesses as absorbed within the mantra at one's own heart. One imagines the supporting maṇḍala as exceedingly vast, and ascertains the four great elements as having the nature of Pukkasī and the others. Then one should rise with the pride of Hevajra, and afterward perform recitation and the rest. At the time of sleep, one makes manifest the yoga of six limbs, or the yoga of innate joy, and falls asleep. One should rise from sleep when roused by the true song of the four goddesses, and having risen, do everything as before. In the same way, day after day, one watches until the signs of accomplishment appear. Having seen them, one should abide, in their proper order, in the nature of the accomplishment one wishes to see.

By whatever virtue I have gained
by clearly setting forth the sādhana called Dispelling Delusion,
of the eight-faced Heruka, source of weighty qualities,
may I, who depend upon birth,
through that cause of benefit in all times,
become a vajra victor.

The work composed by the master Ratnākaraśānti is complete.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

Ghasmarī's offering is given as "strength" (stobs), which may render Sanskrit bala; the Hevajra Tantra elsewhere gives her medicine. The fifth goddess, called "Incense Lady" in Tibetan, is taken as Pukkasī, who fills that place in Hevajra's retinue of eight. The water maṇḍala's seed is spelled paṃ here, where vaṃ is usual.  "In the center are the four māras" reads bdud, māra, where the text has bdud rtsi, "nectar"; the next lines confirm that the central seat is the four māras. "The two suns" follows the text, though elsewhere the generation proceeds from moon and sun. "Like bees" takes phung ba as bung ba, "bee," the usual image for the black side faces. "Roused by the songs" renders an obscure phrase, glu yi dor byas. The left-hand skulls hold a yakṣa and the god of wealth where the Hevajra Tantra has Yama and Vaiśravaṇa.  "The roaming goddesses" renders spyod ma, read after "body, speech and mind" (the text has ma for "from," probably for la, "upon"); these are presumably Bhūcarī and Khecarī, but the text names only "the roaming ones" before Nairātmyā. In the verse on the sealing Buddhas, "the Buddha" (sangs rgyas) is left unspecified; it may stand for Vairocana.  The phrase rendered "should be seen as displaying the bliss free of conceptual elaboration" reads སྟེང་བར, which makes little sense here; it has been taken as a slip for སྟོན་པར, "displaying." The three syllables used to bless the nectar are not named in the text; they are conventionally oṃ, āḥ and hūṃ.
