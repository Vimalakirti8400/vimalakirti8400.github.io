The Great Sūtra Called The Five-and-Three (Pañcatriyānāma Mahāsūtra)

The Great Sūtra Called The Five-and-Three, known in Sanskrit as the Pañcatriyanāma Mahāsūtra and in Tibetan as Mdo chen po lnga gsum pa zhes bya ba, is a Buddhist sūtra in which the Buddha addresses a gathering of monks at Śrāvastī, in the Jeta Grove. The text concerns the various metaphysical positions held by ascetics and brahmins regarding perception, non-perception, annihilation, and liberation, systematically examining the views through which such thinkers make declarations about existence and the self. Its title appears to reflect a structural arrangement of five and three categories that organize the discourse. It is Toh 294 in the Kangyur, belonging to the general class of sūtra literature.

In the language of India: Pañcatriyanāma Mahāsūtram. In the Tibetan language: The Great Sūtra Called The Five-and-Three.

Homage to the Three Jewels.

Thus did I hear. At one time the Blessed One was dwelling at Śrāvastī, in the Jeta Grove, in the garden of Anāthapiṇḍada.

Then the Blessed One addressed the monks: "Monks, all those ascetics (śramaṇas) and brahmins who make declarations about the many bases of their views, all of them make their declarations in terms of five positions. What are the five? Some declare: 'From this point forward, I shall become one who possesses perception.' Others declare: 'I shall be without perception,' or 'I shall be neither with perception nor without perception.' And there is annihilation. And yet others make the declaration: 'In this very life I shall pass into nirvāṇa.'

"Among those ascetics and brahmins endowed with knowledge who speak of possessing perception, some hold that a self exists, and they declare: 'From this point forward, I shall become one who possesses perception.' Others declare: 'There will be annihilation.' And yet others make the declaration: 'In this very life I shall pass into nirvāṇa.' When these five are present they become three, and when the three are present they become five. This is a brief presentation of the Dharma category called The Five-and-Three.

"Monks, now among those ascetics and brahmins who speak of possessing perception, those who make the declaration: 'From this point forward, I shall become one who possesses a self with perception' — those ascetics and brahmins endowed with knowledge who speak in this way, some hold that a self exists, and they declare: 'From this point forward, I shall become one who possesses perception and has form,' or 'one who is formless,' or 'one who has a single unified perception,' or 'one who has varied perceptions,' or 'one who has a limited perception,' or 'one who has come to have a vast perception,' or 'one who has boundless perception.' And yet others, turning away from all of that, make the declaration: 'That is the sense base of infinite consciousness.'

"The Tathāgata knows correctly and precisely those ascetics and brahmins who speak of possessing perception, those who make the declaration: 'From this point forward, I shall become one who possesses perception' — those ascetics and brahmins endowed with knowledge who speak in this way, some holding that a self exists — who declare: 'From this point forward, I shall become one who possesses perception and has form,' or 'formless,' or 'with a single unified perception,' or 'with varied perceptions,' or 'with a limited perception,' or 'with a vast perception,' or 'with boundless perception' — and those who, turning away from all of that, make the declaration: 'That is the sense base of infinite consciousness.'

"Monks, those with varying perceptions and varying designations declare: 'Having perfectly passed beyond the sense base of infinite consciousness in every respect, there is nothing whatsoever,' and, having established the sense base of nothingness, they abide there — non-dual, unmoving. This, they say, is supreme; this is unsurpassable.

"Monks, that is gross, compounded, intended, and dependently originated. And since formations exist, there is also the cessation of formations. Knowing this to be the case, the Tathāgata turns back from that, beholds its pacification, and dwells beholding its definitive transcendence."

"Monks, in this regard there are ascetics and brahmins who advocate the absence of perception and who make declarations — some declaring: 'Henceforth I shall become one without perception.' These contradict and censure what is asserted by those ascetics and brahmins who advocate the presence of perception and who make declarations — some declaring: 'Henceforth I shall become one with perception.' They censure it, and they think to themselves: 'Perception is a disease. Perception is a boil. Perception is a thorn. This — the absence of perception — is peaceful. This is sublime.' And the Tathāgata fully knows, exactly as it is, those ascetics and brahmins who advocate the absence of perception and who make such declarations — some declaring: 'Henceforth I shall become one without perception' — and who contradict and censure what is asserted by those ascetics and brahmins who advocate the presence of perception and who make declarations — some declaring: 'Henceforth I shall become one with perception.' They censure it, and they think: 'Perception is a disease. Perception is a boil. Perception is a thorn. The absence of perception — this is peaceful. This is sublime.'

"Monks, I do not designate consciousness as coming or going, as persisting, as passing away or being reborn, as growing, increasing, and expanding — as something other than form, other than feeling, other than perception, other than formations. Monks, if anyone says: 'I designate consciousness as coming or going, as persisting, as passing away or being reborn, as growing, increasing, and expanding — as something other than form, other than feeling, other than perception, other than formations' — that is nothing more than words, and since it falls outside the domain of valid experience, if questioned on it, that person will not truly know and will become confused in answering.

"Monks, that too is gross, compounded, intended, and dependently originated. And since formations exist, there is also the cessation of formations. Knowing this to be the case, the Tathāgata turns back from that, beholds its pacification, and dwells beholding its definitive transcendence.

These contradict and censure what is asserted by those ascetics and brahmins who advocate the presence of perception and who make declarations — some declaring: 'Henceforth I shall become one with perception.' They censure it. And they also contradict and censure what is asserted by those ascetics and brahmins who advocate the absence of perception and who make declarations — some declaring: 'Henceforth I shall become one without perception.' They censure it, and they think: 'Perception is a disease. Perception is a boil. Perception is a thorn. And the total absence of perception is confusion. This — the sense base of neither perception nor non-perception — is peaceful. This is sublime.' And the Tathāgata fully knows those ascetics and brahmins who advocate neither the presence nor the absence of perception and who make such declarations — some declaring: 'Henceforth I shall become one who has neither perception nor the absence of perception —'"

Monks, I do not say that one will attain that remaining sense base of formations merely through what one has seen, heard, distinguished, known, through moral discipline, through ascetic observance, through livelihood, through the holy life, through austerity, or through devoted service. Monks, I do say that one will attain that sense base through entering into the meditative absorption of the summit of existence.

Monks, that very appearance of striving for it is itself an affliction of that sense base. The Tathāgata fully knows, exactly as they are, those who are distressed and those who strive. Monks, that too is coarse, conditioned, intended, and arisen through dependent origination. They also contend against and censure what those ascetics and brahmins who advocate the absence of perception have said, namely those who declare: 'Henceforth I shall become one without perception.' And they contend against and censure what those ascetics and brahmins who advocate neither the presence nor the absence of perception have said, namely those who declare: 'Henceforth I shall become one who has neither perception nor the absence of perception.' In this way, the Tathāgata holds that those ascetics and brahmins who are annihilationists — those among them who declare "From this point onward I shall become annihilated" — contradict and censure what those ascetics and brahmins who speak of a conscious self (those who declare "From this point onward I shall come to have perception") have declared. For as long as one draws near to the gods of the desire realm, the gods of the form realm, the formless sphere of infinite space, the formless sphere of infinite consciousness, the formless sphere of nothingness, the formless sphere of neither-perception-nor-non-perception, and remains alive, persisting, dwelling and continuing to live — for that long it is accompanied by illness, accompanied by tumors, accompanied by sharp pains, accompanied by fevers, accompanied by utter torment. But from the moment of death onward, this self of mine shall be cut off, shall perish, and shall not come to be again — to that extent this self of mine will be thoroughly and completely cut off. Those who think thus, the Tathāgata knows exactly as they truly are.

Monks, as for those ascetics and brahmins who are possessed of knowledge, who speak of annihilation, and who assert annihilation, perishing, and non-existence with respect to sentient beings that do exist — in them there remains a residue of the mass of ignorance. And to the extent that there remains a residue of the mass of ignorance, to that extent there is fear of the perishing of the transient collection, and being afraid and terrified of the transient collection, they go about disparaging that very transient collection alone. Yet at the very time they are disparaging the transient collection alone, those very people are attached to nothing other than the transient collection.

Monks, that too is coarse, conditioned, intended, and dependently originated. And since, given that formations exist, there also exists the cessation of formations, the Tathāgata, having understood this, turns away from it, seeing peace in it, seeing definitive release, and abides accordingly.

Monks, with respect to those who speculate about the further end — those who articulate and proclaim the various speculative views accompanied by theories about the further end — there are just so many of them. Those are called the various speculative views accompanied by theories about the further end.

Monks, as for those who speculate about the former end — how many are those who articulate and proclaim the various speculative views accompanied by theories about the former end? Monks, those ascetics and brahmins who speculate about the former end — among them, some hold and declare views such as these: that the self and the world are permanent; that the self and the world are impermanent; that they are both permanent and impermanent; that they are neither permanent nor impermanent; that the self and suffering are permanent; that the self and suffering are impermanent; that they are both permanent and impermanent; that they are neither permanent nor impermanent; that the self and the world are self-made; that the self and the world are made by another; that they are both self-made and made by another; that they arose from no cause, being neither self-made nor made by another; that the self and suffering are self-made; that the self and suffering are made by another; that they are both self-made and made by another; that they arose from no cause, being neither self-made nor made by another. Among those who hold such views and speak in such terms, the Tathāgata knows exactly as they truly are — those ascetics and brahmins who speculate about the former end, those among them who hold and proclaim the view that the self and the world are permanent — those too the Tathāgata knows exactly as they truly are. Those ascetics and brahmins are without faith and

without desire, without learning through transmission, without thorough examination of appearances, and without the patient acceptance born of personal insight — the Tathāgata knows this too exactly as it truly is, and understands that any thinking which regards such knowledge as actually existing has no ground to stand on. When those various ascetics and brahmins who do possess knowledge come to understand these things, and yet — though lacking that knowledge themselves — still proclaim themselves knowers and declare that they alone stand in perfect purity, this can only appear as sheer ignorance on their part. For monks, even that is coarse, conditioned, thought-out, and dependently originated. Monks, now as for those who proclaim nirvāṇa in this very life and declare that one passes into nirvāṇa in this very life — how many such forms are there? Monks, that joy of seclusion is impermanent and subject to cessation. From the cessation of the bliss of seclusion there arises mental discomfort, and from the cessation of mental discomfort there arises the joy of seclusion. Monks, what is that other supreme attainment that I, the unsurpassed guide of persons to be trained, have fully and perfectly awakened to by my own direct knowledge and proclaimed? Monks, it is this: the liberation that is undefiled and unconditioned, through the exhaustion of all defilements. The summary verse reads:

Diverse perceptions and form,
views of annihilation and permanence,
shade, water, and a walking staff,
and the final person to be tamed.

The Great Sūtra, the Fifty-Third, is complete.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The title Pañcatriyanāma Mahāsūtram is a hybrid Sanskrit formation meaning "The Great Sūtra Called the Five-and-Three," corresponding precisely to the Tibetan mdo chen po lnga gsum pa. The sūtra belongs to the category of discourses analyzing speculative views about the self and its posthumous condition, closely parallel to passages in the Pāli Dīgha and Aṅguttara Nikāyas dealing with sassatavāda and ucchedavāda. The phrase rendered "from this point forward I shall become one who possesses perception" (de pha chad bdag 'du shes can du 'gyur ro) reflects the Tibetan rendering of a standard Indic formulation about the post-mortem continuity or non-continuity of the self — a concern of the speculative teachers being described. The final paragraph's characterization of even the attainment of the base of nothingness as "gross, compounded, intended, and dependently originated" is a doctrinal marker that the Tathāgata has insight surpassing all such attainments.  The phrase rendered "falls outside the domain of valid experience" translates "yul ma yin pa'i phyir," literally "because it is not a [valid] object or domain." The doctrinal point is that positing consciousness as a self-subsisting entity apart from the other aggregates is not a position that can be coherently investigated or answered, since it does not correspond to any actual object of cognition. This is a key early Buddhist argument against the notion of a standalone, aggregate-transcending self-consciousness.

The word "འབྲས་" rendered here as "boil" follows the standard Pāli parallel (gaṇḍa) found in the Mahādukkhakkhandha-sutta and related texts, where the three terms "disease, boil, thorn" are a stock formula for what is to be rejected. The Tibetan "འབྲས" (literally "fruit" or "result") is unusual here; the Pāli has "gaṇḍa" (boil, tumor), and the Tibetan is almost certainly a rendering of that term. This is a genuine philological point worth noting.  The phrase rendered "sense base of formations" translates "'du byed kyi skye mched," which is unusual — most parallel passages refer to the sense base of neither perception nor non-perception. The Tibetan here appears to use "formations" where the expected term would be "neither perception nor non-perception," and this may reflect a variant reading or scribal issue in the Tibetan; the translation follows the Tibetan as it stands.

The passage breaks off mid-sentence at the close, consistent with a continuation in the following segment.  The passage is a section of the Brahmajāla-sūtra or a related text cataloguing the sixty-two speculative views, here focused on annihilationist doctrines (ucchedavāda).
