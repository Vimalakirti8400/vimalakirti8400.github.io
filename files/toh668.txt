The Dhāraṇī Called the Glorious Dark Protector (Śrī Mahākāla Nāma Dhāraṇī)

The Dhāraṇī Called the Glorious Dark Protector, known in Sanskrit as the Śrī Mahākāla Nāma Dhāraṇī and in Tibetan as Dpal Mgon Po Nag Po Zhes Bya Ba'i Gzungs, is a dhāraṇī text centered on Mahākāla, the great wrathful protector deity who serves as lord of the desire realm and commander of the divine hosts. The text presents Mahākāla approaching the newly awakened Buddha at Bodh Gayā and offering a vidyā mantra for the benefit of beings, situating this powerful protective dhāraṇī within the narrative frame common to canonical sūtra literature. It is Toh 668 in the Kangyur, belonging to the dhāraṇī section of the canon, and was translated into Tibetan by the translator Phurbu Öd.

Translated by the translator Phurbu Öd.

In the language of India: Śrī Mahākāla Nāma Dhāraṇī. In the language of Tibet: the dhāraṇī called the Glorious Dark Protector.

Homage to all the buddhas and bodhisattvas.

Thus did I hear. At one time, not long after the Blessed One had tamed Māra at Vajrāsana (the seat of awakening, Bodh Gayā) and fully and completely awakened, he was dwelling at the foot of the tree of awakening. In the direction where the Blessed One was present, the Great Dark Protector — the great commander of the divine hosts (Skt. gaṇapati), Mahākāla, who had received empowerment as lord of the desire realm — bowed his head at the feet of the Blessed One, sat to one side, and spoke these words:

"Venerable Blessed One, I pray you accept this vidyā mantra I offer:

Oṃ śrī mahākāla gaṇa guti svāhā. Namaḥ śrī mahākāla gaṇe guti śāya. Namo yoginīnāṃ. Tadyathā: oṃ hili hile kite kiteni cide cideni svāhā.

By reciting this, all illness, all epidemics, all malevolent spirits, all harmful beings, all elemental forces, all that have drawn near with harmful intent, and all that have gazed with harmful intent — from above, as high as the realm of the gods who wield power through emanations, and downward — are bound. Below, from the depths of the hells of sentient beings upward, they are bound. And laterally, in every direction all around, as far as the mountains, they are bound.

Furthermore, I am the guardian of temples and maṇḍalas, the physician against the illness of the three poisons, the life-force of all vidyādhara holders, the tutelary deity of all yogins, the lord over all who tame Māra, the one who thoroughly crushes the city of Laṅkāpurī of the rākṣasas, and the one who reduces to dust Vajravidāraṇī, the lightning-goddess among the daughters of the yakṣas. I have accepted and pledged to protect the teachings of the Three Jewels."

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The epithet "གཞན་འཕྲུལ་དབང་བྱེད" refers to the sixth class of desire-realm gods, those who exercise power over others' emanations (Skt. paranirmitavaśavartin). The text specifies binding all harmful forces from this divine level downward to the hells, and laterally to the surrounding mountains — this threefold directionality (above, below, lateral) is a standard formula for totalizing protective scope.

The phrase "གནོད་སྦྱིན་གྱི་བུ་མོ་ནམ་མཁའི་གློག་མ་སྲིན་རྡུལ་དུ་རློག་པ" is slightly ambiguous. I have read "nam mkha'i glog ma" as a descriptive epithet ("lightning of the sky," i.e., Vajravidāraṇī, a yakṣa-goddess) reduced to dust, though it could alternatively describe a separate figure. The identification with Vajravidāraṇī is uncertain; this passage may simply refer to a sky-dwelling lightning-yakṣiṇī generically.
