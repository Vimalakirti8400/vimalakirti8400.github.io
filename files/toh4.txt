The Sūtra of Individual Liberation for Fully Ordained Nuns (Bhikṣuṇī Prātimokṣa Sūtra)

Toh 4 in the Kangyur is the Bhikṣuṇī Prātimokṣa Sūtra, known in Tibetan as the Dge slong ma'i so sor thar pa'i mdo, or in English as the Sūtra of Individual Liberation for Fully Ordained Nuns. It is a Vinaya text — a code of monastic discipline — setting out the rules of individual liberation, the prātimokṣa, specifically for fully ordained Buddhist nuns, the bhikṣuṇīs. The text opens with verses of homage to the Buddha, Dharma, and Saṅgha before proceeding to enumerate the precepts that govern the conduct and spiritual life of nuns within the monastic community. As one of the foundational disciplinary scriptures, it belongs to the Vinaya section of the Kangyur, the collection of texts attributed to the Buddha himself.

In the language of India: Bhikṣuṇī Prātimokṣa Sūtra. In the language of Tibet: The Sūtra of Individual Liberation for Fully Ordained Nuns. First fascicle.

Homage to the Omniscient One.

I bow to the buddha, sole guide of beings, to the sacred Dharma, and to the noble Saṅgha — precious jewels. Now listen with effort as I set forth individual liberation, the cause of complete freedom from suffering.

Whose banner of renown resounds through the three worlds, who has proclaimed the sound of the sacred Dharma like the roar of a lion, who has attained the omniscient treasure-store of the Jewel — at whose feet Brahmā and Indra (Śakra) touch their crown-jewels in reverence, who has crossed the fathomless and boundless ocean of suffering — I bow my head to that sovereign of beings, and shall open the training-foundation of omniscience, that precious vessel, in the midst of the noble assembly.

The very heart and essence of all that abides in the great ocean of the Buddha's Vinaya — fathomless and without boundary — is this individual liberation.

This is the supreme guide of all phenomena under the sovereignty of the sacred Dharma-king. This is the great market-treasury of training, the trading-house of the community of monks. This is the medicine that clears the poison of those seized by broken moral discipline. This is the iron hook for young two-legged beings who have strayed in the intoxication of youth. This is the method of crossing over to liberation from saṃsāra — deeper than any ocean. This is the sure and solid bridge for beings traveling the path of good rebirth. This is the path that defeats the afflictions, the supreme guide for the sovereign. This is the foundation — like a stairway — for entering the city of liberation.

"When I have passed into nirvāṇa, this shall be your Teacher." With reverence, the Self-Arisen One himself extolled it with care before the assembled community of monks.

Even the very word "buddha" is exceedingly rare in the worlds. To obtain human birth is extremely rare. Going forth into renunciation is extremely rare. And for those who have gone forth, perfection in moral discipline is extremely rare. Even if one's moral discipline is wholly pure, finding a good spiritual companion is very hard indeed.

The arising of a buddha in the world, the teaching of the Dharma, the obtaining of good moral discipline, and the finding of a good companion — though rare — having now obtained them all: wise ones who wish me well, wishing to make these fruitful, should strive to listen to individual liberation for the sake of those committed to their vows.

The monk who has subdued the passions and tamed every sense faculty, who has gone forth and does not waver from buddhahood, who wishes for certain liberation — let such a one always guard the individual liberation.

Even across countless aeons, to hear individual liberation, to retain and hold it in mind — this is rare; and to put it into practice is rarer still.

Blissful is the arising of buddhas. Blissful is the teaching of the Dharma. Blissful is the harmony of the Saṅgha. Blissful is the austerity of those in harmony. Blissful is the sight of the noble ones. Blissful is the company of the holy. Were the childish ones never to be seen, one would dwell in constant happiness. Blissful is the sight of those endowed with moral discipline. Blissful is the sight of the learned. Blissful is the sight of arhats who are freed from rebirth, those foe-destroyers. Blissful is a pleasant ford, blissful is a pleasant river-stream. Blissful is one who is habituated to the Dharma. Blissful is one who has obtained wisdom. Blissful is the exhaustion of conceit and arrogance.

Those who are disciplined, with tamed faculties, who grow old in peaceful hermitages, and the learned who dwell within the forests, their youth spent — blissful is the dwelling of those whose youth has passed.

Noble ones, what has gone by among your seasons, and what remains — only so much remains. Noble ones, old age and death are coming and will be manifested, and the teaching of the Teacher will also fall into ruin. The Mount Meru of the Dharma will crumble. The wish-fulfilling tree of the Dharma will wither. The wheel of the Dharma will decay. The lamp of the Dharma will be extinguished.

The ocean of Dharma, too, will dry up. The darkness of ignorance will grow ever more powerful. The practice of striving will utterly decline, and without those who strive, before long this world will be without light. Therefore, noble ones, practice yoga with diligence.

The awakening of the Tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly and fully awakened buddhas, and likewise all other virtuous qualities akin to that — the qualities on the side of awakening — are attained through diligence. The Saṅgha of śrāvakas of the Blessed One have few concerns and few activities, so what is the first business of the Saṅgha? Noble ones, ask those who have not yet arrived about their aspiration and their purification, and having asked, let it be declared.

With ten fingers joined in reverence
before the Śākya Lion,
recite the Prātimokṣa
and hear it from me for the sake of those to be tamed.
Having heard, strive to accomplish
exactly as the great seer taught,
and with respect to the minor infractions,
make them as things you actually do.

For the mind-horse that is hard to bridle,
always striving to go astray,
this thread — the Prātimokṣa —
is the sharp, hundredfold fitting nail.

Those great ones who are turned back
and do not transgress their bounds by word alone —
they are good human horses
who will certainly be victorious in the battle with afflictions.

Those who lack this thread
and will never wish for it —
they will be roiled by the battle of afflictions
and will wander utterly without rest.

Noble ones of the Saṅgha, please listen. Today is the fourteenth or fifteenth day of the Saṅgha's restoration ceremony (poṣadha). If the time has come for the Saṅgha and it meets with approval, let the Saṅgha grant permission: today the Saṅgha shall perform the restoration ceremony and recite the recitation of the Prātimokṣa sūtra. This is the motion.

Noble ones, we shall perform the restoration ceremony. I shall recite the recitation of the Prātimokṣa sūtra. Listen well and take it thoroughly to heart, and I will explain it to you. Whoever among you has a transgression, let her confess it. If there is no transgression, remain silent. If you remain silent, I shall understand, noble ones, that you are completely pure. Just as a nun answers when questioned individually, so too shall this assembly of nuns be proclaimed to up to three times. Further, any nun who, when it has been proclaimed up to three times before an assembly of nuns such as this, has a transgression and yet does not confess it while being mindful of it — that one speaks a conscious lie. Noble ones, speaking a conscious lie has been declared by the Blessed One to be an obstructing factor. Therefore, a nun who wishes to be fully purified of a transgression that has occurred and who, being mindful of it, perceives a transgression, should confess it. Having confessed, she will abide in touch with ease. If she does not confess and does not purify, it will not be so.

Noble ones, I have set forth the introduction to the recitation of the Prātimokṣa sūtra. I now ask you, noble ones: are you completely pure in this? Are you completely pure in this? I ask this a second and a third time. Noble ones, since you are completely pure in this and remain silent in this way, so it is understood to be.

In summary:
Unchastity, stealing,
killing a human, speaking falsehood,
the child of a sākha, the perfume-selling youth,
the intimate friend, and the person born of a slave.

Noble ones, these eight rules leading to defeat arise from the recitation of the Prātimokṣa sūtra, which is proclaimed twice each month.

Furthermore, if any nun who has come to share the same training as the community of nuns, without having surrendered her training and without having declared the training impaired, engages in unchaste conduct — the practice of sexual intercourse — even with a creature born in an animal's womb, that nun too has incurred defeat and must not remain in the community.

Furthermore, if any nun takes what has not been given by others, whether dwelling in a village or dwelling in a wilderness — takes it in a manner that counts as theft — then to whatever extent she has taken without it being given, if she is seized by a king or a great minister, who says to her: "Woman, you are a thief, a fool, an idiot, a robber," and she is executed, bound, or banished — if a nun thus takes what has not been given, that nun too has incurred defeat and must not remain in the community.

Furthermore, if any nun, with deliberate intent, takes the life of a human being or of one who has the form of a human, either with her own hand or by providing a weapon to that person, or by seeking out a weapon that will be used against that person, or by inducing that person to die, or by praising death to that person — saying such words as: "O person, what use to you is this evil, impure, and wretched life? For you, death is better than living" — and, through the desire of her mind and the conceptual thoughts of her mind, in many and various ways induces that person to die or praises death to that person, and the person addressed by such instigation thereby dies, that nun too has incurred defeat and must not remain in the community.

Furthermore, if any nun who has no direct knowledge and no full knowledge of the supremely excellent qualities of a human being — whether noble attainment, realization, knowledge, vision, or meditative experience — nevertheless claims, "I know this, I see this," and from that a transgression arises; and then, wishing to purify it, at some other time, whether asked or not, she says: "Noble ones, I said I knew without actually knowing; I said I saw without actually seeing — I spoke an idle, false boast," then, save for the conceit of pride, that nun too has incurred defeat and must not remain in the community.

Furthermore, if any nun who has become impassioned, together with a man who has become impassioned, offers, presents, and causes him to experience contact from the eye downward and the knee upward, that nun too has incurred defeat and must not remain in the community.

Furthermore, if any nun who has become impassioned, together with a man who has become impassioned, acts in a manner involving any of the following eight bases while directing her body toward a place that a man could approach a woman — concealment, tossing and swaying, fondling, moving toward a locality, giving a signal or sign, welcoming a man's coming and going, making him her own, or directing her body toward such a place — if a nun does such a thing at any of these eight bases, that nun too has incurred defeat and must not remain in the community.

Furthermore, if any nun, knowing that a certain other nun has committed an act leading to defeat, conceals it; and then when that nun has fallen into decline, or died, or wandered away, or fled to another district or another region, that first nun says: "Noble ones, I knew from before how this nun had committed an act leading to defeat" — that nun too has incurred defeat and must not remain in the community.

Furthermore, if any nun takes the vows of one who is not worthy of receiving the prostration vow from a concordant community of nuns — vows given after a concordant community of monks has performed the act of suspension against a certain monk and that monk remains under suspension — and she causes her hair to stand on end, and her hair to be disheveled, and causes the community of monks grief, stirs them up, draws near to them in a comparable manner, stations herself within the boundary, and while knowing she should ask forgiveness, instead says: "Noble one, your hair has become disheveled —"

"— your hair has become disheveled; do not cause the community of monks to bristle with anger, do not stir them up, do not draw near to them in a comparable manner, do not station yourself within the boundary and ask forgiveness. I shall attend upon you, noble one, without any concern for whatever preoccupies you — your alms bowl, your monastic robes, your net, your cup, your belt, your recitation, your chanting, your yoga, your mental practice — and shall serve you free from attachment to whatever it may be."

If that nun says those words, the other nuns shall address her thus: "Noble one, you have received vows from a concordant community of nuns by which you are not worthy of receiving the prostration vow — this having been done after a concordant community of monks performed the act of suspension against a certain monk. Your hair has stood on end, your hair has become disheveled, you have caused the community of monks to bristle with anger, you have stirred them up, you have drawn near to them in a comparable manner, you have stationed yourself within the boundary, and knowing you should ask forgiveness, you have instead said: 'Noble one, your hair has stood on end, your hair has become disheveled; do not cause the community of monks to bristle with anger, do not stir them up, do not draw near to them in a comparable manner, do not station yourself within the boundary and ask forgiveness. I shall attend upon you, noble one, without any concern for whatever preoccupies you — your alms bowl, your monastic robes, your net, your cup, your belt, your recitation, your chanting, your yoga, your mental practice — and shall serve you free from attachment to whatever it may be.' Do not say such a thing. Noble one, you should abandon this basis that follows in the direction of the act of suspension."

When the nuns have so instructed that nun, if she abandons that basis, then that is well. If she does not abandon it, for the sake of making her abandon it she is to be duly instructed and duly admonished up to a second and third time. If, when duly instructed and duly admonished up to a second and third time, she abandons that basis, then that is well. If she does not abandon it, that nun has also incurred a defeat and is not to remain.

Noble ones, I have now recited the eight factors that constitute defeat.

If a nun commits any one of those transgressions, she incurs a defeat just as she did the first time, and in the same way thereafter; she is not permitted to remain in common dwelling or enjoyment with the community of nuns.

In regard to this I ask you, noble ones: Are you pure in this? I ask a second and third time: Are you pure in this? Since the noble ones are pure in this matter and remain silent, I take it that it is so held.

Summary verse:
The first is lewd conduct; the second, groundless accusation;
The third, the taking of a partial side;
The fourth, acting in whatever way one pleases;
The fifth, absence of attachment —
Traveling at night and by day on the road,
And crossing to the far bank of a river, with consent.

Noble ones, these twenty rules of communal uplift for nuns arise from the fortnightly recitation of the sūtra of individual liberation.

Furthermore, whatever nun addresses a woman with words suited to a man, or addresses a man with words suited to a woman, speaking as if she were his wife or his dear companion — even if it is a chance encounter — this matter constitutes from the first instance a transgression with remnant, requiring communal uplift.

Furthermore, whatever nun, being angry and filled with aversion, thinking by any means whatsoever to sever this one from the celibate life, brings a baseless charge of defeat against a nun who is pure and without transgression — whether she is asked about it at that time or on some other occasion —

even when not asked, and that dispute too is baseless, and the nun speaks while abiding in aversion and out of aversion — this matter also constitutes from the first instance a transgression with remnant, requiring communal uplift.

Furthermore, whatever nun, being angry and filled with aversion, thinking by any means whatsoever to sever this one from the celibate life, slanders a pure nun without transgression by means of a charge corresponding to what is not that one's own part — a charge that would result in defeat — and whether on that occasion or at another time she is asked about it or not asked about it, that dispute too, one corresponding to what is not her own part, is undertaken through mere whim, by mere intention, and the nun speaks while abiding in aversion and out of aversion — this matter also constitutes from the first instance a transgression with remnant, requiring communal uplift.

Furthermore, whatever nun, becoming attached, accepts something, whatever it may be, from a man who has also become attached — this matter also constitutes from the first instance a transgression with remnant, requiring communal uplift.

Furthermore, whatever nun says to another nun words such as these: "Noble sister, if you were not yourself attached, then being without attachment you might accept something, whatever it may be, from a man who has become attached" — this matter also constitutes from the first instance a transgression with remnant, requiring communal uplift.

Furthermore, whatever nun, being alone, departs from the rains retreat dwelling at night — this matter also constitutes from the first instance a transgression with remnant, requiring communal uplift.

Whatever nun, being alone, departs from the rains retreat dwelling during the day — this matter also constitutes from the first instance a transgression with remnant, requiring communal uplift.

Furthermore, whatever nun, being alone, sets out and travels on the road — this matter also constitutes from the first instance a transgression with remnant, requiring communal uplift.

Furthermore, whatever nun, being alone, crosses to the far bank of a river — this matter also constitutes from the first instance a transgression with remnant, requiring communal uplift.

Furthermore, whatever nun knowingly does not ordain a woman who has been fully released by a consenting group and approved by the king — this matter also constitutes from the first instance a transgression with remnant, requiring communal uplift.

Summary verse:

Taking the belongings of the dead,
granting forbearance to a nun,
abandoning, quarreling,
leaning upon and causing others to lean upon,
splitting the Saṅgha and siding with those who do,

Furthermore, whatever nun follows after and clings to the belongings of the dead — this matter also constitutes from the first instance a transgression with remnant, requiring communal uplift.

Furthermore, whatever nun, knowing that the act of suspension has been carried out against a nun by a harmonious assembly of nuns acting in accordance with the Dharma, leads her beyond the boundary and causes her to seek forbearance — this matter also constitutes from the first instance a transgression with remnant, requiring communal uplift.

Furthermore, whatever nun, enraged, agitated, and indignant, being displeased, speaks thus: "I abandon the Buddha, I abandon the Dharma, I abandon the Saṅgha. Among the renunciants who are sons of the Śākyan, only those who possess ethical conduct, who possess good qualities, who are skilled, who have virtuous Dharma — it is not so; there are other renunciants and brahmins who possess ethical conduct, who possess good qualities, who are skilled, who have virtuous Dharma, and from among those I shall practice the celibate life" — if she speaks thus, then the nuns should say to her: "Noble sister, you, enraged, agitated, and indignant, being displeased, are saying: 'I abandon the Buddha, I abandon the Dharma, I abandon the Saṅgha. Among the renunciants who are sons of the Śākyan, only those who possess ethical conduct, who possess good qualities, who are skilled, who have virtuous Dharma — it is not so;

there are other renunciants and brahmins who possess ethical conduct, who possess good qualities, who are skilled, who have virtuous Dharma, and from among those I shall practice the celibate life" — she should not say this. The nuns should instruct her thus: "Noble sister, abandon this kind of view, which is a sinful view." When the nuns have so instructed that nun, if she abandons that ground matter, that is good. If she does not abandon it, in order to have her abandon that ground matter she should be correctly instructed and correctly admonished a second and a third time. If, having been correctly instructed and correctly admonished a second and a third time, she abandons that ground matter, that is good. If she does not abandon it, this rule incurs a transgression at the third repetition, one that is to be confessed and entails a meeting of the remaining Saṅgha.

Furthermore, whatever nun engages in wrangling, the nuns should instruct her thus: "Noble sister, do not wrangle. Let go of hostility. Do not dispute. Do not quarrel." When they have so instructed her and she is stopped, if she should say, "There are certain nuns here who go by desire, who go by aversion, who go by delusion, who go by fear — and of nuns who wrangle in this way, some are expelled and some are not expelled," then the nuns should say to that nun: "Noble sister, do not say that the nuns told you not to wrangle, not to drag up faults, not to dispute, not to quarrel — that having been so stopped you said: 'There are certain nuns here who go by desire, who go by aversion, who go by delusion, who go by fear, and of nuns who wrangle in this way, some are expelled and some are not expelled.' Noble sister, abandon this kind of language of going by desire, going by aversion, going by delusion, and going by fear." When the nuns have so instructed that nun, if she abandons that ground matter, that is good. If she does not abandon it, in order to have her abandon that ground matter she should be correctly instructed and correctly admonished a second and a third time. If, having been correctly instructed and correctly admonished a second and a third time, she abandons that ground matter, that is good. If she does not abandon it, this rule also incurs a transgression at the third repetition, one that is to be confessed and entails a meeting of the remaining Saṅgha.

Furthermore, whatever nun associates with laypeople and, through that association, causes siding with factions, frivolous distraction, and coarse, disruptive conduct, the nuns should instruct her thus: "Noble sister, do not associate with laypeople. Do not, through associating with them, engage in siding with factions, frivolous distraction, and coarse, disruptive conduct. Noble sister, live apart. Know that when you live apart the wholesome qualities will increase, not decline." When the nuns have so instructed that nun, if she abandons that ground matter, that is good. If she does not abandon it, in order to have her abandon that ground matter she should be correctly instructed and correctly admonished a second and a third time. If, having been correctly instructed and correctly admonished a second and a third time, she abandons that ground matter, that is good. If she does not abandon it, this rule also incurs a transgression at the third repetition, one that is to be confessed and entails a meeting of the remaining Saṅgha.

Furthermore, whatever nun, having been instructed by a harmonious assembly of nuns to dwell apart from those particular nuns who live together in a group, abandons that instruction and, knowing full well that those two dwell apart, goes to join that very same side and says: "Sisters, do not the two of you dwell apart. When the two of you dwell apart, you should know that virtuous qualities will only decline and not increase. The two of you should dwell together in a group. When the two of you dwell together in a group, you should know that virtuous qualities will only increase and not decline" — if such a nun speaks in this way, the nuns should instruct her thus: "Sister, you are aware of those nuns who, dwelling together in a group, engage in taking sides, in distraction and confusion, and in harsh and disruptive conduct, and yet knowing full well that those two dwell apart, you say: 'Sisters, do not the two of you dwell apart. When the two of you dwell apart, you should know that virtuous qualities will only decline and not increase. The two of you should dwell together in a group. When the two of you dwell together in a group, you should know that virtuous qualities will only increase and not decline.' Do not say such things. Sister, abandon this view of such a kind, a view that is sinful." The nuns should instruct her in this way. If, having been so instructed by the nuns, she abandons that ground matter, that is good. If she does not abandon it, in order to have her abandon that ground matter she should be correctly instructed and correctly admonished a second and a third time. If, having been correctly instructed and correctly admonished a second and a third time, she abandons that ground matter, that is good. If she does not abandon it, this rule also incurs a transgression at the third repetition, one that is to be confessed and entails a meeting of the remaining Saṅgha.

Furthermore, if a nun, having taken up and firmly held a dispute that would weaken the harmony of the Saṅgha and create a schism, persists in it, the nuns should instruct her thus: "Noble one, do not weaken the harmony of the Saṅgha. Do not take up and firmly hold a dispute that would create a schism. Be in harmony with the Saṅgha. Do not break apart what is harmonious; be universally joyful and without quarrel; be of one voice and one proclamation; become like water and milk blended into one; and, illuminating the Teacher's teaching, dwell in ease. Noble one, abandon this ground matter of such a kind, a kind that divides the Saṅgha." The nuns should instruct her in this way. If, having been so instructed by the nuns, she abandons that ground matter, that is good. If she does not abandon it, in order to have her abandon that ground matter she should be correctly instructed and correctly admonished a second and a third time. If, having been correctly instructed and correctly admonished a second and a third time, she abandons that ground matter, that is good. If she does not abandon it, this rule also incurs a transgression at the third repetition, one that is to be confessed and entails a meeting of the remaining Saṅgha.

If there are one, two, or many nuns who are companions of that nun, who side with her dissenting speech, and if they say to the nuns: "Noble ones, whether for good or ill, do not say anything against this nun. Why is that? Noble ones, this nun speaks the Dharma; this nun speaks the Vinaya; this nun has taken up and firmly holds what is in accordance with Dharma and Vinaya and applies it as her standard; this nun speaks with awareness and not out of ignorance. Whatever she approves and accepts, that we too approve and accept" — if they speak in this way, the nuns should address those nuns thus: "Noble ones, whether for good or ill, do not say anything against this nun."

Why is that? Noble ones, this nun speaks what is contrary to Dharma; this nun speaks what is contrary to Vinaya; this nun has taken up and firmly holds what is contrary to Dharma and Vinaya and applies it as her standard; this nun speaks out of ignorance and not with awareness. Whatever she approves and accepts, noble ones, do not accept that.

Noble ones, do not wish to divide the Saṅgha. Noble ones, wish rather for the unity of the Saṅgha. Noble ones, bring the Saṅgha into harmony. Do not split the harmonious Saṅgha. Be of one joy, without quarrelling, reciting together with one voice, blended like water and milk. If you illuminate the Teacher's teaching in this way, you will dwell in the touch of happiness. Noble ones, you should be instructed to abandon this conduct of taking sides and speaking in discord, which tends toward the splitting of the Saṅgha.

When the nuns are thus instructed by the other nuns, if they relinquish that ground, so much the better. If they do not relinquish it, they should be admonished and instructed a second and a third time for the sake of relinquishing that ground. If, when properly admonished and properly instructed a second and a third time, they relinquish that ground, so much the better. If they do not relinquish it, this matter itself gives rise to a transgression at the second or third time, together with what follows — and that is a Saṅgha remainder.

Now suppose a great many nuns are residing in dependence on a certain town or village, and they are engaging in wicked conduct that brings disgrace upon households — if it is seen, heard, or clearly known that they are bringing disgrace upon households, if it is seen, heard, or clearly known that they are engaging in that wickedness, then the nuns should address those nuns thus: "Noble ones, you are engaging in wicked conduct that brings disgrace upon households. It is seen, heard, and clearly known that you bring disgrace upon households. It is also seen, heard, and clearly known that you engage in wickedness. Noble ones, it is enough for you to remain here; depart from this dwelling from this day forward." You should give them this instruction.

If those nuns then say to the nuns: "Noble ones, there are some nuns here who act out of desire, some who act out of aversion, some who act out of delusion, some who act out of fear — for the very same transgression, some nuns are expelled and some are not expelled" — if they speak in this way, the nuns should address those nuns thus: "Do not say, noble ones, 'There are nuns here who act out of desire, who act out of aversion, who act out of delusion, who act out of fear, and for the very same transgression some nuns are expelled and some are not expelled.' Why is that? It is not that those nuns act out of desire, or act out of aversion, or act out of delusion, or act out of fear. Rather, noble ones, you yourselves are those who engage in wicked conduct that brings disgrace upon households. It is seen, heard, and clearly known that you bring disgrace upon households.

You are also seen, heard, and clearly known to engage in wicked conduct. Therefore, noble ones, you should be admonished to give up such verbal paths as these: 'acting out of desire,' 'acting out of aversion,' 'acting out of delusion,' and 'acting out of fear.'

When the nuns have admonished those nuns in this way, if they relinquish that ground, so much the better. If they do not relinquish it, they should be admonished a second time and a third time, completely and correctly, for the sake of inducing them to relinquish that ground. If, when admonished a second and third time completely and correctly, they relinquish that ground, so much the better. If they do not relinquish it, this matter too becomes a transgression requiring the procedure up to three times, together with a formal act — it is a matter for the remainder of the Saṅgha.

Now, suppose a certain nun here is of a nature that makes her difficult to admonish. When the nuns speak to her in conformity with the Dharma and in conformity with the Vinaya, in accordance with the rules of training pertaining to the recitation and the sūtras of the Sugata (the Well-Gone One), she says: "Noble ones, whether your words are virtuous or non-virtuous, say nothing at all to me. And I too, whether my conduct be virtuous or non-virtuous, will say nothing to the noble ones. Noble ones, abandon this verbal path of admonishing me. What is the use of anything you say to me?" If she thus renders herself one who is not to be spoken to, the nuns should address that nun in this way: "Noble one, do not make yourself one who is not to be spoken to when the nuns speak in conformity with the Dharma and in conformity with the Vinaya, in accordance with the rules of training pertaining to the recitation and the sūtras of the Sugata. Rather, noble one, make yourself one who is to be spoken to alone. When the nuns speak to the noble one in conformity with the Dharma and in conformity with the Vinaya, the noble one too should speak to the nuns in conformity with the Dharma and in conformity with the Vinaya. For it is by this means — by mutually speaking to one another, by mutually instructing and guiding one another, by mutually drawing one another out from transgressions — that the assembly of the Blessed One, the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly and fully awakened buddha, will grow. Noble one, abandon this kind of conduct of making yourself one who is not to be spoken to."

When the nuns have admonished that nun in this way, if she relinquishes that ground, so much the better. If she does not relinquish it, she should be admonished a second time and a third time, completely and correctly, for the sake of inducing her to relinquish that ground. If, when admonished a second and third time completely and correctly, she relinquishes that ground, so much the better. If she does not relinquish it, this matter too becomes a transgression requiring the procedure up to three times, together with a formal act — it is a matter for the remainder of the Saṅgha.

Noble ones, I have now set forth the twenty rules that are matters for the remainder of the Saṅgha. Of these, twelve are transgressions from the very first offense. Eight are so by the procedure up to three times.

Should a nun commit any one of these transgressions and knowingly conceal it for as long as she does so, she must undergo the penalty of unwished-for removal for the same length of time. Having undergone the unwished-for removal, she must thereafter observe the probationary conduct of satisfying both Saṅghas for half a month. Having observed that conduct of satisfaction, and having made the formal act conform to the Dharma in preparation for rehabilitation and satisfied both assemblies, she is then to be rehabilitated in a place where the combined assembly of both Saṅghas numbers at least forty.

If an assembly that is short by even one of the forty members of the combined Saṅgha performs the rehabilitation of that nun, the rehabilitation does not take effect, and those assembled Saṅghas are themselves to be censured. That is the procedure for this matter.

In this regard, I am to ask the noble ones: "Are you pure in this?" I am to ask "Are you pure in this?" a second and a third time. When the noble ones are pure in this, they indicate it by remaining silent, and it is thus understood.

Summary verse:

Keeping, parting with, setting aside,

Noble ones, these thirty-three rules entailing forfeiture are recited every half-month as they arise from the individual liberation sūtra (Skt. prātimokṣasūtra).

A nun who has obtained a robe and pulled out the lining threads is to keep any surplus robe for up to ten days without making it allowable. If she keeps it beyond that, it is a transgression entailing forfeiture. A nun who has obtained a robe and pulled out the lining threads — if even for a single night she separates herself from any one of the five robes beyond the boundary, except with the Saṅgha's permission, it is a transgression entailing forfeiture. A nun who has obtained a robe and pulled out the lining threads, and who then finds herself wanting an out-of-season robe, may accept that robe. Having accepted it, if she rises promptly, she is to have it made into a robe immediately and keep it. If she does not rise promptly and there is a prospect of supplementing the robe with what has not yet been supplemented, she is to set that robe aside until the end of one month. If she keeps it beyond that, it is a transgression entailing forfeiture.

Furthermore, whatever nun washes, scrubs, or beats an old robe belonging to a monk who is not a relative commits a transgression entailing forfeiture. Whatever nun accepts a robe from a monk who is not a relative — except in exchange — commits a transgression entailing forfeiture. Whatever nun goes to the home of a householder or a householder's wife who is not a relative and solicits a robe, except at an appropriate time, commits a transgression entailing forfeiture. The appropriate time is this: if a nun's robe has been stolen, lost, burned, carried off by the wind, or carried away by water — that is the appropriate time. If a nun's robe has been stolen, lost, burned, carried off by the wind, or carried away by water, she may go to the home of a householder or a householder's wife who is not a relative and solicit a robe. If on that occasion a faithful brahmin or householder wishes to offer many robes, that nun may accept from him at most a complete set consisting of an upper robe and a lower robe. If she accepts more than that, it is a transgression entailing forfeiture.

If a householder or a householder's wife who is not a relative has set aside funds for a robe on behalf of a nun, with the thought: "Having set aside these funds for a robe, I shall purchase with these funds such-and-such a robe and, when the nun by the name of such-and-such arrives, I shall at the proper time present her with a suitable robe" — and if, before she has been informed of this, that nun forms some intention and, out of desire for something better, goes to the home of a householder or a householder's wife who is not a relative and says: "Venerable sir, please use the funds set aside as the price of my robe to purchase such-and-such a robe for me, and present me with a suitable robe at the proper time" — and if the robe is duly produced, it is a transgression entailing forfeiture.

If a householder or a householder's wife who is not a relative has each separately set aside funds for a robe on behalf of a nun, with the thought: "We two have each separately set aside these separate funds for robes; we shall purchase such-and-such separate robes with these separate robe-funds, and when the nun by the name of such-and-such arrives, we two shall each separately and at the proper time present her with a suitable robe" —

Now, in that situation, if before she has been approached, some thought arises in that nun — out of desire for something fine — and she goes to the householder or the householder's wife who is not a relative and says: "How good it is, venerable ones, that you have set aside separate robe-funds on my behalf! With those separate robe-funds, please each purchase such-and-such separate robes, then pool them together and present me with a suitable robe at the proper time" — and if the robe is duly produced, it is a transgression entailing forfeiture.

If robe-funds have been sent by a king, or a great minister, or a brahmin, or a householder, or a village man, or a country man, or a man of wealth, or a merchant, or a caravan leader, in the hand of a messenger on behalf of a nun, and that messenger takes those robe-funds and goes to where that nun is and says to her: "Noble one, please be aware: a king, or a great minister, or a brahmin, or a householder, or a village man, or a country man, or a man of wealth, or a merchant, or a caravan leader — or the mistress of a caravan leader — has sent these robe-funds for you. Noble one, out of compassion please accept them" — then that nun should say to the messenger: "Venerable messenger, it is not fitting for nuns to accept robe-funds. When we find a suitable robe at the proper time, we will accept it."

If that messenger then says to the nun: "Noble one, is there any steward who serves the noble ones, any steward who acts on behalf of the noble ones?" — then that nun, wishing for a robe, should point out to the messenger either the monastery steward or a lay steward, saying: "That person is a steward who serves the nuns; that one acts on behalf of the nuns."

Then that messenger should take the robe-funds and go to where that steward is, and on arriving should say to the steward: "Venerable steward, please know this: with these robe-funds, purchase such-and-such a robe, and when the nun by the name of such-and-such arrives, present her with a suitable robe at the proper time."

Then that messenger, having thoroughly and correctly instructed and indicated this to the steward, should go to where that nun is, and on arriving should say to the nun: "Noble one, the steward you pointed out to me — I have correctly indicated the matter to him. Please go to him, and he will present you with a suitable robe at the proper time."

That nun, wishing for a robe, should go to the steward and remind him, saying two or three times: "Venerable steward, I need a robe. Venerable steward, I need a robe." She should prompt and remind him two or three times. If the robe is then produced, that is excellent. If it is not produced, she should remain silent — neither speaking nor pressing the matter in any direction — up to four, five, or six times. If, after remaining silent up to four, five, or six times, the robe is produced, that is excellent. If it is not produced, she may then exert herself to have the robe obtained; and if the robe is thereby obtained, it is a transgression entailing forfeiture. If it is not produced, she should herself go to the place from which those robe-funds came.

Alternatively, through a trustworthy messenger she may send word to those persons, saying: "Venerable ones, you sent robe-funds as an offering for such-and-such a nun; know that nothing has been accomplished for that nun. Venerable ones, please take care that your own wealth is not lost." That is the proper procedure in such a case.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The verse "When I have passed into nirvāṇa, this shall be your Teacher" is a well-known canonical declaration attributed to the Buddha regarding the Prātimokṣa as his substitute after his parinirvāṇa. The attribution "the Self-Arisen One" in that stanza refers to the Buddha himself, using an epithet more commonly associated with Brahmā in other contexts; here it functions as a distinctive honorific for the Buddha in this liturgical framing.

The phrase rendered "market-treasury of training, the trading-house of the community of monks" uses mercantile metaphors present in the Tibetan, somewhat unusual doctrinally but clearly intentional as a literary device praising the Prātimokṣa's value.  The verse "with ten fingers joined" through "hundredfold fitting nail" is a mnemonic gāthā presenting the Prātimokṣa as a sharp peg or nail that curbs the wandering mind. The image of the "sharp, hundredfold fitting nail" (mthun pa gzer rnon brgya pa) is a traditional simile for the restraining force of the Prātimokṣa rules on a wandering mind compared to a horse difficult to bridle.

The summary verse at the close names the eight pārājika (defeat) rules for nuns. The identifications "child of a sākha," "perfume-selling youth," and "person born of a slave" are compressed allusions to three of the eight specific rules unique to the bhikṣuṇī Prātimokṣa; their precise referents depend on the full narrative context of each rule and may vary slightly across Vinaya traditions.  The eighth defeat rule is left deliberately incomplete, as the Tibetan passage itself breaks off mid-sentence with the words addressed to the suspended monk. This is a continuation passage and the rule concludes in the subsequent text.

The phrase rendered "the conceit of pride" translates a technical term indicating a specific form of arrogance or presumption, namely the mistaken self-regard that leads one to overclaim spiritual attainments.
