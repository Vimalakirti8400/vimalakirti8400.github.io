The Six-Limbed Yoga (Yogaṣaḍaṅga)

The Six-Limbed Yoga, known in Sanskrit as the Yoga Ṣaḍaṅga and in Tibetan as the Rnal 'byor yan lag drug pa, is a brief verse instruction on the sixfold yoga of the completion stage of tantric practice. Opening with homage to Vajradhara, it names the six limbs of withdrawal, meditative concentration, breath control, retention, subsequent mindfulness and meditative absorption, and then describes each in turn: the gathering of the winds into the central channel, the signs that arise in meditation, the holding of wind and mind in the drops of the cakras, the blaze of the inner heat, and finally the luminous visions into which the mind dissolves. It is Toh 1375 in the Tengyur, where it belongs among the commentaries and instructional works on the tantras. According to its colophon it was taught by the glorious Śabareśvara to the Indian paṇḍita Vibhūticandra, who himself rendered it into Tibetan.

In the language of India: yoga ṣaḍaṅga nāma. In the language of Tibet: The Six-Limbed Yoga.

Homage to the glorious Vajradhara!

Withdrawal, meditative concentration,
breath control, and likewise retention,
subsequent mindfulness, and meditative absorption:
these are taught as the six limbs of yoga.

Through the three that are unmoving,
the ten winds enter the dhūtī, the central channel;
outer and inner conceptual thought ceases and the signs arise:
this is the limb of withdrawal (pratyāhāra).

The signs come to be seen as stable;
wisdom, conception and analysis, joy and bliss,
and the one-pointed, unmoving, nonconceptual state:
these are the limb of meditative concentration.

When the ten winds have entered the central channel,
the winds themselves move in and out
within the drops of the four cakras:
this is the limb of breath control (prāṇāyāma).

When the life-wind has entered within the drop,
wind and mind are held in the drop
of haṃ in the cakra of great bliss:
this is the limb of retention (dhāraṇā).

By the power of wind and mind being held in the drop,
the light of the fire of caṇḍālī, the inner heat, appears,
and a special bliss and warmth arise:
this is the limb of subsequent mindfulness (anusmṛti).

When the ten signs have become complete,
the appearance of the great drop arises.
Within the great variegated drop
the figure of a black line (rekhā) arises.
Then the six classes of beings and the like,
extremely subtle emanation bodies,
and many enjoyment bodies are seen:
this is the luminosity of variegated reflected forms.
When the mind dissolves into that,
the limb of meditative absorption is brought to its culmination.

The Six-Limbed Yoga, taught by the glorious Śabareśvara to the paṇḍita Mahā Vibhūticandra. He himself translated it into Tibetan and taught it.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

"The three that are unmoving" is read in the usual six-yoga sense of the threefold stillness (of body, gaze and mind, or of body, speech and mind); the text does not specify. In the prāṇāyāma verse the Tibetan reads rlung bcud bu mang, which makes no sense as written; it is taken as a slip for rlung bcu dbu mar, "the ten winds into the central channel," matching the earlier verse. In the next-to-last verse, pra ba is read as phra ba, "subtle." In the colophon the paṇḍita's name is spelled Vibhūhicandra, evidently a slip for Vibhūticandra. The phrase sgra rang 'gyur is taken to mean that Vibhūticandra made the Tibetan translation himself.
