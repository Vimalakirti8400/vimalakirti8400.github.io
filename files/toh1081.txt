The Dhāraṇī Called Obstructing Generosity

The Dhāraṇī Called Obstructing Generosity is a short scripture whose title is given only in Tibetan, with no Sanskrit title recorded, which suggests it may have been composed or compiled in Tibet rather than translated from an Indian original. After paying homage to all the buddhas and bodhisattvas, it warns that obstructing the generosity of others brings consequences even worse than stinginess. It illustrates this through a story in which the noble disciples Śāriputra and Maudgalyāyana, while meditating in a remote place, encounter a tormented female hungry ghost whose wretched appearance reveals the karmic results of such conduct. The text is Toh 1081 in the Kangyur, where it belongs among the dhāraṇī texts, a class of works centered on incantations and their associated teachings.

In the Tibetan language: The Dhāraṇī Called Obstructing Generosity.

Homage to all the buddhas and bodhisattvas.

Far worse even than stinginess is the evil result of obstructing the generosity of others. It is like this.

The noble Śāriputra and the noble Maudgalyāyana were staying in a certain remote place, attending to discerning wisdom. At midday the two went out to a spot where they would take the air, and there they met a female hungry ghost. Her body was blue and emaciated, and she made a rattling "khok khok" sound. Her hair was matted, her eyes were red and squinted to the left, and her long lower lip was twisted to the right. Her neck, arms, and legs were extremely thin and long. The nails of her clenched fists had pierced straight through her hands, and those nails were a livid bluish green. From her mouth she cried out five kinds of sounds, namely: "If it is needed," "If there is none," "Do not give!", "Even if you give, do not make it big; it will turn out to be too much," and "That much is too much; reduce it and give." She was a terrifying sight.

Then the two thought, "Compared with the wilderness, the village is near and food is plentiful." So they went to the town and begged, and they obtained much food and drink: the three white substances, the three sweet substances, rice, barley flour, and so forth. They carried it to the hungry ghost and offered it to her, but she would not accept it. Crying out the five sounds described above, she fled.

At that the two were deeply troubled. Their minds were so taken up with the female hungry ghost that they could think of nothing else, and they went at once to where the Blessed One was staying in the Bamboo Grove. When they bowed at his feet, they were so breathless and exhausted that they could not bring themselves to ask their question.

The Blessed One said, "Sons of good family, what is wrong with you? If you have a question, catch your breath and ask it."

"Blessed One, we are deeply troubled. We hurried to you in great anxiety, and we are out of breath."

He said, "What has happened?"

They said, "The two of us were in a certain remote place, attending to discernment as the Blessed One taught, and we went out a long way to walk. There we met a female hungry ghost whose body was blue and emaciated. She made a rattling 'khok khok' sound. Her hair was matted, her eyes were red and squinted to the left, and her long lower lip was twisted to the right. Her neck, arms, and legs were extremely thin and long. The nails of her clenched fists had pierced straight through her hands and were a livid bluish green. From her mouth she cried out five kinds of sounds, namely: 'If it is needed,' 'If there is none,' 'Do not give!', 'Even if you give, do not make it big; it will turn out to be too much,' and 'That much is too much; reduce it and give.' She was a terrifying sight. The two of us thought, 'Compared with the wilderness, the village is near and food is plentiful,' and so we went to the town and begged. We obtained much food and drink: the three white substances, the three sweet substances, rice, barley flour, and so forth. We carried it to that female hungry ghost and offered it to her, but she uttered from her mouth the five sounds described above, would not accept anything we gave, and ran off. So we have come to ask what caused this, and what means there are to help her."

He said, "Sons of good family, that is most excellent. I shall explain, so listen well and keep it in mind. What caused this? Her blue and emaciated body comes from not daring to eat anything herself. Her thin neck comes from her being stingy when others ate and enjoyed things. Her thin limbs, with the nails piercing straight through her fingers, come from telling other donors, 'That is too much; reduce it and give,' and from not daring to enjoy anything herself. Her matted hair comes from doing what displeased everyone. Her red eyes come from being completely swollen with desire. Their squint to the left comes from looking at others with an ill-willed eye. Her long lower lip twisted to the right comes from saying, 'Even if you give, it will be too much.' Her crying out the five kinds of sounds is the result that corresponds to her deeds, arising from the growth of the habitual tendencies of what she used to say.

"Through the power of such karma, nothing that others give can help her. Apart from water alone, nothing can be given to her. So that the gift is not obstructed as others' gifts are, even now, take clean water that has not been touched by barley flour, recite the mantra over it once, snap your fingers once, and give it. Her continuum will then be freed and her desires satisfied. The mantra is this:

oṃ jvala midaṃ sarva preta bhyaḥ svāhā"

They asked, "Please explain the meaning of its syllables."

He said, "oṃ is the nature of the five wisdoms, the ultimate. jvala is desire blazing like fire. midaṃ is the stinginess and obstruction of generosity committed in a human continuum. sarva is all that has come about from such karma. preta is the essence of the hungry ghosts, meaning the ripening of the continuum of karma. bhyaḥ means to purify karma and suffering. svāhā means 'May karma, suffering, hunger, and thirst be purified.' Now go quickly and nourish her in this way."

When he had said this, the two went quickly and did as he instructed, and at last she was glad, enjoyed what was given, and her body recovered somewhat.

Then, when the time came for the Dharma teaching, the two took turns. After Śāriputra had eaten his meal, he gave her a portion to eat, but when he gave her the water, she again cried out as before and fled. Once more the two were dismayed and went to ask the Blessed One.

"Blessed One, we did as you said, and it seemed she could be nourished. But fearing that the time for the Dharma teaching would pass, we hurried, and again she cried out as before and fled. So we have come to ask once more."

He said, "That is because you took it with a hand that had barley flour between the nails. First wash your hands, and then give. There is no need to go to her; doing it right here will suffice."

The two washed and gave the water offering, and the female hungry ghost came to that very place and became somewhat radiant.

Then the Blessed One spoke again: "A water offering such as this is not for this occasion only. At all times make it your commitment and give it. Spread it widely to others as well. Why is that? Its benefits are immeasurable, beyond all limits. To state them only in brief: the merit of giving to the rich people of the world does not come near even a hundredth part of the merit of giving to a few poor people. Why is that? Because the rich, in their pride, arrogance, and conceit, are eager to take up negative states. And the merit of giving to the poor people of the world does not come near even a hundredth part of the merit of giving to a few hungry ghosts.

Why is that? The poor can make an effort, they can search, and they can beg. Hungry ghosts, because of their karma, are not like that. If food is not dedicated to them, then even when it reaches their mouths it turns into a deadly poison. And the merit of giving to the hungry ghosts of the world does not come near even a hundredth part of the merit of giving water to a single hungry ghost of obstruction. Why is that? Those other hungry ghosts can be satisfied by visualization, satisfied by offerings set out, satisfied by dedication, satisfied by touch, satisfied by leftovers, and satisfied by rinsing water, each according to the course of their karma. But the hungry ghost of obstruction, because of its karma of having obstructed others, cannot receive anything through other mantras, mudrās, or visualizations. Because it clung to all goods and held them back from others, it cannot taste any other substance. Because the only thing it did not hold back from others was water, it can receive nothing but pure water untouched by barley flour. It cannot receive anything by any means other than the mantra that pacifies its own mind-stream and the mudrā of a single finger-snap. Why is it set as a single snap? If there are two, the sound becomes someone else's and the ghost receives nothing. Therefore it must be a single sound, the one that awakens its own understanding.

Through this practice one is not overcome by obstacles, one has little illness, a long life, and abundant wealth. It frees those who garble their syllables, and it restores brightness to those whose radiance has faded. If one spreads this to others, one's mind-stream is liberated, the line of rebirth as a hungry ghost is cut, and in the end one becomes a fully and perfectly awakened buddha.

When he had spoken these words, those sons of good family praised what the Blessed One had taught.

This completes the dhāraṇī called Obstruction.

The title term, rendered "obstructing generosity," refers to discouraging or hindering others' giving, as the body of the text makes clear. The opening gives only a Tibetan title, with no Sanskrit title. The clause "So that the gift is not obstructed as others' gifts are" renders an obscure phrase (literally "because others' manner of obstruction is not done"); its exact sense is uncertain. The phrase rendered "he gave her a portion to eat" after Śāriputra's meal is also terse and its precise sense uncertain. The explanations of the mantra syllables are traditional word-plays (for instance, linking midaṃ with the Tibetan word for "human") rather than Sanskrit etymologies.  The title term, rendered here as "Obstruction," refers, as the text's opening makes clear, to the fault of preventing others from giving, a fault said to be worse than stinginess. The "hungry ghost of obstruction" is a ghost reborn through that karma. In this passage the Tibetan word often meaning "tantra" plainly means the "mind-stream" or "continuum," and is translated that way. The phrase rendered "frees those who garble their syllables" is somewhat uncertain. It probably refers to impaired speech, though it could also mean that the practice makes garbled letters or words clear.
