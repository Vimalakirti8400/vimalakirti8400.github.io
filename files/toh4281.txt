Commentary in Eight Thousand Lines on Sādhukīrti's Mañjuśrī Grammar

This work is part of a Sanskrit grammatical commentary that its colophons call the Commentary in Eight Thousand Lines on Sādhukīrti's Mañjuśrī Grammar, composed by Rājadeva, master of glorious Kaliṅga; in Tibetan the grammar is called the Jampal gyi Dra Daytröpa, and the Sanskrit form of the full title is not certain. It opens with homage to Mañjughoṣa and works rule by rule through the secondary suffixes of Sanskrit, the taddhita endings that form words meaning "descendant of," "belonging to," "made of," "fit for," "the state of," and many related senses, giving each rule, a short explanation and examples, in close parallel to the corresponding sections of Pāṇini's grammar and the Kāśikā commentary. The portion translated here covers the fifth chapter of that commentary, and its colophon records that the Zhalu translator Dharmapālabhadra rendered it into Tibetan at the command of the fourth Shamarpa, beginning at Densa Til and finishing at Drathang. It is Toh 4281 in the Tengyur, where it belongs among the treatises on grammar, the science of language.

After ṅas, in the sense of descendant.

na ba tanti: "the forebears do not fall, because of this one"; that is the descendant. When a descendant of that one is to be expressed, aṇ comes after a stem ending in the sixth case. Thus the descendant of Upagu is au pa ga paḥ, and the descendant of Aśvapati is a śva pa taḥ.

After at, iṇ.

Further: godhā, ka la śa, a bī ci ro ha na, bhā ra ta, bhā ma ra, mu ruṣṭa, mṛ kaṇḍa, karbū ra i ta ra, a nya ta ra, de ba ta ra, ā liḍha, su datta, su dakṣa ka dru, su nā man, karṣa gu da, a ka śā ya, śa ka la ku mī ri ka, ku be ri kā, kvi śva ri kā, jihmā śi na, pā datta śa ka la, śa ba la, kha dvū ra ka, amba ki, a śo ki ka, gandha biṃ ga la, khe ḍonmattā, kundā śa ku ni, a nu pa, sṛṣṭa, ja ra tin, śu kra, u gra, jī ba, śvan, a śva, a ji ra. This is an open class defined by type: gāṅge yaḥ, bai mā tre yaḥ. Some say the rule reads "from su bhra and the rest." In their system the descendant of Subhrū is sau bhre yaḥ.

From bi karṇa ku svī ta ka, in the sense of Kāśyapa.

After bi karṇa and ku sī ta ka, ḍha ka comes in the sense of a descendant of Kāśyapa: bai karṇe ya, kau sī ta ke yaḥ. Otherwise bai karṇiḥ.

From bhru, also buk.

ḍak comes in the sense of descendant, together with the augment (ā ga ma) buk: bhrau be yaḥ.

For ka lyā ṇi and the rest, in.

ḍhak comes in the sense of descendant, and there is also the change to i naṅ: ka lyā ṇi ni yaḥ, sau bhā gi ne yaḥ. The group is: ka lyā ṇī, su bha gā, durbha gā, bandha kī, a nu sriṣṭi, ja ra dri, ba lī bardī, jyeṣṭhā, ka nāṣṭhi, ma dhya mā pa rastrī.

From ku la ṭā, optionally.

When ḍhak is applied, after ku la ṭā the change to i naṅ comes optionally. The descendant of ku la ṭa is kau la ti ne yaḥ or kau le ṭe yiḥ. The example given here is of a woman who goes about among families while her conduct is not corrupted. One whose conduct is corrupted falls under the definition of a base woman, and e rag comes: kau la ṭe raḥ.

From ca tra ka, also ai ra ka.

It comes in the sense of descendant: cā ṭa kai raḥ. Because a particular gender is mentioned, it also comes from ca ṭa kā.

In the feminine it is dropped.

The ai ra ka introduced after ca ṭa kā is dropped in the sense of a female descendant. Thus the female child of a ca ṭa ka, or of a female ca ṭa ka, is strī ca ṭa kā. When ai rak has been dropped from the word ca ṭa ka, then, since it is gone, the dropping is governed by the rule "go nya and the rest," so that ṭā pa is withdrawn; but again, from a ca and the rest, by the defining mark of a class, dī pa would come, and by the exception (a bā da), ṭā pa comes.

From jānṭa and pānṭa, a rak.

It comes in the sense of descendant: jāṇṭā raḥ, paṇḍā raḥ.

From go dhā, also erak.

After the word go dha, e rag comes, and ā rak as well: gau dhe raḥ, gau dhā raḥ. Because the word belongs to the śu bhra group, dhak comes too: gau dha yaḥ. Some apply ā rak and ai rak in the sense of the descendant of a vicious creature.

From base women, optionally.

e rak comes in the sense of descendant: kā ṇe raḥ or kā re yaḥ; dā se raḥ or dā se yaḥ. "Base" means lacking in body or lacking in moral discipline. Because the meaning is baseness, the natures that the word expresses are presented as base.

After bhrārtṛ, bya.

For the son of a cousin, and also in the sense of patne, it is a fixed form: sa patne tvād u dātta. The word sa patna is a synonym for enemy.

cha.

After the word bhrā ti, the suffix cha comes in the sense of descendant: bhra trī yaḥ.

From svo si.

After the word sva si, cha comes in the sense of descendant: so srī yaḥ.

When preceded by bi tri or mā tri, chan.

After the word sva sri preceded by po tri or mī tri, chan comes in the sense of descendant: prai tri ṣvi srī yaḥ.

With ḍha ka, elision.

After the word sva sri preceded by prai tri or mā tri, when ḍhak comes, its final is elided; and by this very indication ḍhak also comes: sai tre ṣva se ya, mā tri ṣva sa yaḥ.

From kṣa tra, in the sense of class, gha.

When the class is to be conveyed, gha comes after the word kṣa tra in the sense of descendant: the descendant of kṣa tra is kṣa tri yaḥ. Why is it said "in the sense of class"? Because otherwise we have kṣā tṛḥ.

From rā jan, ya ta.

After the word rā jan, in the sense of class, yat comes in the sense of descendant: rā ma janyaḥ, if he is of the royal class; otherwise rā janaḥ.

From ma nu, also as, with ṣu ka.

After the word ma nu, when the class is to be conveyed, aṇ comes, and yat as well; and when these are applied there is also the augment (ā ga ma) ṣuk: mā nu ṣāḥ, ma nī yaḥ. When a mere descendant is meant, there is only aṇ: mā na pī, meaning creatures. In the sense of a contemptible (ku tis ta) descendant, there is only ṇa, because the word belongs to the pṛ ṣo da ra group: mā ṇa baḥ.

From śva śur.

yat comes in the sense of descendant: śva śuryāḥ.

From ku la, also ḍhak.

From ku la, in the sense of descendant: for the descendant of ku la, ḍha kañ comes, and yat as well: kau le yakaḥ, ku leḥ.

From what ends in it, also kha.

After what ends in the word ku la alone, kha comes in the sense of descendant: ā ḍhya ku lī naḥ, ku lī naḥ.

After dur, ḍhak optionally.

After the word ku la that follows dur, ḍhak also comes in the sense of descendant: dauṣku lī naḥ.

From ma hā ku la, aṇ and khañ.

After the word ma ha sku la, aṇ and khañ come optionally in the sense of descendant: ma hā ku laḥ, ma hā ku lī naḥ.

From gṛṣṭi and the rest, and from the names of quadrupeds, ḍhañ.

It comes in the sense of descendant: garṣṭe yaḥ, hārṣṭe yaḥ. The group is: gṛṣṭi hriṣṭa, ha li, bā li, bisna, ka ḍi, a ja basti, mi tra yu. The graṣṭa group is stated for words that do not denote quadrupeds. For the words a ja basti and mi tra yu the rule applies where aṇ would be obtained, and for the rest where ḍhak would be obtained by the rule "after āt, when it is not iñ." For quadrupeds it is obligatory: śā ba le yaḥ, bā hu le yaḥ.

From re ba tī and the rest, ṭhak.

It comes in the sense of descendant: rai ba ti kaḥ, a śva pā li kaḥ. The group is: re ba tīḥ, a śva pā līḥ, ma trī pā lī, bṛk pañci na, pṛ ka grā hā, daṇḍa grvā hā, kukku ṭā kṣa. The rule applies where ḍhak would be obtained after what ends in dī, aṇ after what ends in ṇa, and añ after āt.

From a lineage form, for a woman, in contempt, ṇa ca.

After what ends in a lineage suffix, when a woman is meant and contempt is expressed, ṭhak comes, and also ṇa ca. The descendant of a woman of the line of gārga is gārga rko jāllaḥ, or glo cu kā ya naḥ. Why is it said "in contempt"? Because otherwise we have gārge yaḥ, a brahmin boy. Why is it said "lineage"? Because otherwise we have au pa ga be yaḥ. Since only the relation to the father's kin is indicated, the descendant is shown to be a contemptible one (kutsa).

For sau bā ri and the rest, optionally.

"From lineage" continues to apply. For the descendants, grandsons and so on of the renowned line of the sau bī ra and others, when the descendant of such a woman is meant in contempt, ṭhak comes optionally. The descendant, grandson or later offspring of bha ga bitta is bha ga bitti; his descendant is bha ga bṛtti ka jānmaḥ, or else bha ga bittā ya naḥ. The descendant, grandson or later offspring of Tṛṇabindu is tārṇa bandi bī; his descendant is tārṇa bandi bi kaḥ, or else tārṇa bandi ba yaḥ. The descendant, grandson or later offspring of ā ka ṣā ya is ā ka ṣā ye yī, with ḍha ka, because the word belongs to the śu bhra group. His descendant is ā ka ṣā ye yīḥ, with iñ, because the bā hu group is an open class defined by type. Why is it said "for the sau bī ra"? Because otherwise we have au ba ga be yaḥ jānmaḥ. It is said precisely "in contempt"; otherwise tārṇa bandi pe yaḥ.

After phi ña, also cha.

For the renowned lines of the so so bā ra, after what ends in phi ña, when the descendant of a woman is meant in contempt, cha comes, and ṭhak as well. The descendant of ya mundā is ya mundhā ya niḥ; his descendant is yā mundhā ya ni kaḥ. The descendant of su yā man is sau yā mā ya niḥ; his descendant is sau yā ma nī yaḥ or sau yā mā ya ni kaḥ. The descendant of bṛṣ is bārṣyā ya niḥ; his descendant is bārṣya ya ṇī yaḥ or bārṣya ya ṇī kaḥ. It is said precisely "for the sau bī ra"; otherwise tai kā ya nirjālmaḥ. It is said precisely "in contempt"; otherwise yā mundā ya nāḥ. In both cases aṇ is dropped.

From phanṭā hrī ti, ṇa and phiñ.

After phaṭvā tri ti, for the line of the sau bī ra, ṇa and phiñ come: phraddhā hre taḥ, phruddhā hri tā ya niḥ. It is said precisely "for the sau bī ra"; otherwise phā nṭā hri tā ya nāḥ.

From mi ma ta.

ṇa and phiñ come in the sense of descendant: mai ma taḥ, mai mā tā ya niḥ. It is said precisely "for the sau bī ra"; otherwise mai ma tā ya naḥ, with phak, because the word belongs to the na ḍa group.

From ku ru and the rest, ṇya.

After words such as ku ru and the like, ṇya comes in the sense of descendant: kau ra byaḥ, gargaryaḥ, kai śi nyaḥ. The word ku ru is read in this list in a sense other than the royal class; therefore in the plural we have kau ra byāḥ. ra tha kā raḥ, jā tyanta ram: only from a word denoting a craftsman does ṇya come, for the immediate descendant. The word ke śi nī is taught as ending in dī pa, and so it is not to be taken as a form arising merely by implication.

Because the word takṣan is read here, ṇya comes. Because it denotes a craftsman, iñ comes: tākṣa ṇiḥ. Because it is read in the śi ba group, aṇ comes: tākṣṇaḥ. The group is: ku ruḥ, garga ra, mañju ṣu, a ja mā ra, ra tha kā ra, ca pa ṭa ka, kak li, ma di bā ka, pi kṛ, man, aṇḍa jā li, dhā ta ki, dha mo ṣṇī ṣa, karṇa kīd, kai śva ri ka piñja la, kū ṭa, śa lā kā, pu rā, e ḍa kā, dharmha, ke śi nī dhe nu, sūrya, nyā ya, jñā nā yaḥ, śyā ba ra tha, syā pu tra, be dhā ta ki, ni tāntā bṛkṣa, ārdra bri kā ra, satyadkā ra bṛ ha tiṃ kā ra, pa thi kā ra ni, śambhu, ha nu, śaṃ ku, i ṇa, paṇḍī, takṣa na, bā mar thyā ya na.

From sam rā ja, in the sense of the royal class.

After the word sam rā ja, ṇya comes in the sense of descendant: the descendant of sam rā ja is samrā jaḥ, if he is of the royal class; otherwise not.

From what ends in sā nā, and from kā ru and lakṣan, also iñ.

From a stem ending in se nā, from a craftsman, and from lakṣan, iñ also comes. That is, after what ends in se nā, after a word denoting a craftsman, and after lakṣan, iñ comes in the sense of descendant, and also ṇya: hā ri ṣe ṇiḥ or ha ri ṣe ṇyaḥ; bhai ma si niḥ or bhai ma se ñaḥ; jā ta se niḥ or jā ta se nyaḥ; be śvakse niḥ or be śvakse nyaḥ; tāntra bā yiḥ or tāntra bā yyaḥ; lākṣa ṇiḥ, lakṣa ṇiḥ or lakṣa ṇyaḥ.

From tik and the rest, phañ comes.

phiñ comes in the sense of descendant: tai kā ya niḥ, kai ta bā ya niḥ, kau ra byā ya niḥkau. The word kau ra byaḥ is to be taken here as denoting the royal class. The word au ra sa is read because it goes together with what denotes the royal class; whichever of ku ru and the rest ends in ṇa takes iñ alone. That is dropped by the rule "caṇya" and the rest. The group is: ti ka, ka ti pa, saṃ jñā bā ṇa, u khā, u ras, sendhya ba, sā dhya, ya munda, rū pya, grā mya, tai la, nī la, gra sa, a mi tra, ku ru de, pa ra, tai ta la, au ras, kaur bya, bhau ri ki, bhau li ki, cau pa ya ta, kṣai ta ya ta, candra mas, śu bha, gaṅ gā ba re ṇya, bastra, ā ba ṭyā ya na, kha lyā kā, dhya nau mi kā, sū yā man, u da nya, ya jña.

From da gu, ko śa la karmā raccha ga and bri ṣa, also yu ṭa.

phiñ comes in the sense of descendant, and also the augment (ā ga ma) uṭ: dā ga byā yi naḥ, kau śa lyā yi naḥ, kārmārya ya ṇaḥ, cchā gya ya niḥ, bārṣyā ya niḥ.

After aṇ, from a stem of two vowels.

After a stem of two vowels ending in aṇ, phiñ comes in the sense of descendant: kāttrā ya niḥ, śai bā ya niḥ. Why is it said "from a stem of two vowels"? Because otherwise we have au pa ga bīḥ. Why is it said "after aṇ"? Because otherwise we have dākṣā ya ṇa.

From tyat and the rest, optionally.

phiñ comes in the sense of descendant: tyā dā ya niḥ, ya dāḥ ya niḥ; or else tyā daḥ, tā daḥ, yā daḥ.

From what is not a lineage form, phiñ.

After a stem that is not a lineage form, phiñ comes optionally in the sense of descendant: glu cu kā ya niḥ, mū cu kā ya niḥ, or mlau cu kiḥ. Because the option is a restricted one, as explained, in some cases it does not come: dākṣaḥ, plākṣaḥ. Why is it said "from what is not a lineage form"? Because otherwise we have au ba ga biḥ.

From a bṛddha stem, phiñ.

After a bṛddha stem that does not end in a lineage suffix, phiñ comes optionally: ā mṛ guptā ya niḥ or ā mra guptiḥ; grā ma rakṣā ya naḥ or grā ma rakṣaḥ; īṣṭa tvānnā pi tā ya niḥ or na pi tiḥ. Why is it said "from a bṛddha stem"? Because otherwise we have yā jña dattiḥ. It is said precisely "from what is not a lineage form"; otherwise au pa ga bīḥ.

For bā ki na and the rest, also kuṭ.

phiñ comes in the sense of descendant, and also the augment (ā ga ma) kuṭ: bā kin kā yā niḥ or bā ki niḥ; kā re ṭha kā ya niḥ or gā re ṭhiḥ. The group is: bā ki ni ma gā re ṭhaḥ, kārka ṭya kā ka laṃ kaḥ, carminaḥ, barmin.

From what ends in pu tra, optionally.

For the descendant of what ends in pu trā, after a bṛddha stem that is not a lineage form, phiñ comes optionally; and by the obligatory application of bhatsan, kuṭ comes as well: gārgī pu tra kā ya niḥ, gārgī pu trā ya ṇiḥ, gārgī pu triḥ. This is stated where phiñ is already established by the option given in "from a bīddha stem"; its purpose is to make the augment (ā ga ma) optional.

From the name of a country, in the sense of the royal class, aṇ, and also in the sense of rā jan.

From whatever is the name of a country, the stem (aṅga), when it expresses a descendant of the royal class figuratively regarded as not different from that country, and also in the sense of rā ja na, aṇ comes. The descendant, or the king, of the Ikṣvākus is ai kṣvā kaḥ; by the set form in the sūtra "dāṇḍhi nā yan" and the rest, the letter u is elided. Likewise mā ga dhaḥ, kā li gaḥ, śau ra ma saḥ, pāñcā laḥ, bai de haḥ. Why is it said "from the name of a country"? Because otherwise we have dā pā ra thiḥ. Why is it said "in the sense of the royal class"? Because the descendant of a brahmin of the Pañcālas is pañca liḥ; likewise gāndha riḥ.

From śā lvi ya.

In the sense of descendant, and also in the sense of rā ja na, aṇ comes: the descendant of the Gandhāras, or the king of the Gandhāras, is gnadha rāḥ; likewise śā lve yaḥ. Since each is connected separately, the pairing is not one of corresponding order.

From a bṛddha stem, ñyaṅ.

From the name of a country, in the sense of the royal class, for the descendant of an elder, and also in the sense of rā jan, ñyaṅ comes: āmbaṣṭhyaḥ, sau bīdyaḥ.

From i taḥ, ko śa la and a jā da.

After what ends in it, and after ko śa la and a jā da, in the sense of descendant and also of king, gasum ṅa comes. The descendant of kunta, or the king of the kunti, is kauntyaḥ; likewise ā bantyaḥ, kau ṣa lyaḥ, ā jā dyaḥ. Why is it made "followed by t"? Because the descendant of the royal class of the country named Kumārī is kau mā raḥ.

From a stem of two vowels, aṇ.

For the descendant, and also the king, of the royal class bearing a country name of two vowels, aṇ comes; this is an exception to añ: adgaḥ, badgaḥ. The mention of aṇ, where the form is already established by añ, serves for phiñ.

From names beginning with n, and from ku ru, ṇya.

When the royal class bearing a country name that begins with the letter n is expressed, and also after the word ku ru, ṇya comes in the sense of descendant and also of king; this is an exception to añ. The descendant, or the king, of the ni ca ka is nai ca kyaḥ; likewise nai ṣadhyaḥ, kau ra byaḥ.

From pūṇḍa, yaṇ.

It comes in the sense of descendant, and also of king: pāṇḍyaḥ. It is said precisely "from the name of a country"; otherwise pāṇḍa baḥ.

From śā lvā a ba ya ba, pra tya gra tha, kā la kū ṭa and aśma ka, iñ.

The descendant of that royal class of the śā lvā is śā lve śāsmā de ba, by a set form. The country where they dwell is śā laḥ. Its components are the u dumbū rāḥ, ri la kha lāḥ, yu gnadha rāḥ, bhū liṅgāḥ and śa ra daṇḍa.

For the descendant or king of these, that is, of the royal clans of the countries that bear these names, and of pra ta gra tha and the rest, iñ comes. The descendant or king of the u dumbā ras is u dumbā riḥ; likewise tai la kha liḥ, mā dra kā riḥ, yau gandha rīḥ, prā tya gra thiḥ, kā la kū ṭaḥ.

From kambo ja and the rest, elision.

Here the suffix made for a descendant or a king is elided. The descendant or king of the kambo jas is kambo jaḥ; likewise ke ra laḥ, co laḥ, śa kaḥ, ya ba naḥ. From a stem of two vowels, aṇ is elided; from the others, it is añ.

In the feminine, from Kuru, kunta and a banti.

For Kuru and the rest, the descendant suffix made in the feminine is elided. The descendant of the Kurus, a woman, is Kuru: the ṇya made from Kuru by the rule "from Kuru and from stems beginning with n" is elided, and by "from ūt, uṅ" it takes ūṅ. Likewise kuntī: here ñyaṅ is elided. Why is it said "from it, denoting a human class"? Consider kau ra byaḥ, kaunt ya byaḥ, ā banta. It is said precisely "from the name of a country, for a royal clan"; otherwise kau ra byaḥ.

From āt, but not from brā cya and the bha rga group.

From a stem of the a type, leaving aside prā cya and the bharga group, what is made for a descendant in the feminine is elided: sū ra se nī, where añ has been elided; ma drīḥ, where aṇ has been elided. Why is it said "not from prā cya and the bharga group"? prā cya tā: prāñcā lī, bai de hī, mā ga dhī, āṅgī, bāṅgī, sohmī, pauṇḍī. And from the bharga group: bhārgī, kā ru ṣī. For ke ka yā t, elision is also accepted. ke kā yī takes di p, because it belongs to the gau ra group. It is said precisely "in the feminine"; otherwise mā drāḥ. The bharga group is: bharga, ka ru ṇa, ke ka ya, kaṣmī ra, śā lva, su stha la, u ra sa, kau ra bya.

From yañ and añ, in the plural, when not feminine.

What is made with yañ and añ is elided in the plural when not feminine: gārgāḥ, bi dhāḥ, pañcā lāḥ. Why is it said "in the plural"? gārgyāḥ, bai dyāḥ, stri yaḥ. It is said precisely "also for a descendant and a king, according to the derivation"; otherwise what comes from "outside" is bā hyāḥ, and what comes from utsa is autsāḥ. If one asks how pri ya gā rgyāḥ comes about, it is because it is formed from what is not plural. In bri ya garga, yañ applies only in the plural. Likewise the family of the son of Garga is gā rgya ku laṃ, and the family of the Gargas is garga ku laṃ.

kuṇḍi na.

From the word kuṇḍi ṇa, in the plural when not feminine, yañ is elided; and its ending in the letter a is also by a set form. It is said precisely "in the plural"; otherwise kauṇḍi nyaḥ. It is said precisely "when not feminine"; otherwise kauṇḍi nya stri yaḥ.

From ñyaṅ and the rest.

ñyaṅ and the rest are elided in the plural: āmbaṣṭhāḥ, aṅgāḥ, ku ra baḥ. It is said precisely "in the plural"; otherwise āmbaṣṭhyaḥ. It is said precisely "when not feminine"; otherwise stri yaḥ, where by the rule "from yaṅ" it takes ṭāp. It is said precisely "also for a descendant and a king"; otherwise adga sye me aṅgāḥ.

From yaska and the rest.

In the plural, aṇ and the other suffixes are elided: yaskā laskāḥ, dvu hyāḥ. It is said precisely "in the plural"; otherwise yāskaḥ. It is said precisely "when not feminine"; otherwise yaskyaḥ, women. From mi tra yu, an. khar ba na takes phañ by the ṇi group. bhaṇḍi ta, bha ḍi ta, bha ḍa ka: these take phañ by the a śva group.

a tri, bhṛ gu, kutsa, ba śiṣṭha, go ta ma and iṅgi ras.

Their descendant suffixes are elided in the plural: a tra yaḥ, where the ḍhak given by the rule "from it, not from iñ" is elided. For bhṛ gu and the rest, it is ṛṣyaṇ: bhṛ ga baḥ, kutsāḥ, baśiṣṭhāḥ, go ta māḥ, aṅgi ra saḥ. It is said precisely "in the plural"; otherwise ā tra yaḥ, bhārga baḥ. It is said precisely "when not feminine"; otherwise ā tra yyaḥ, women.

Of a gastya, also a gasti.

From the word a gastya, in the plural, the ṛṣi aṇ for a descendant is elided, and the stem is also changed to a gasti: a gasta yaḥ. It is said precisely "when not feminine"; otherwise ā gastyaḥ, women.

From a stem of many vowels, among the easterners, iñ.

In the plural the descendant suffix iñ is elided: pannā ga rāḥ, manta re ṣa ṇāḥ, yuddhaṣṭhi rāḥ, ūddhā la kāḥ, where the iñ obtained by being read in the ba hu group is elided. Why is it said "from a stem of many vowels"? baidga yaḥ. Why is it said "among the easterners"? hāsti dā sa yaḥ. It is said precisely "in the plural"; otherwise pannā gā riḥ. It is said precisely "when not feminine"; otherwise mantva re ṣa ṇyaḥ, women.

Optionally, from u pa ka and the rest.

In the plural, the descendant suffix is optionally elided: u pa kāḥ, o pa ka ya nāḥ; nā ma kāḥ, nā ma kā ya naḥ. The group is: u pa ka, na ma ka, ka piṣṭha la, ā hā ra ka, kha ṭvā ra ka, śi kha, bhraṣṭa ka, su paṣṭi, piṣṭa, u pa cā ra ka, kha di ra jigha, śa lā thala, pa tañja la, pa dañja la, kā ṭhe ra, ku śī ta ka, kā śi kratsna, sa dā, ma dha, ka la śī ka ṇa, dvas kaṇṭha, kṛṣṇā ji na, kṛṣṇā sunda ra, ba dhi ra ga, barṇa ka, bā ṭa li ka, ja tū karṇa, a nu lo ma, piñja la, ā pa dasma, pra ti lo ma, pra tā na, a na bhi hi ti. For the two, u pa ka and la ma ka, the phak given by the na ḍa group is elided. From su paṣṭi, baṣṭi, ma yū ra krṇa and ba dhi ka, aṇ by the śi ba group. From the word kāṣṭha ra, aṇ by the general rule. From the rest, iñ by the rule "from at". For a nu lo ma and pra ti lo ma, it is by the very mark of the bā tu group. It is said precisely "when not feminine"; otherwise kā biṣṭhi lyaḥ, women. It is said precisely "for the kā paṣṭhis"; otherwise au pa kā ya niḥ, kā paṣṭha liḥ.

From ti ka, ki ta ba and the rest, in the sense of "and".

From the ti ka group and the ki ta ba group, in the plural and in the feminine, in a copulative compound (dvandva), the suffix is elided: tai kā ya na and kai ta bā ya na ya are ti ka ka ti bāḥ, where the phiñ given by the ti ka group is elided. bātpha ra ya and bhāṇḍi ta thāḥ: by the rule "from at", iñ. Likewise ya pha ka na ra kāḥ, ba ka na kha sva gu dag pa ri ṇaddhāḥ, laṃ ka ṭa śānta mu khāḥ, kubja ka ku bhaḥ. From kubja, iñ by the rule "from at"; from ka ku bha, aṇ by the śi ba group.

u ra sa laṃ ka ṭāḥ: from u ra sa, phiñ by the tika group; from laṃ ka ṭa, iñ by the rule "from at". agni be śa da śe ra kāḥ: from agni be śa, yañ by the Garga group; from da śe ra ka, iñ by the rule "from at". u pa ka la ma kāḥ: phak by the na ḍa group. bhaṣṭa ka ka paṣṭhi līḥ, kṛṣṇa sunad rāḥ: iñ by the rule "from at". The three, u pa ka la ma ka and the others, though already established by the preceding rule, are read here for the purpose of restriction.

Not from the eight beginning with go pa ba na.

Within the collection of the bi da group, the eight beginning with go pa ba na are not elided: go ba ba nā, śrai gra bāḥ, bainda bāḥ. Why is it said "the eight"? ha ri tāḥ. The eight are: go pa ba naḥ, śi gru, bi nu, bhā ja na, a śvā ba ta naḥ, śyā mā kā, śa myā ka, śyā yantu. This is said with regard to the añ obtained from the bid group, under the rule "from yañ and añ, in the plural and when not feminine".

Before ji ta, of a vowel.

What is made for a descendant in the plural, the feminine excepted, is not elided before a suffix that begins with a vowel and comes earlier than ja ti, that is, in the operations up to that point. The students of the Pañcālas are prañcā lī yāḥ; the students of the Atris are ā tre yī yā; likewise ā gastvi yāḥ, kauṇḍi nī yāḥ. Why is it said "before ji ta"? What benefits the Gargas is garga ī yāḥ. Why is it said "of a vowel"? What comes from the Gargas is garga rū pyaḥ. It is said precisely "in the plural"; otherwise the descendant of kambo ja is kamvo jaḥ, and his students are kambo jāḥ; likewise cau rā kai ra lāḥ.

For a yu ba na, elision.

What is made for a yu ban, a junior descendant, is elided before a suffix that comes earlier than jit and begins with a vowel. The junior descendant of the descendant of phāṇḍī hi ta is phāṇḍā hi taḥ, phāṇḍā hi tā ya niḥ, and his students are phāṇḍā hi tāḥ. Where the phiñ of ṇa is elided, aṇ comes by the rule "from iñ". It is said precisely "before ji ta"; otherwise what benefits bhā ga bhitti ka is bhā ga bhitti kā ya. It is said precisely "of a vowel"; otherwise phāṇḍa hi ta rū bya.

Optionally, from pha ka, phik and phiñ.

What is made for a yu ban is optionally elided in an operation that begins with a vowel and comes earlier than ji ta. The junior descendant of the descendant of gargya is gārgā ya ṇaḥ; the junior descendant of the descendant of dā kṣa is dākṣā ya ṇaḥ. Their students are gārgī yaḥ, gārgī ṇi yāḥ, dākṣā ya ṇi yāḥ. The descendant of yaska is yāskaḥ; the junior descendant of the descendant of yaska is yāskā ya niḥ, with phañ by the rule "from aṇ, from a stem of two vowels". His students are yā skī yāḥ, yā skā yā nī yāḥ. Why the letter k? So that phañ does not come to be excluded. The students of the son of Atri are a tre yī yāḥ: after the bañ of the a śva group it is always elided.

From what is not brahmin.

"Before a vowel earlier than jit" is here discontinued. From a gotra that is not brahmin, what is made for a yu ban is elided: au dumba riḥ, the father; au dumba riḥ, the son. Because it is a component of śā lu, it takes iñ, and after that phak is elided. kārṇa kha riḥ, father or son; bhāṇḍi jaṃ ghiḥ, father or son: for that same reason iñ, and after it phak is elided. sauhmaḥ, father or son; ādga, father or son: since these have two vowels and end in aṇ, the phiñ given by the rule "from aṇ, from a stem of two vowels" is elided.

From pai la and the rest.

What is made for a yu ba na is elided. The descendant of pā lī is bai la saḥ, father or son: the phiñ given by the rule "from aṇ, from a stem of two vowels" is elided. The descendant of śe laṃ ka is śā laṃ kiḥ, father or son: phak is elided. Those ending in iñ refer to a sense other than eastern. The group is: pai laḥ, śā laṃ ki, sātya ki, sā tyaṃ kā mi, au dañci, au da majji, au da pra ji, au da me ghi, au da śuddhiḥ, dai bistha li, bai ga lā dā ya niḥ, rā śi, rā hakṣa ti, bhau liṅgi, au laṅgī, ha mā ni, au jji hā ṇiḥ.

From the eastern, iñ, but not from to lu ni and the rest, nor from bha ra ta.

From an eastern gotra ending in iñ, excepting tau lu ni and the rest and bha ra, what is made for a yu ban is elided: prānnā gā riḥ, father or son; māntva re ṣva ṇiḥ, father or son. Why is it said "from the eastern"? dāmbiḥ, the father; dākṣā ya ṇaḥ, the son. Why is it said "from iñ"? rā gha biḥ, the father; rā gha biḥ, the son. Why is it said "not from tau lva ni and bha ra ta"? to lva niḥ, the father; tau lva ṇyā ya naḥ, the son. The group is: tau lva ni, bā ra ṇi, rā ba ṇi, dai lī pi, dai ba ti, dai ba yajñi, dai ba ma ti, prā dā ha ni, nā ya da ki, a su ri, a hi ka, anta ni, nai mi ṣi, ā li bandha ki, baidki, pauṣki, pauṣka ra sā di, baitdki, kā re ṇu pā li, be karṇi. And from the bha ra ta gotra as well: ārju ṇiḥ, the father; ārju nā ya naḥ, the son; yau dhiṣṭhi riḥ, the father; yau dhiṣṭhi ri yaṇ, the son.

From ṇi, from ārṣa and from ñit: of añ and iñ.

From stems ending in ṇi and in ārṣa, and from ñit as well, the añ and iñ made for a yu ban are elided: kau ra byaḥ, father or son. That this is stated with reference to a brāhmaṇa, through the elision of the iñ given by the rule "from at", is because kau ra bya is read in the tika group as denoting a royal clan. Why is it said "from kau ra and iñ"? The descendant of Dakṣa is dākṣā ya ṇaḥ. It is said precisely "for a yu ban"; otherwise the descendant of the Island-born is drai phyiḥ.

For a single gotra.

"Descendant" is what is called the gotra of the grandson and so on; here the gotra as fully defined is to be understood. When that is to be expressed, since it would follow that a suffix arises for the descendant of each separate generation, a restriction is made: the suffix comes only once, for a single gotra of descent. By this earlier phrase, even though the descent is not immediate, it is the descendant of both the primary and the ordinary ancestors. The descendant of Upagu is au pa ga baḥ, and for his descendant too it is o pa ga baḥ. The descendant of Devadatta is dai ba dattiḥ, for his son too it is de ba dattiḥ, and likewise for that one as well.

From a gotra, for a junior descendant.

When the descent of a junior descendant is to be expressed, the suffix comes only after a stem that ends in a gotra suffix: the junior descendant immediately following the primary line, and not for the ordinary ones. gārgyā ya naḥ, bātsyā ya naḥ, o pa ga biḥ, nā ḍā ya niḥ.

A descendant from the grandson onward is gotra.

Whatever descendant there is from the grandson onward takes the name gotra: the descendant of Garga, counting from the grandson, is gārgyaḥ. Why is it said "from the grandson"? The immediate descendant is gārgaḥ. The term gotra is employed in such rules as "from a gotra, in the feminine". The junior descendant of the descendant of gārgya is:

From ca turtha and the rest, excluding a woman, while living: gotra and youth.

By ca turba and the rest: what is connected with the clan, the descendant and the woman excepted, is gotra. That from which it arises is the line, that is, the father, the grandfather and so on. When one of that line is living, the descendant from the grandson onward bears the name "youth", and does not bear the name gotra: gārgaḥ, gārgo, garg yaḥ; or, when ji ba ta, that is, living, the junior descendant of the descendant of gargya is gārgyā ya ṇaḥ, and the junior descendant of the descendant of the line of Dakṣa is dākṣā ya ṇaḥ, with phak by the rule "from yañ and iñ". Why is the woman excluded? The female descendant of gargya is gārgā, bītsā: what is expressed is simply yañ. The word "ni" has the sense of restriction.

bhā ta ṛkṣa ya sī.

When an elder brother is ji bat, living, the fourth and the rest come to bear the name "youth"; this concerns what is not the line. When the elder of gargya is jī bat, ka nī yān bhrā tā is gārgyā ya ṇaḥ. The line is that of those who begot before the father, and so on; this is stated because a brother, not being such, is not a cause of birth. It is said precisely "not a woman"; otherwise gārgī sva sāḥ.

When another who is a sapiṇḍa and more senior is ji bat, also.

Kin within the limit of seven generations are called sapiṇḍa. In statements such as "in the case of both of whom, dā śā hā ni ku śa syānnaṃ na bhu jya te", it is the general usage. When someone other than a brother, who is a sapiṇḍa and more senior, is jī bat, then, the woman excepted, the living descendant of the fourth and so on optionally comes to bear the name "youth". What is specified by ta ras is that the sapiṇḍa should be superior in both respects, in position and also in age. pi ti byed, bi tā ma he, bhra ta ri, bā dha ya: to establish these, it says "when jī bat". The living descendant of gārgya is gārgyā ya ṇaḥ or gārgyaḥ; dākṣā ya ṇaḥ or dākṣīḥ. Why does the word ji bat qualify both? When he is mṛ te or mṛ ta, it is gārgyaḥ. Why the specification by ta ras? When the other is inferior in position and age, it is gārgyaḥ. It is said precisely "not a woman"; otherwise gārgī, dākṣi.

Gotra, in honour.

When honour is to be conveyed, the gotra descendant optionally takes the name "youth": ta tra bha bān gārgyā ya ṇaḥ, or gārgyāḥ; ta tra bha bān bātsa ya na, or bātsyāḥ.

From yu ban, in contempt.

When contempt is to be conveyed, the name "youth" is optional for a yu ban. This is an option on what would otherwise apply necessarily as the principal designation. In the alternative where the name "youth" is denied, the name gotra itself applies, because there is no separate alternative: gārgyo jā la naḥ, or gā rgyā ya ṇaḥ. Why is it said "in contempt"? gārgyā ya ṇaḥ. Not being gu ru a yatta is the utmost contempt.

Thus ends the second quarter of the fourth chapter of the Commentary in Eight Thousand on Sādhukīrti's Mañjuśrī Grammar, composed by the master of the land of the bodhisattva, Rājadeva, lord of glorious Kaliṅga.

In the remaining senses.

Whatever is other than what was explained earlier, ending with the descendant, rakta and the rest, is the "remaining" sense, that is, together with the remainder. In it aṇ comes. Visible form to be grasped by the eye is cākṣu ṣaṃ; sound is śrā ba ṇaḥ; taste is rā sa naḥ; the tangible is sparṣa naḥ. What is to be seen on the fourteenth is rakṣa śca turad śaṃ. Flours ground on a stone are śakta bo dārṣa dāḥ; u dū kha li kṣuṇḍā ya, or au dū kha lāṃḥ. A chariot drawn by horses is ā śvaḥ; one drawn by four is cā turṣa ka ṭaṃ. The general heading "in the remaining senses" extends to the end of the quarter. For that very reason, through this inference of a distinction of another sense, there are also distinctions of the base; all those that establish distinctions of the suffix are likewise "remaining" senses. Where a distinction of sense is understood, it is not the remaining sense. Nevertheless, this is because the remaining sense is a matter whose particular meaning is determined in each case.

From rāṣṭa, gha.

It comes in the remaining sense: rāṣṭī yaḥ. It is an exception to aṇ, stated where aṇ would otherwise be obtained.

From pā ra, a bā ra, a bā ra and a bā ra pā ra, kha.

It comes in the remaining sense: pā rī ṇaḥ, a pā rī ṇaḥ, pā rā bā rī ṇaḥ, a bā ra bā rī ṇaḥ. Because they are produced by taking the compound apart as well, there are four forms. It is stated where cha would be obtained for the first and the third, and where aṇ would be obtained for the rest.

From grā ma, yat and khañ.

From the word grā ma, in the remaining sense, come yat and khañ, as an exception to cha: grā myaḥ, grā mi ṇaḥ. Marking it with ñ serves to prohibit the treatment as masculine in "gra mī ṇā bhyārya".

From kartri and the rest also, ḍhañ.

From kartri and the rest, and also from gra ma, ḍhak comes: kurbi rā sta yaḥ, kartra yaḥ, meaning arisen from these or come from these; kārtre ya ka, gra me ya kaṃ, aumbhe ya kaṃ. The group is: kartri, umbhi, puṣka ra, puṣka la, kuṇḍi nā, na ga ra, mā hiṣma trī, barma tī, kumbhi, kuḍyā, with elision of the ya of kuḍyā as well: kau ḍe ya kaḥ.

From ku la, kukṣi and grī ba, for a dog, a sword and an ornament.

ḍha kañ comes in that order. What is born of a family is kau le ya kaḥ, a dog; otherwise kau laḥ. kau kṣe ya kaḥ is a sword; otherwise kaukṣaḥ. grai ba ya kaṃ is an ornament; otherwise gre baḥ.

From na dī and the rest, ḍhak.

It comes: nā de yaḥ, mā he yaḥ. The group is: nā dīḥ, ma hīḥ, bā rā ṇa sīḥ, śrā bastī, kau śambī, ba na kau śambī, kā śa pā ri, pūrba na ga rīḥ, yā bā, bā mā, ma dorbātsa ru kī, khā di rī. Some read the word pūrba na ga rī as pūrba na ga ri. For these: bau re yaṃ, bā ṇe ya, gai re yaṃ. It is stated where cha would be obtained by the rule "from bṛddha", where buñ would be obtained for bā rā ṇa si and the rest as an exception to cha, and where aṇ would be obtained for the rest.

From dākṣi ṇa, baṣcā ta and pu ras, tyak.

From these, in the remaining sense, tyak comes: dākṣi ṇā tyaḥ, bā ścā tyaḥ, pau rastyaḥ. Because it is used together with the word paścāt, the word dākṣi ṇa is also taken as uninflected.

From bahlā, urdi, pardi and kā pi śā, ṣphak.

From these, in the remaining sense, ṣphak comes: bāhlā ya naḥ, auddā ya naḥ, pa dā ya naḥ; kā bi śā ya naṃ, honey; kā bi śā ya nā, grapes. The letter ṣ serves the purpose of dīp.

From raṅ ku, optionally, for a living creature.

ṣphak comes: rāṅka baḥ, an ox. Why is it said "for a living creature"? rāṅka baḥ, a woollen blanket. For a human, buñ comes, because it is read in the kaccha group: rāṅ ka ba kaḥ, a man.

From prā ka, a bā ka, u dag, pra tyak and dīp, yat.

From brāk and the rest, in the remaining sense, yat comes: prā cyaḥ, a bā cyaḥ, u dī cyaḥ, pra tī cyaḥ, di pyaḥ. prā ka and the rest, which end in krin, take number, and have the senses of direction, place and time. Those that end in the elision after asta take no number; they are taken by adopting a distinction. Among these, yat is formed from those used of direction and place; from those expressing time, because it is desired, it is na ṭa: prākta naṃ.

From kanthā, ṭhak.

It comes in the remaining sense: kaṇṭhī kaḥ.

For ballu, buk.

The region not far from the river called ballu is ballu. From the word kantha of that country, buk comes: kāntha kaḥ.

From kva, a māḥ, i ha, ta tra and tas, tyab.

It comes in the remaining sense: kva tyaḥ, a mā tyaḥ, i ha tyaḥ, ta tra tyaḥ, ta ta styaḥ.

Optionally, from ai ṣa mas, hyas and śvas.

From these tyab comes optionally: ai ṣa mastaṃ, ai ṣa masta naṃ; hyastaṃ, hasta naṃ; śāstaṃ, śvasta naṃ.

From ni and ni ṣa, in the senses of permanent and abiding.

It comes. What abides definitely is ni tyaṃ, the permanent. One who abides in a class that has gone outside is niṣṭya, an outcaste woman (caṇḍālī) and the like.

From a ra ṇya, ṇa.

It comes: ā ra ṇyaḥ, flowers. The statement of ṇa serves the purpose of ṭāp.

From dū ra and uttar, etya and ā hañ.

They come in the remaining sense, in that order: dū re tyaḥ, a traveller; auttā rā haḥ.

From a compound whose final word is rū pya, ña.

From a compound whose final word is rū pya, in the remaining sense, ña comes: kā ka rū pyaṃ, bārka rū pyaṃ, śai ba rū pyaṃ. Since it is stated "from what ends in rū pya", it does not apply where there are many prior members. Also from what ends in the suffix rū pya; therefore for that it is simply aṇ: bā hu rū pyaṃ. buñ is also to be seen, as in mā ṇī ka rū pya ka.

Also from a compound with a direction word as prior member, and from ma dre ta ra, when it is not a name.

From a compound with a direction word as prior member, other than madra, when it is not a name, ña comes: baurba śā laḥ, paurba śā lāḥ. Why is it said "from ma dre ta ra"? paurba ma draḥ, paurba ma drī. Why is it said "when it is not a name"? pūrbai ṣu kā ma sa mī; in "prā cau na ga rā ṇāṃ" the final word is strengthened. Because the word "direction" is without the word pūrba, the sense "direction" is what is taken. Hence no unwanted consequence arises for digdā haṃ, dagga ja and the like.

From śak la and the rest, from a gotra.

From śa ka la and the rest, ending in gotra suffixes, aṇ comes, as an exception to cha. The parasol of the Śākalyas is śā ka lāḥ; likewise kāṇṭhaḥ, gau kakṣaḥ. Why is it said "from a gotra"? What comes from the throat is kāṇṭhāḥ, and its parasol is kāṇṭhī yaḥ.

From iñ.

From a stem ending in iñ, in the gotra sense, aṇ comes, as an exception to cha. The students of Dakṣa are dā kṣāḥ. It is said precisely "from a gotra"; otherwise, with reference to su taṃ ga me, where it does not apply, sau taṃ ga miḥ. From pra ka di, ku mu da, ba rā ha, su taṃ ga ma and the rest, iñ; ṭhakka; by the rule "from iñ", iñ. The actual form in that case is sau taṃ ga mī yaḥ.

Not from one of two vowels, nor from the eastern.

From a stem of two vowels ending in iñ, and from an eastern one, aṇ does not come in the gotra sense: baidkī yāḥ, pauṣpī yaḥ, kā śī yāḥ, bā śī yaḥ. Why is it said "of two vowels"? pānnā gā rāḥ, māntva re ṣa ṇāḥ. Why is it said "from the eastern"? dākṣāḥ, plākṣāḥ.

From bṛddha, cha.

From a base bearing the name bṛddha, cha comes, in the remaining sense. What comes from the hall is śā lī yaḥ; likewise mā lī yaḥ, ai ti ka ya nī yaḥ, au pa ga bī yaḥ, ā cārya yaḥ, tya dī yaḥ, ai ta dā yaḥ, au pa ga bā yaḥ, i dā mī yaḥ, a da pī yaḥ. Why is it said "from bṛddha"? What comes from "Desire and Bringing Near" is brā bhā panna ya naḥ. From bha bat, ṭhak and cha come: bha ba dī yaṃ. Because "from bṛddha" continues, it does not come from what ends in śa tri: bhā ba taṃ.

From u pa, for a country, ṭhañ.

From a word ending in u that denotes a country, in the remaining sense, ṭhañ comes: śā pa ra jambu kaḥ, nai ṣā da kurṣu kaḥ. Why is it said "for a country"? The students of the skilled one are pā ṭa bāḥ.

From bṛddha, from brā cya.

From a bṛddha ending in u that denotes an eastern country, ṭhañ comes: ā dha ka jambu kaḥ. Stating what is already established by the preceding rule is for the sake of restriction: only from a bṛddha that is eastern, and not from a bṛddha as such. Hence mālla basta paḥ.

From kā śi and the rest, also i ka ña.

It comes; and by the syllable "also", ṭhan as well: kā śi kā, kā śi kī, bai di kā, bai di kī. It is said precisely "from de śa"; otherwise the students of Kāśi are kā śī yaḥ. It is said precisely "from the eastern"; otherwise what comes from the village of the bā hi kas named Devadatta is dai ba dattaḥ. The group is: kā śi, be di, sambā ti, sambā ha, acyuta, mo da mān śu gu lā da, hasti, karṣū, de ba karṣū, ku nā ma na, hi ra ṇya ka ra ṇa, go bā sa na sa ra hi na, bha ridga, a rinad ma, sarba mi tra, de ba datta, dā sa mi tra, dā sa grā ma, sau dhā ba tā na, yu barā ja, sindhu mi tra, de ba rā ja, akṣa mi tra, śa ṭha mi tra, sā nu mi tra.

From kā la, with pūrba and the like as prior member.

ikañ and ṭhañ come: paurba kā li kā, paurba kā li kī; a pat kā li kā, āpat kā li kī; aurdhva kā li kā, aurdhva kā li kī.

From bā hi ka gra ma.

Whatever lies outside the region where the dharma, the proper conduct of the country's lineage, is kept pure is na bhi hī da ka. A village there is bī hā ka grā maḥ. From a bṛddha denoting that, ikañ and ṭhañ come: śā ka li kā, śā ka li kī, māntha bi kab, māntha ba kī. It is said precisely "from bṛddha"; otherwise mā ha na ga raḥ.

Optionally, for the u śī na ras.

When whatever falls in the category of the u śī na ras is to be expressed, ika ña and ṭhañ come optionally from a bṛddha. What comes from Aṅkajāla is āṅka jā li kā, āṅka jā li kī, āṅka jā li yā; likewise sau darṣa ni kā, sau darṣa nī yā. This is an option where it was not otherwise obtained. The word grā ma, although not compounded with bā hi ka in the preceding sūtra, continues into this one, because this depends on the speaker's intention.

From what ends in prastha, bah, pu ra, from what has ya as penultimate, and in the sense of desert, buñ.

From prastha and the rest, from what has ya as penultimate, and in expressing a desert, buñ comes for a bṛddha: mā lā prastha kaḥ, bai lo ba ha kaḥ, nandī pu ra kaḥ. Although this is established by the rule "from what ends in ma", pu rāntā is taken for the sake of what is not eastern. From what has ya as penultimate: sāṃ kā ṣya kaḥ, kāmbī lya kaḥ. In expressing a desert:

pā ra dhanva kaḥ, ai rā ba ta kaḥ. Whatever there is here, such as bā hī ka grā mā, bā tan prastha and the like, and whatever ends in the letter u and denotes a country: for all of these, because it is desired, buñ alone comes. Hence bā da na prastha kaḥ, kaukku ṭī ba ha taḥ, dā sa ra bya kaḥ. What comes from the country called Fierce Life is ā ti bra mā ya ba kaḥ.

From what has ra as penultimate, and from āt, from the eastern.

From what has ra as penultimate, and also from what ends in ī, for an eastern country, buñ comes from a bṛddha: prā ṭa li phu ṭa ka; and what comes from kā kandī is kā ka daṃ ka. Why is it said "eastern"? dāttā mindri yaḥ. It is said precisely "from bṛddha"; otherwise dā śa pu raḥ. Adding t after it is for convenience: since there is no eastern country ending in the letter i, it is simply what ends in the letter i that is mentioned.

From countries.

From a bṛddha denoting a country, buñ comes: ā darṣa kaḥ, ā bi sā re kaḥ. Because bṛddha continues, the suspicion that the bare base is to be taken is removed. The purpose is that buñ applies even in the domain of the exception that expresses plurality: ā bi sā ra ga ta ka.

From countries that are plural only.

Countries that are plural only are those that are not used in the singular and so on. From those plural-only countries buñ comes, as an exception to aṇ. What arises among the aṅgas is aṅga kaḥ; likewise bāṅga kaḥ, sau hma kaḥ, pauṇḍa kaḥ, tre karta kaḥ, jāmba ba kaḥ, kā lañja ra kaḥ, bai kā śa kaḥ. Why is "country" mentioned? What arises among the barta nis is bārta naḥ. The word barta ni is used in the plural because of its distinct parts, not because of distinct individuals.

su, sarba, ardha and di ka.

From those that a word for a country follows, buñ comes: su pā ca la kaḥ, su mā ga dha kaḥ; sarba pañca la kaḥ, sarba mā ga dha kaḥ; arha pāñcā la kaḥ, arha mā gaṅha kaḥ. By the rule "from su, sarba and arha, of a country", the final word is strengthened. From the word di ka: pūrba pañca la kaḥ, u tta ra pañcā la ka; by the rule "from a direction, of what is not madra", the final word is strengthened.

From compounds whose final word is kaccha, agni, baktra or bartta, and from dhū la and the rest.

From compounds ending in kaccha and the rest that denote a country, and also from dhū ma and the rest, buñ comes: bhā ru kaccha kaḥ, pai pya li kaccha kaḥ, kāṇḍā gan kaḥ, sai bhū jāgna kaḥ, baindhū baktra kaḥ, cakra barta kaḥ. From dhū ma and the rest: dhau ma kaḥ, ṣā ḍadga kaḥ. The group is: dhū ma, ṣa ḍaṃ ga, śa śā da na, arju bā ba, daṇḍa ya na stha lī, gho ṣastha lī, mā ha ka stha lī, rā ja gri ha, sattra sa ha, bhakṣyā lī, ma dra ku la, dryā hā ba, tvyā hā ba, saṃ sbī ya, barba ra, gartta, bi da ha, a ṇartta. Although buñ is established for these by the rule "from countries that are plural only", they are stated for the sake of the sense other than a country. The wealth of the royal clan of Videha is bai de ha kaḥ; likewise ā narta kaḥ. As for the word pāṭheya, although it is established by "what has ya as penultimate", it is listed so that pā ṭha is meant as other than a country: pā ṭhe ya kaḥ. Further: mā tha ra gho ṣa, śa sya, ba lī, ā rā jñī, dhārtta rājñī, a ba ya ba; and, for the sau bī ras, from ku la: what arises in the country bearing a gotra name within the respective country of the bi ras is kau la kaḥ; that is how it is. Further: kukṣi, dī ba, anta rī pa, a ru na, ujja ya nī, dākṣi ṇā pa ṭha, śā ke ta.

From sa mu dra, for a boat and a man.

buñ comes. A boat that is sent out on the ocean is sā mu dri kā; a man who is on the ocean is sa mu dra kaḥ. Otherwise it is simply aṇ: sa mu draḥ, water.

From na ga ra, buñ comes also in the senses of contempt and the lute: nā ga ra kaḥ, lute players. Why is it said "in the senses of contempt and the lute"? nā ga rāḥ, brahmins.

From a ra ṇya, for ba thi, nyā ya, adhyā ya, hasti, na ra and bi hā ra.

For these, buñ comes from the word a ra ṇya: ā ra ṇya kaḥ, for banthāḥ, nyā yaḥ, a dhyā yaḥ, ha stā, ma nu ṣya; and also from bi hā ra. Why is it said "for these"? ā ra ṇyāḥ, animals. How then ā ra ṇya hasti nī? Because there is no such usage, buñ does not come. In expressing cow dung, buñ comes optionally from a ra ṇya: ā ra ṇya kaḥ, cow dung; or a ra ṇāḥ.

From Kuru and yu gandha ra.

buñ comes optionally: kau ra ba kaḥ, kau ra baḥ; yau gandha ra kaḥ, yau gandha raḥ. Because the word Kuru is read in the kaccha group, buñ is always heard for a man and his abode; but on the basis of the commentarial explanation, añ is also accepted. Hence "saṃ rakṣa tāṃ kau pa ra bāḥ" is established.

From bṛ ji and ma dra, kan.

It comes. What arises among the bṛ jis is bṛ ji ka; likewise ma dra kaḥ.

From what has k as penultimate, aṇ.

From what has k as penultimate, aṇ comes, as an exception to buñ from a country. What arises among the Ṛṣikas is ārṣi kaḥ. Mentioning aṇ, although aṇ continues, serves to set aside the ṭhañ given by "from u, for a country". Hence what comes from Ikṣvāku is aikṣvā kaḥ.

From kaccha and the rest.

In the remaining sense aṇ comes. The group is: kāccha, sindhu, barṇa, aṅgār, ma dhū ma te, kambo ja, kaśmī ra, sva lā, Kuru, gandhā ra, ma nu ṣaṇḍa ra, ma nu ṣaṇḍa, dvī pa, a nṛ pa, a ja bā ha, bi rū pa rka, kulla, raṅku. From kaccha, since it is not a plural-only country, aṇ is established by "in the remaining sense" itself; it is read in the group for the sake of buñ for a man and his abode. Likewise for bi rū pa ka, aṇ is established by "from what has k as penultimate"; from the word ballu, ṭhañ would be obtained by "from u, for a country"; from Kuru, buñ by "from Kuru and yu ba gandha ra"; and for the rest, buñ would be obtained by "from countries".

For a man and his abode, buñ.

From kaccha and the rest, buñ also comes for a man and his abode: kāccha ka, a man; kāccha kaṃ, this one's laughter; saindha pa kaḥ, a man; saindha bi kā, this one's small thing.

From sā lva, when it is not a foot soldier.

buñ comes for a man and his abode: sā lva kaḥ, a man; sva lva kaṃ, this one's laughter. Why is it said "not a foot soldier"? sā lvaḥ, a foot soldier.

bram ze ti: from goṅ and ya ba gū.

From sā lva, buñ comes: sā lo kaḥ, an ox; svā lvi kā, gruel. Otherwise sva lvaṃ.

From ka ṭa and the rest, and from the eastern, cha.

It comes, as an exception to a ṇa: ka ṭa na ga rī yaḥ, ka ṭa gho ṣī yaḥ. Why is it said "from the eastern"? kā ṭa na ga raḥ.

From kṛ ka ṇa and parṇa, of bhā ra dhvā ja.

From kṛ ka na and parṇa, denoting the countries of bhā ra dhvā ja, cha comes: kṛ ka ṇī yaṃ, barṇī yaṃ. Why is it said "of bhā ra dhvā ja"? kārka ṇaḥ. Country in general is excluded by the word bhā ra dhvā ja; and bhā ra dhvā ja too is to be taken as a country, not as a gotra.

From a bṛddha ending in kanthā, pa la da, na ga ra, grā ma, ha ma or dānda ka, and from one that has kha as penultimate.

cha comes: dākṣa kanthī yaḥ, dākṣi ba la dī yaḥ, dākṣin ga ri yaḥ, mā hi ka grā mī yaḥ, dākṣi hri dā yaḥ. cha is formed where ṭhañ and ka would be obtained by the rule "from bā hī ka gra ma". a ri ha ṇa kī yaḥ, ā khya ṇa kī yaḥ, ai ṇa kī yaḥ, a śva kvī yaḥ, śau ṣa kā yaḥ: this is where aṇ would be obtained by "from what has k as penultimate". ā ya mu khī yaḥ, kau ṭa śi khā yaḥ: this is where buñ would be obtained by "from what has ra as penultimate and from it, from the eastern".

From what ends in garta, and from ga ha and the rest.

From a compound with garta as its final word that denotes a country, and also from ga ha and the rest when they denote a country, cha comes: bṛ ka gartī yaḥ, śvā pa da gartī yaḥ, ga hī yaḥ, antasthī yaḥ. The group is: ga ha, anta sa ma, bi ṣa ma, utta ma, aṅga ma ga dha, pūrba pakṣa, a dha ma śā kha, utta ma śā kha, sa māṃ na śā kha, e ka grā ma, e ka pa lā śa, e ka bṛkṣaḥ, ai gra gra ha, i ṣu gra, iṣṭha nī ka, dantā gra, a ba śyanda na, kā ma prastha, khā ḍā yi na, kā ṭhe ra ṇi, lā the ra ṇi, śai ba gi ri, śaudgi, ū su ri, ā hiṃ si, a mi tri, byā ḍi, bhau je, adhya śvi, āgni śarmi, dai ba śarmi, sau tri, bā rā ṭa ki, bālmī ki, kṣau ma dha nvi, au pa bandi bi, utta ra, anta ra. This is an open class (ākṛtigaṇa). Hence aṅgā dhi ka rī yaḥ, būrba trā siddhī yaḥ, and the like.

From sarbat.

cha comes: parba tī yaḥ, a man.

Optionally, for what is not a man.

From parba ta cha comes: sarba tī yaṃ, fruit, or pārba taṃ; parba tī yaḥ, an elephant, or pārba taḥ.

From tas after mu kha, e ka and parṣa.

To whatever follows mu kha and the rest, cha comes. What comes from the mouth is mu kha tī yaḥ; likewise parṣā tī yaḥ. When cha is added at once to what is not a man, the ṭi is elided by the rule "of an indeclinable".

From sva, pa ra, ja na, de ba and rā ja na, kuṭ also.

cha comes, and to it the augment kuṭ: sva kī yaḥ, pa ra kī yaḥ, jan kī yaḥ, de ba kī yaḥ. The form "svī yaṃ" is Pāṇini's.

From pṛ thi bā ma dhya, ma also.

From pṛ thi pā ma dha, cha comes, and it is also changed to ma ṅha ma. What is born within the earth is ma dhya mī yaḥ.

For a resident and for one who roams, aṇ also.

From pṛ thi bī ma dhya, when a resident or one who roams is expressed, aṇ comes, and it is also changed to ma dhya ma. Those who reside within the earth are mā dhya māḥ. Why is it said "a resident"? ma dhyā mī yaḥ, the common caste.

From be ṇu ka and the rest, chaṇ.

It comes in the remaining sense: bai ṇu kī yāḥ, bai tra kī yaḥ, brasthī yaḥ, autta ra pa dī yaḥ, mā dhya ma kī yaḥ.

From yuṣma and asmad, optionally kha ña, and also yuṣmā ka and a smā ka.

From yuṣmad and asmad, khañ comes optionally, and when it is attached they are also changed to yuṣmā ka and aṣmā ka: yauṣmā kīṇ, āsma kī ṇaḥ; yuṣma dī yaḥ, asmā dī yaḥ.

aṇ.

For yuṣmad and asmad, aṇ comes optionally, and when it is attached they become yuṣmā ka and asmā ka: yoṣmā kaḥ, asmā kaḥ.

ta pa ka and ma ma ka, for one.

When yuṣmad and asmad apply to a single referent, before khañ and aṇ they are changed to ta ba and ma ma ka: tā ba kī naḥ, mā ma kī naḥ, tā ba ka, mā ma kī naḥ, tā ba kaḥ, mā ma kaḥ. With cha: tra dī yaḥ, ma dī yaḥ; these are the changes to tra and ma by the rule "also before a suffix and a final word". Because of the general heading "remaining", there is no change before the descendant aṇ: yuṣmā ka ma pa tyaṃ yauṣma daḥ; ta pā pa tya trā daḥ.

From dvī pa, when near the ocean, yañ.

From a dvī pa that is near the ocean, yañ comes, as an exception to the aṇ due to its being read in the kaccha group and to the buñ for a man: dvai pyaḥ. Why is it said "near the ocean"? dvai paḥ, dve pa kaḥ.

From ardha, yat.

It comes: ardhyaṃḥ.

pa ra, a ba ra, adha ma and utta ma as prior members.

From a saddha, yat comes: pā rīrdhyaṃ, a ba rā rdhyaṃ, utta mārdhyaṃ.

From a direction word, ṭhañ also.

From ardhya following a word expressing direction, ṭhañ comes, and by the syllable "also", the yat of ardha as well: paurbārdhi kaṃ, pūrbā rdhyaṃ; dākṣi ṇārdhi kaṃ, dakṣa ṇā rdhyaṃ. Although pa ra and a ba ra express direction, this is because, from ardha preceded by them, yat alone is desired.

From a part of a village or of a country, añ also.

From ardha meaning a part of a village, and also a part of a country, following a direction word, ṭhañ comes, and añ also. What comes from the eastern half of a village or a country is paurbārdhi kaḥ, paurbārdhi kaḥ; dākṣi ṇā rdhi kāḥ, dakṣi ṇārdhaḥ. It is said precisely "from direction words"; otherwise ardhyāḥ, this being certainly of a village.

From what has a prior member.

From ardha preceded by another word, ṭhañ comes: bā le yārdhi kaṃ, kau ta mārdhi gaṃ.

From what expresses time.

ṭhañ comes: mā si kaṃ, sāmbatsa ri kaṃ, dai ba si kaṃ, mau hūrtti kaṃ. Because "expresses" is mentioned, ṭhañ comes from whatever is applied to time in any way: kā damba puṣpi kaṃ, bai he pa lā li kaṃ. The time at which the kadamba comes into flower, fruit and shoot is figuratively designated as not different from these, and is called by their names.

From śa rad, for a rite of faith.

From śa rad, ṭhañ comes only for a rite of faith: śā ra di kaṃ, of faith. Otherwise śa ra daṃ. Why is "rite" mentioned? śā radaḥ, a person of faith.

Optionally, for illness and fever.

For these, ṭhañ comes optionally from śa ra da: śā ra di kaḥ, śa ra daṃ, illness and fever. Why is it said "for illness and fever"? śa ra daṃ, water.

From śa śva ta, naṣā and pra doṣa, ṭhañ comes optionally: śā śva ti kaḥ, śā śva taṃ; nai ṣi kaṃ, nai ṣaṃ; prā da ṣi kaṃ, prā do ṣaṃ.

From śva pa, tuṭ also.

Here ṭhañ comes optionally, with the augment tuṭ as well: śau basti kaṃ, śvastyaṃ, śvasta naṃ.

From ci ra, pa tut and pa rā ri, na.

It comes optionally, with the augment tuṭ as well: ci ratnaḥ, cirant na; ba rutnaḥ, ba ruttaḥ; pā rā ratniḥ, pa rā ritna.

For pūrbā nna and a pa rātna, tanṭ.

It comes optionally: pūrbātna tanaḥ, baurbā tni kaṃ; a pa rāte ta naḥ, a pa rātni kaṃ. By the rule "from the sense of time, before ta ra, tan and ta ma, optionally, for time", the seventh case ending is not elided. The letter ṭ serves the purpose of dīp.

From pā yaṃ pratne, prā ge and ci ri, which take no number, tanaṭ always comes: sā yaṃ ta naṃ, pra ge ta naṃ, ci ranta naṃ. By a set form, the ending in e is simply the ending in a.

do ṣā ta naṃ. It is said precisely "from what expresses time"; otherwise what comes from heaven is svorbaṃ.

From sandhya and the rest, from words for times and lunar mansions, aṇ.

From sandha and the rest, and from words for times and lunar mansions, aṇ comes: sāndhyaṃ, sāndhi belaṃ, graiṣma, śai ṣi ram, tai ṣa raṃ, tai ṣaṃ, po ṣaṃ. The group is: sandhya, sandhi be la, a mā bā syā, tra yo da śī, pañca da śī, porṇa mā sī, bṛ ti pa ta. By mentioning nakṣa tra, the time marked by a lunar mansion is to be taken, because time is the general heading. By mentioning aṇ, aṇ itself comes as stated, and not śas; this is the sense of "from paurṇa māsya and the rest".

From sāṃ batsa ra, for pha la and parba ṇa.

aṇ comes: sāmbatsam ra pha laṃ, sāṃ batsa raṃ parba. Otherwise sambatsa ri ka, ā yaḥ.

From he manta, optionally elision of ta.

From he manta aṇ comes, and optionally ta is elided: hai ma naṃ, hai mantaṃ. Some say that aṇ too is optional; hence hai manti kaṃ.

From barṣā and prā bṛt, ṭhak and ai ṇya.

They come in that order: bārṣi kaṃ, pra bṛṣe ṇya.

From a part, of a pa ri tu: kit, ñit, ṇit.

When something is formed from words expressing time with a suffix marked k, ñ or ṇ, it is to be understood that, even where these have prior parts, the operation falls on the time word at the end. With kit: pūrba bārṣi kaṃ, a pa ra bārṣi kaṃ. With ñit: pūrba graiṣmi kaṃ. With ṇit: prūrba hai ma naṃ, a pa ra hai ma naṃ.

From ā di and ma dhya, ma.

It comes: a di maḥ, ma dhya maḥ.

From a dhas and a bas, elision also.

ma comes, and the final is also elided and changed.

From ma dhya, a, in the sense of the present.

"The present" is what is spoken of as being fitting. When that is to be expressed, the letter a comes from ma dya: ma dhyā, meaning a woman who is neither too tall nor too short, of fitting proportions.

From a gra, anta and paśca, a ma.

For these, a mi comes: a gri ma, inti maḥ, paṣci maḥ.

The remaining senses have been explained. Now I shall state the particular remaining senses.

Thus ends the third quarter of the fourth chapter of the Commentary in Eight Thousand on Sādhukīrti's Mañjuśrī Grammar, composed by the master of the land of the bodhisattva, Rājadeva, lord of the land of glorious Kaliṅga.

Homage to the Buddha.

From ṅi, in the sense of born, from bṛṣ, ṭhan.

From the word prā bṛṭ ending in the seventh case, in the sense of born, ṭhan comes, as an exception to e ṇya. One born in the rainy season is prā bṛ ṣi kaḥ. Where something is stated in the sense of born and the like, the i ka and other suffixes made for it do not come from other stems either; these are simply remaining senses, because particular remaining senses are established from particular bases. For that very reason this is a sense other than the base. Others too come here as appropriate: the general an and the rest, gha and the rest, as remaining senses. One born in Śrughna is sroghnaḥ; likewise mā thuraḥ. One born in the realm is rāṣṭi yaḥ; likewise bā rā pā ri ṇiḥ. This one born in the rainy season is prā bṛ ṣe ṇya; likewise brārtha baḥ.

From śa rad, for a name, buña.

It comes from the stem ending in the seventh case, in the sense of born, as an exception to the aṇ for ri lu: śā ra da kāḥ, darbha grass, or mudga beans. Why is it said "for a name"? śār daṃ, the crop.

From pūrbā dna, a pa rā hna, ardā, mū la and pra do ṣa, kan.

It comes from stems ending in the seventh case, in the sense of born: pū rbāhna kaḥ, a ba rā hna kaḥ; here it is an exception to ta naṭ and an. The time marked by the moon's conjunction with the lunar mansion Ārdrā is ārdha, and one born then is ārdhra kaḥ; likewise mū la kaḥ. It is an exception to the aṇ from a lunar mansion. By the rule "ke an" the vowel is short. pra dau ṣa kaḥ: an exception to the ṭhañ and añ of "from śa śva ta, ni śā and pra do ṣa". a baska ra kaḥ: an exception to the general an. It is said precisely "for a name"; otherwise pūrbāhne ta naṃ, baurbā hni kaṃ.

panthaka.

In the sense of born, kan, as the name of one who shows the path, by a set form.

Optionally, for sindhu and a ba ka ra.

In the sense of born, for a name, kan comes optionally: sindhu kaḥ, or sindhu paḥ, with añ because it is read in the kaccha group; a pa ka ra kaḥ, with aṇ in the remaining sense. It is said precisely "for a name"; otherwise saindhu ba kaḥ, a man, by the rule "for a man and his abode, buñ".

For ba sya and a ba sya with a mā first, and a also.

For ba sya and a ba sya preceded by a mā, in the sense of born, kan comes optionally, and a also: a mā ba sya kaḥ, a mā ba syaḥ; a mā bā sya kaḥ, a mā bā syaḥ. Because they belong to the sandhya group, aṇ.

From batsa śva la, a śvi nī, mṛ ga śi raḥ, a bhi ji ta, a śva yug and śa ta bhi ṣak, elision optionally.

That is, of what is made in the sense of born. One born in a calf pen is batsa śā laḥ or bātsa śā laḥ. Because the particular gender is taken, one born in a calf pen is batsa śā laḥ or bātsa śā laḥ. The time marked by the moon's conjunction with Aśvinī is a śvi nī; one born then is a śvī or ā śvī naḥ. The time marked by the moon's conjunction with Mṛgaśiras is mṛ ga śi rāḥ; one born then is mṛ ga śi rāḥ or mārgga śī rā. Likewise ā bhi ji ta; a śva yu ga, a śva yu jaḥ; śva ta bṛ ṣa jaḥ. From śa ta bhiṣ, an, with elision of iṭ as well: śva ta bhi ṣaḥ.

From what ends in sthā na, from go śā la and kha ra śa la.

What is made in the sense of born is always elided. One born in a cow stall is go śā laḥ; or one born in a cow pen is go śā laḥ. Likewise kha ra śā laḥ. By the rule "se nā, su rā, śā lā and ni śā", both genders apply.

From śra biṣṭā, phalgu nī, a nu rā dhā, svā ti, ti ṣya, puṣya, pu narba su, bi śā khāḥ, ā ṣā ḍhā, ba hu lā and hasta.

What is made in the sense of born is elided. The time marked by the moon's conjunction with the star śva biṣṭha is śra biṣṭhīḥ; one born when joined with it is śva biṣṭhaḥ. By the rule "where there is elision, and elision of what follows it, not for go ṇya and the rest", the feminine suffix is set aside. Likewise phalgu naḥ, arju naḥ, a nu rā dhaḥ, svā tiḥ, tiṣyaḥ, pu narba suḥ, ba ra ru ciḥ, ni śā khaḥ, āṣā ḍhaḥ, ba hu la; ba hu lāḥ denotes Kṛttikā, the Pleiades; hastaḥ. When these apply to a woman, the feminine suffix arises: śra biṣṭhā mā na bi kā, a nu ra dhā mā ṇa bi kāḥ.

From ci trā, ro hi ṇī and re ba tī, optionally when not feminine.

From ce tra and the rest, outside the domain of the feminine, it is optionally elided; for the feminine, always.

ce traḥ, ce traṃ ku laṃ; ro hi ṇaḥ, rau hi ṇaḥ; re ba ta, rai ba taḥ. For the feminine: ci traḥ, ro hi ṇīḥ, re ba ti, with dīp because they belong to the gau ra group. From bhi ṣa ja, ṭid, that is, aṇ: śāt bhiṣa jaḥ.

From śva biṣṭhā and ā ṣa ḍha, not for chaṇ.

For these, in the sense of born, chan is not elided; for that very reason the formation of chaṇ for these is to be inferred: śrā biṣṭhā ya, āṣā ḍhrī yaḥ.

Also for kṛ ta, labdha, krī ta, ku śa la and the rest, of those.

Those remaining suffixes that serve for the sense of born also come in the senses made, gained and the rest, from stems ending in the seventh case. For one born in Śrughna, or made, gained, bought or skilled there: kritaḥ, labdhaḥ, krī taḥ, ku śa laḥ: śrau ghnaḥ; likewise pā rī ṇaḥ, grā myaḥ, grā mī ṇaḥ. This is stated for the sake of establishing the distinctions and for ease of understanding. Otherwise it is jā tī ya kaṃ. "Of those" also continues, as appropriate.

From words for time, in the senses good, flowering and ripening.

In these senses the suffixes come from stems ending in the seventh case: he ma he sādhuḥ, he ma naḥ, prā bā raḥ, hai manti, hai manti ka; ba sante puṣpyanti, bā santi, bā santuḥ su ma na saḥ; śa ra dī pa cyante, śā ra dāḥ śā la yaḥ.

From a śva yu jyā, for what is sown, buñ.

The word a śva yu ja is a synonym of Aśvinī; the full moon marked by the moon's conjunction with it is a śva yu jī. Therefore buñ comes from it ending in the seventh case: ā śva yu ja kā, pulses. Why is it said "from a śva yu ja"? he mante āptā he ma nāḥ, hai manti kaḥ, he mantāḥ. If one asks why it does not come here, in a śva yu jyā muptaṃ kṣe traḥ: the word upta here ends in kta without a base and refers to the field, so it does not have the character of the principal. The use of "only" serves the following rule.

Optionally, from graiṣma and ba stanta.

For what is sown, buñ comes optionally from stems ending in the seventh case, as an exception to aṇ from words for time: greṣma kaṃ, graṣmaṃ; pā santa kaṃ, bā santaṃ.

Only for a debt to be paid.

Those of the remaining suffixes that apply to words expressing time come from stems ending in the seventh case only for debts to be paid. A debt to be paid monthly is mā si rkaṃ; likewise ardha mā sirkaṃ, sāmbatsa ri kaṃ. Why is it said "only a debt"? mā se de yā bhakṣā.

From ka lā pi, a śvattha and ya ba bu sa, buñ.

From ka lā pi and the rest, applied to the times marked by ka lā pi and the rest, buñ comes for a debt to be paid. The time when the peacocks bear their tail feathers is ka lā pī kā laḥ; the time when the aśvattha trees bear fruit is a śvatthaḥ; the time when the barley has grown its husks is ya ba pu ṣaḥ. A debt to be paid at the time of tail feathers is ka lā pa kaṃ, with the ṭi elided by the rule "from ka lā bya and the rest"; likewise a śvattha kaṃ.

From a ba ra sa mā and grāṣmi, buñ.

From a ba ra sa mā and grāṣmi, buñ comes for a debt to be paid, where ṭhañ would be obtained from a bar sva mā and aṇ for time from grāṣmi. A debt to be paid in the next year is a ba ra sa ma kaṃ; a debt to be paid in summer is graiṣma kaṃ.

From saṃ batsa ra and ma gra hā ya na, ṭhañ also.

For these, ṭhañ comes for a debt to be paid: sāmbatsa ri kaṃ, sāmbatsa ra kaṃ; ā gra hā ya ṇi kaṃ, ā gra hā laṇ kaṃ. As for what is to be expressed by "also": in "ṭhañ; summer; a debt to be paid yearly; fruit", aṇ does not come here by the rule "from sāmbatsa ra, with the word fruit".

For an animal that dwells there.

When an animal that dwells at a time is to be expressed from a word expressing time, the remaining suffixes come as prescribed from stems ending in the seventh case. An animal that dwells about at night is nai śi kaḥ or nai śaḥ; pra doṣaḥ, prā do ṣi kaḥ. In the alternative given by the rule "from śa śvat, ni śā and pra doṣa", ṭhañ. Why is it said "animal"? ullu kaḥ, which dwells at night.

Of this, so ḍhaṃ. From time, from stems ending in the seventh case.

In the sense of ḍhaṃ, the remaining suffixes come as prescribed. What produces colour at night is soḍhaṃ; "of this": nai śi kaḥ, nai śaḥ, a student. sau ḍha expresses mastery through habituation.

From dig and the rest, in the sense of arisen, yat.

From dig and the rest, ending in the seventh case, in the sense of arisen, yat comes: what arises in a direction is di śyaṃ. The group is: bargya, de śa, barga, pū ga, ga ṇa, pakṣa, dhya yyā, mittra, me dha, anta ra, pa thin, ra ha sa, a lī ka, u khā, sā kṣin, ā di, anta, mu kha, ja gha na. These are read in the sense of what is neither the body nor a part of it. "Army" is that of the chief and of the frontier world. me gha, yū tha, nyā ya, a nu baṃ śa, biṇ, kā la, ā kā śa. The word bha ba has the senses of perception, existence, armour and non-arising. This is because the meaning is understood from what precedes. Why is it said "from dik and the rest"? srau ghna, bārha sba tyaṃ. u da kyā, for a menstruating woman, is a set form.

From body and limb.

From words expressing the body and its limbs, ending in the seventh case, in the sense of arisen, yat comes: what arises from the teeth is dantya; likewise auṣṭhyaṃ, rā la baṃ, murdha ṇya, mu khyaṃ, kaṇyaṃ, u ra byaṃ.

From dṛ ti, kukṣi, ka la śi, basti, asti and a hi, ḍha ña.

From dṛ ti and the rest, ending in the seventh case, in the sense of arisen, ḍhañ comes: what arises from a leather bag is dārtte yaṃ; likewise kaukṣa yaṃ. The word ka la śi is a synonym of "pot": kā la śe yaṃ; likewise baste yaṃ, āste yaṃ. This word āsti is an indeclinable, not one ending in a verbal ending (tiṅ); it has the sense of existing and the sense of wealth. This is stated where yat would be obtained for dṛ ti and ku kṣi because they are limbs of the body, and aṇ for the rest.

For grī ba, aṇ also.

From the word grī ba aṇ comes, and by the syllable "also", ḍhañ as well. What arises from the neck is grai be yaṃ. This is stated where aṇ would be obtained by "from body and limb".

From gambhi ri and pañcaja na, ña.

It comes in the sense of arisen: gāmbhī ra, pāñcaja nyaṃ. From ba hir and de ba, yañ comes: bā hyaḥ, dai byaṃ.

From ca turmā sa, for a sacrifice.

From it, ending in the seventh case, in the sense of arisen, ñya comes: ca turmā syā ni ya jñā ni. It is compounded by the rule "compound with a final word, in the sense of a taddhita".

For a name, aṇ.

It comes from ca turmā sa: what occurs at the end of the four months is cā turmā sī. It also designates ā ṣā ḍhī, paurṇṇa mā si, kārtti kī and phalgu nī.

From pa ri mu kha and the rest, of indeclinable formation.

From stems ending in the seventh case, in the sense of arisen, ñya comes: what arises from the mouth is pa ri mu khyaṃ; likewise pa ri ha na byaṃ. Why is it said "pa ri mu kha and the rest"? au pa kū laṃ, o pa kū paṃ. The group is: pa ri mu khaṃ, pa ri ha nu, pormaṣṭha, purya lū kha la, pa ri sā ra, a nu sī ra, u pa sṭhūn, u pa ka lā pa, a nu pa tha, a nu śaṅka, a nu ti la, a nu śī ta, a nu ma sa, a nu sū pa, a nu baṃ śa. Why is it said "from an indeclinable formation"? ma rig to mu khaṃ is pa ri mu khaṃ, and what arises there is pā ri mu khaṃ.

From a compound with anta ra as prior member, ṭhañ.

From an indeclinable formation whose prior word is antar, ṭhañ comes: āntarge hi kaṃ, āntarṇaṃ śi kaṃ. In the sense of the analysis "ge ha sya ma dhyaṃ, baṃ śa sya madhyaṃ", it is an indeclinable formation.

From grā ma, with pa ri and a nu.

From grā ma preceded by pa ri or a nu, in the sense of arisen, ṭhañ comes: pā ri grā mi kaṃ, a nu grā mi kaṃ. It is said precisely "from an indeclinable formation"; otherwise pa ri ga to grā maṃ is pa ri grā ma, and what arises there is pa ri grā ma.

antaḥ pū ri ka, for a name.

It is a set form, said of something possessed by one particular person who has gone into the royal household. Why is it said "for a name"? What arises from the middle of the inner city is āntarpu ri kāṃ.

Also from sa mā na and the rest.

From stems ending in the seventh case, in the sense of arisen, ṭhañ comes: sā mā grā mi ka, sa ma ni da śi ka, sā dṛ śi kaḥ. By the syllable "also", from the simple word too: sā mā ni kaḥ.

From what ends in lo ka.

From that word, in the sense of arisen, ṭhañ comes: pā ra lau ki kaṃ, ai ha lo ki kaṃ.

Also from sā mā na and the rest.

From stems ending in the seventh case, in the sense of arisen, ṭhañ comes. What concerns the self is ādhyā tma, and what arises from it is ādhyātmi kaṃ; likewise ā dhi dai bi kaṃ, ā dhi bhau ti kaḥ, cā turthi kaḥ, śai śiṅ kaḥ, bai śe ṣi kaḥ. The strengthening of the final word is a particular of the remaining sense. sām pra ti kaḥ, aurdhva da mi kaḥ, aurdhva de hi kaḥ. The word urdhva da ma ka is like the word ūrdhva. This is an open class.

From aṃ gu li and ji hvā mū la, cha.

From these, in the sense of arisen, cha comes, blocking yat: aṅgu lī yaḥ, ji hvā mū lī yaḥ.

From what ends in bargga.

From that word, in the sense of arisen, cha comes: ka barggī yaḥ, barggī yaḥ.

When not of a sound, yat and kha also.

From what ends in barggā, in the sense of arisen, when it does not refer to a speech sound, cha comes, and yat and kha also: arju na barggā yaḥ, arju na barggīṇ. Why is it said "when not of a sound"? cā barggā yaḥ, a letter.

From ma dhya, maṇ and mi ya also.

They come in the sense of arisen, and by the syllable "also", cha as well: mā dhya maḥ, ma dhya mī yaḥ, mi dhyī yaḥ.

For di na, mum.

When the suffix di ba follows ma dhya, the augment mum comes; for that very reason the formation of the suffix di na is to be inferred. What arises from the middle is ma dhyā npā nīḥ: "they sing", uṅga yanti.

From la lāṭ, for an ornament, kaṇ.

It comes, blocking yat: la lā ṭi kā. Why is it said "for an ornament"? la lā ṭyā.

From karṇa.

For an ornament, kan comes; this too is a blocking. The ornament arising from the ear is karṇi kā. Why is it said "for an ornament"? karṇyaṃ.

From a compound with u pa as prior member, ṭhak.

From kan preceded by u pa, in the sense of arisen, ṭhak comes. What is near the ear is u pa karṇaṃ, and what arises from it is au pa karṇi kaḥ. It is said precisely "for an ornament"; otherwise u pa karṇya.

From jā nu and ni bī.

Preceded by up, in the sense of arisen, ṭhak comes. What is near the knee is u pa jā nu, and what arises from it is au pa jā nu kaṃ; likewise u pā nī bi kaṃ. Because it is established as arising from many, it is adopted without distinction.

For an explanation, from the name of what is to be explained, also.

What is explained by this is what is to be explained; thus there is the name of what is to be explained and the name of the explanation. From that, from stems ending in the sixth case in the sense of explanation, and also from stems ending in the seventh case in the sense of arisen, the taddhita suffixes come as prescribed. The explanation of the sub endings, and what arises from the sub endings, is sau paṃ ti daṃ; likewise kārttaḥ. Why is it said "from the name of what is to be explained"? su ko śa la, the explanation of pā ṭa li pu tra. If one says, "Since one who has properly entered su ko śa la makes the statement 'pā ṭa li pu ṭa li pur ṭar', su ko śa la is precisely what is to be explained of pā ṭa li pu tra," that is not so either: since pā ṭa li pu tra is fully established as what is to be explained itself, it does not come from its formation.

From what ends in sthā ma and a ji na, elision.

What is made in the sense of arisen is elided: a śvatthā mā, kṛṣṇā ji naṃ.

From the names of sacrifices, for a mantra and a ritual manual.

What is made in the senses of arisen and explanation is elided. The mantra arising from the fire sacrifice is igniṣṭo maḥ; the ritual manual that explains the fire sacrifice is agni ṣṭo maḥ.

From ri ka, and from stems of many vowels, not from saṃ hi ta, ṭhañ.

From the word ri ka, and from names of what is to be explained that have many vowels, leaving aside the word saṃ hi ta, ṭha comes in the senses of arisen and explanation: ārci ka, cā tu rdo tri kaṃ, brāhma ṇi kaṃ, prā tha mi kaṃ, ā dha ri kaṃ, pau ra ṣca ra ṇi kaṃ, pā śuk kaṃ, aiṣṭi kaṃ, nā mi kaṃ, ā khyā ti kaṃ, nā mā khya ti kaṃ, ṣā tra ṇa tri kaṃ, nā tā na ti kaṃ. Likewise from words expressing sacrifices: rā ja sū yi kaṃ, bā ja pa yi kaṃ, āgni ṣṭo mi kaṃ, pā ga ya jña kaṃ. Why is it said "not from saṃ hi tā"? What arises from saṃ hi tā is sāṃ hi taḥ.

From the name of a sage, for recitation.

Stating what is already established by the preceding rule is for the sake of restriction. From words for sages, as names of what is to be explained, ṭhañ comes in the senses of arisen and explanation only when recitation is expressed. The explanation of the text of Vasiṣṭha, or what arises from it, is bā śiṣṭhi kaḥ, a recitation; likewise bai śva mi tri kaḥ. Why is it said "for recitation"? bā śi ṣṭhī ri ka. One need not make an excluding restriction by saying "only ri ṣi, for recitation". There is no unwanted meaning in the treatise, because the authors of the system have explained it properly.

From pau ra ḍā śa and pu ro ḍa śa, ṣi ka na.

It comes in the senses of arisen and explanation. pu ro ḍā śāḥ are cakes, pi ṇṭa kaḥ; the mantra that consecrates them is pau ro ḍā śaḥ; its explanation, or what arises from it, is pau ro ḍā ṣi kaḥ, pau ro ḍā śi kīḥ. The word pu ro ḍā śa also means the mantra: pu ro ḍā śaḥ; its explanation, or what arises from it, is pu ro ḍā śi ka, pu ro ḍā śi kīḥ.

From ri ga ya na and the rest, aṇ.

It comes in the senses of arisen and explanation, as an exception to ṭhañ and the rest: ri cā ma dhya ya na, ri ga dhya ya naṃ. By extension, it is said of the text that is the object of the act of recitation.

In the sense of its explanation, or of what arises from it: ār ga ya na. By a set form, the na is ṇa. It is the explanation of the pada. ri ga ya na is in the sense of the explanation of words. chandobhāṣā, chando bi ci ti, chando bi ji ti, chando mā ṇaḥ, chando byā khyā na, nyā ya, pu na rukta, byā ka ra ṇa, ni ga ma, bastu, bidyā, aṅga bidyā, kṣa tra bi dyā, tri bidyā, utpā ta, da saṃ bats, yajña, ca, cakra, me ta ra, ślakṣṇa, ni rukta, mu hūrtta, ni mitta, u pa ni ṣad, śīkṣā.

From chandas, yat also.

It comes in the senses of arisen and explanation. By the word "also", aṇ comes too: cchanda syaṃ, cchanda saṃ.

From āyasthāna, in the sense of "come from".

āya is the portion taken by the lord. The place where it arises is the āyasthāna. From a fifth-case ending denoting an āyasthāna, ṭhañ comes in the sense "come from this". What comes from the enclosure of the toll-takers is śaulka śā li ka; likewise ā ta ri kaḥ, a ba ṇi kaḥ. Why is it said "from āyasthāna"? What comes from Śrughna is śraughnaḥ; likewise rāṣṭa ya, and so on.

From śuṇḍika and the rest, aṇ.

It comes in the sense of "come from", from an āyasthāna, as an exception to ṭhañ: śauṇḍi kaḥ, kārka ṇaḥ. The list: śu ṇḍa kaḥ, kri ka ra ṇaḥ, sthāṇḍa la, u pa bā na, u da ka, tīrtha, bhū mi, tra ṇi, barṇa, u pa la, śrau ta pa. The word ki ka ṇa denotes what is not the domain of bhā ra dhvā ja.

From relations of learning and of birth, buñ.

Relations of learning and of birth are the preceptor and the like, and the grandfather and the like. buñ comes in the sense of what has come from such a relation of learning or of birth. What has come from the preceptor is au pā dhya ya kaṃ; likewise śe ṣya kaṃ, ā cārya kaṃ, mā tā ma hā kaṃ, bai tā ma ha kaṃ, ma ru la kaṃ.

From ṛt, ṭhañ.

ṭhañ comes in the sense of what has come from a relation of learning or of birth ending in ṛt: hau tri kaṃ, bi tṛ kaṃ, mā tri kaṃ, svā sṛ kaṃ, bha ti kaṃ. It is said precisely "relations of learning and of birth"; otherwise sā bi traṃ.

From pitṛ, yat optionally.

It comes in the sense of "come from": bai tṛ kaṃ, pi tyaṃ.

From a man and from causes, rūpya and mayaṭ.

rūpya and mayaṭ come optionally in the sense of what has come from a man or from a cause: de ba datta rū pyaṃ, de ba datta ma yaṃ, dai ba dattaṃ. From a cause: sa mā dā ga taṃ, sa ma rū pyaṃ, sa ma ma yaṃ, bi ṣa ma pyaṃ, bi ṣa ma ma mi yaṃ, bi ṣa mā yaṃ. Because of the class beginning with gaha itself, cha. Here it is not as with the view of number, since that is not wanted.

From a gotra, as if "this is its".

Setting aside the descendant in general, only the worldly gotra descendant is taken. From its ending, the taddhita suffixes come in a sense like "this is its". What has come from glu bu ka ya ni is glau bu ka yan kaṃ. What has come from the sons of Upagu is au pa ga ba ki. Likewise gārgā, dākṣaṃ, śā ka laṃ, śā ka li kaṃ.

pra bha ba ti.

From a fifth-case ending, in the sense of pra bha ba ti, aṇ and the rest come as prescribed: from hi ma ba ta. pra bha ba ti means being first perceived when going forth into view. Thus hai ma ba tiḥ, gaṅ gā, dar daḥ, sindhuḥ.

From bi dū ra, ñya.

It comes from a fifth-case ending in the sense of going forth, that is, originating. What goes forth from bi du ra is bai ḍūryaḥ, the jewel. By the word bai ḍūra the mountain of bā la bā la ya is spoken of everywhere. "The messengers of the path that goes there" is another sūtra. How then is it śru ghnaṃ ga ccha ti pan thāḥ, "the path goes to Śrughna"? Or it is the messenger: śrau ghnaḥ, rāṣṭi yaḥ. The gate that opens out toward Śrughna is śrau ghnaṃ. It also applies when one wishes to express what has come to bear a name, and a kinsman at the end.

With reference to śi śu kranda and the rest, and to illnesses, cha, for a composed text.

A text composed with reference to the illness of a child's crying is śi śu kran dī yaḥ; likewise ya ma sa bhī yaḥ, śin tā nbe ṣa ṇī yaḥ. adhi kri tya, pra stu tya and ā gūrya: this is the meaning of "with reference to". Why is it said "from a child's crying and the rest"? A text composed with reference to Subhadrā is sau bha draḥ; likewise so mi traḥ. Why is it said "text"? Something else made with reference to a child's crying is śai śū krandaṃ. The list: śi śu kra da ni, ya ma sa bha, indra ja na na, śī tī nve ṣa na, pradyu ma na, śī tā ha ri ṇa. They are read by leading the students near.

From compounds in the sense of "and", other than deva-asura and the like.

From a second-case ending, cha comes for a text composed with reference to: śye na ka po tī yaḥ, agni kā śā pī yaḥ, sambatma ru ta tiḥ, bakya pa dī yaḥ, ki rā tā rju ni yaḥ, śabdu rthā sambandha nī yaḥ. Why is it said "other than deva-asura"? de bā su raḥ, dakṣo pu ra, gauṇa mu khyaḥ.

Elision, variously, for ākhyāyikās.

The suffix of composition, from a text that is an ākhyāyikā composed with reference to something, is dropped variously. The ākhyāyikā composed with reference to the one given by the son of Vasudeva is bā sa ba dāttā; likewise su ma nott ra dā, su ma nott ra dā, ūrba sī. It also fails to occur: bhai mar thī.

From su, in the sense of residence.

From su, that is, from a first-case ending, aṇ and the rest come as prescribed in the sense "this is his residence". One who resides in Śrughna is śraughnaḥ; likewise grā myaḥ.

From places of origin, for those who live by weapons.

From names of particular mountains with a first-case ending, as places of origin, cha comes in the sense of a sixth case, "of this", when denoting those who live by weapons. For those whose place of origin is the mountain hriṅgo la: hriṅga la'i yāḥ, those who live by weapons. Likewise bho ja ka ṭī yaḥ, bho ja gi rī yaḥ. Why is it said "from mountains"? sāṃ kā śya kāḥ, those who live by weapons. Why is it said "for those who live by weapons"? ri kṣo daḥ: these belong to the mountain's skins, that is, they are brahmins: ārkṣo dāḥ. Why is it said "places of origin"? Those who live by weapons and dwell in the Vindhya are bainddhyaḥ. The place of origin is the place where one's forebears dwelt in former generations, as far as residence within the lineage extends. The place where one has properly dwelt oneself is the residence.

From śaṇḍika and the rest, ñya.

It comes in the sense "this is its place of origin". That whose place of origin is śaṇḍa ka is śāṇḍi kyaḥ; likewise kau ca pa bāryaḥ, śākyaḥ. The list: śaṇḍi ka, ku ca bā ra, śa ka, sarba se na, sarba ke śa, śa ṭa, ra tha, śaṃ kha, ca na kaḥ, bo dha.

From sindhu and the rest, aṇ.

It comes in the sense "this is its place of origin". That whose place of origin is sindhu is sain dhu baḥ; likewise bālla baḥ, kāśmī raḥ. The list: sindhu, ballu, gan dhā ra, ma dhu mat, kambo ja, kaś mī ra, śā lva, ka skan dhi, śab di ka, u ras, datta, takṣa, śi lā, bat soddha ra ṇa, kra me dur, dāṇ ḍa bā ra, grā ma ṇī, śa rā la, saṃ ka, kin na ra, saṃ ku ci taḥ, si ma ha, koṣ ṭha, karṇa, barb ba ra, cha ga la, saṃ u ta, ā ba, sā na. Since aṇ is already established by their having been read above in the class beginning with ka ca, they are stated here for the purpose of an exception to buñ.

And those denoting countries too.

bā hī ka grā mā ṇāṃ is for the purpose of an exception to ṭhañ and kañ.

From tu dī and bar ma ti, ḍhañ.

It comes in the sense "this is its place of origin", as an exception to aṇ: tau de yaḥ, bār ma te yaḥ.

From śa lā ru ra, chaṇ.

It comes in the sense "this is its place of origin": śā lā ru rī yaḥ.

From di, for devotion, from mahārāja, ṭhak.

"From di" means from a seventh-case ending: ṭhak comes from mahārāja in the sense of devotion. One devoted to the great king is mā hā rā ji kaḥ. Why is it said "from mahārāja"? One devoted to the Buddha is baud dhaḥ. One devoted to Śrughna is śraughnaḥ; likewise rāṣṭrī yaḥ.

From what is without mind, from places and times.

From a seventh-case ending, ṭhak comes in the sense of devotion: ā sū pi kaḥ, śāṣ ku li kaḥ. Why is it said "from what is without mind"? śraughnaḥ, hai ma naḥ.

From bā su de ba and ar ju na, vun.

It comes from a seventh-case ending in the sense of devotion: bā su de ba kaḥ, ar ju na kaḥ. bā su de ba is mentioned for the sake of a kṣatriya name that is not a gotra.

From a gotra, in the plural, buñ.

From a seventh-case ending of a stem ending in a gotra suffix, buñ comes in the sense of devotion, in the plural: au pa ga ba kaḥ, kā ṭa pa kaḥ, dakṣa kaḥ. It also fails to occur: one devoted to Pāṇini is pā ṇi nī yaḥ.

From a kṣatriya.

From a seventh-case ending denoting a kṣatriya, buñ comes in the sense of devotion, in the plural: nā ku la kaḥ, sā ha de ba kaḥ. It also fails to occur: one devoted to pau ra ba is pau ra bī yaḥ.

From a country name, the gotra ending likewise, all.

Whatever gotra suffix is applied to a kṣatriya from a country name, from its ending, from a seventh-case ending, in the sense of devotion, everything comes just as for the country. One devoted to the countries of aṅga and baṅga is āṅga kaḥ, bāṅga kaḥ; likewise one devoted to the kṣatriyas of aṅga is āṅga kaḥ, bāṅga kaḥ. The word "all" is mentioned so that the base too becomes just as for the country. One devoted to those outside madra is mā dra kaḥ; likewise bṛ ji kaḥ. Why is it said "from a country name"? pau ra bī yaḥ. It is said precisely "from a kṣatriya"; otherwise one devoted to the brahmins of Pañcāla is pāñ cā laḥ. The word "ending" is mentioned so that it is just the same under elision too; otherwise the procedure for that ending itself would have to be understood directly. One devoted to the āṅgas and the aṅgas is aṅga kaḥ.

From ṭā, for what is proclaimed.

"From ṭā" means from a third-case ending: aṇ and the rest come as prescribed in the sense of what is proclaimed. What is proclaimed by Pāṇini is pā ṇi nī yaṃ; likewise ā pi śā laṃ, au śa na saṃ, bār das pa tyaṃ, cān dra, ain draṃ.

For a Veda, solely for the one who recites it and the one who knows it.

For a Veda that has been proclaimed, whatever taddhita comes from a third-case ending comes solely in the domain of the one who recites it and the one who knows it. The Veda proclaimed by Pippalāda, or one who recites that Veda, is pai bya lā daḥ; likewise mai tra ya ṇī yaḥ, cā ra ya ṇī yaḥ. Why is it said "for a Veda"? The verses proclaimed by Pippalāda are pe bya lā daḥ. Why is the word "solely" used? pi bya lā de na prok to be daḥ, "the Veda proclaimed by Pippalāda".

From tit ta ri, ba ra tan tu, khaṇ ḍi ka and u kha, chaṇ.

For a Veda proclaimed by these, chaṇ comes, and that too strictly in the domain of reciting and knowing it. The Veda proclaimed by tit ta ri, or one who recites that Veda, is tit ti rī yaṃ; likewise bā ra tan ta bī yaḥ, khāṇ ḍā kī yaḥ, au khī yaḥ.

From ka śya pa and kau śi ka, also for the ritual manual of the sages, ṇini.

For a Veda proclaimed by these, or for a ritual manual too, ṇini comes, and that too solely for reciting and knowing. The Veda or ritual manual made by the sage, or one who recites that Veda, is kā śya pi ṇaḥ, kau śi ka niḥ. Why is it said "of the sages"? kā śya pī yaḥ kal paḥ. Why is it said "ritual manual"? kā śya pī yāḥ, together with the ancient histories. The words "it is not that the proclaiming of a Veda by these is excluded" apply to something else. The reciting too is a ritual manual, and it is said to have been proclaimed by them. How does the word "also" apply there? It has the sense of inclusion: all that exists in tradition is explained as its domain. Therefore proclamation in some other way by the word is not excluded. The ritual manual is what serves the proclamation of the Veda. A branch together with its chant is included too; otherwise kā śya pī yā pū ra ṇa paṃ ti tā would not be given as an example.

From śau na ka and the rest.

ṇini comes for a proclaimed Veda, and that too solely for reciting and knowing, as an exception to aṇ. One who knows or recites the Veda proclaimed by śo na ka is śau na ka na; likewise bā ja sa ne yi naḥ. It is said precisely "Veda"; otherwise śau na kī yaḥ, the training. The list: śau na ka, bā je ne ya, śāṃ garba, śā pe ya, śā sye ya, khā ḍā ya na, skan dha, skam bha, de ba darṣa, rajju bhā ra, rajju kaḥ, ka ṣā ya, ka ṭha śā ṭha. This is what is read in the class. Therefore one who knows or recites the Veda proclaimed by them is klā ṭha śa ṭhi naḥ.

From the disciples of ka lā pi and bai śal pā ya na.

For a proclaimed Veda ṇini comes, and that too solely for reciting and knowing. Of these, ka lā pi has four disciples: ha ri dru, tum bu ru, u la pa and cha li na. bai śam pā ya na has nine disciples: ā lam ba, pa liṅ ga, ka ma lā, prā cyā, śyā mā yan, ka ka tha, lī pi na, u dī cyā, ri cyā bha, ā ru ṇi, taṇ ḍa and ma dha. Direct disciples are taken here, in order to treat the disciples of disciples separately. The disciple of ka ṭha also belongs to khā ḍā ya na, because it is read in the class beginning with pra śau ka.

One who knows or recites the Veda proclaimed by ha ri dru is ha ri dra bi ṇaḥ; likewise taum ba ra bi ṇaḥ, cha ga li na, ḍhin na kaṃ bakṣā ti, ā lam bi naḥ, pā liṅ ga naḥ, śya ma ya ni naḥ, ā rtsā bhi niḥ, ā rtsā bhi naḥ, ā ru ṇi naḥ, tāṇ ḍa naḥ. From ka ṭha, elision will be explained. From ka lā piṇ, aṇ also.

From ka ṭha and ca ra ka, elision.

From the ending of these, the suffix applied for a proclaimed Veda is elided. One who knows the Veda proclaimed by ka ṭha is ka ṭhāḥ; likewise ca ra kāḥ. ṇini and aṇ are elided respectively. ca ra ka is an explanation of bai ṣam bā ya na.

From ka lā pin, aṇ.

It comes in the sense of what is proclaimed, as an exception to ṇini. One who knows or recites the Veda proclaimed by ka lā pin is kā lā pāḥ. The mention of aṇ is for the purpose of an additional establishment: since chaṇ is also prohibited, in some cases aṇ itself comes as prescribed. Therefore what is proclaimed by ma thur is mā thū ri, the commentary; and sau la bhā ni, the brahmin woman.

From cha ga lin, ḍha.

It comes in the sense of what is proclaimed, as an exception to ṇini. One who knows or recites the Veda proclaimed by cha ga lin is cha ga le ya niḥ.

From kar man da and kṛ śāś va, for the sūtras of monks and of dance, i ṇi.

For what is proclaimed by these, i ṇi comes respectively for the sūtra of monks and the sūtra of dance, and that too solely for reciting and knowing. For one who knows or recites the sūtra of monks proclaimed by Devadatta: kar man di naḥ, that is, the monks. One who knows the sūtra of dance proclaimed by Lean Horse is kri śā śvi na, that is, the actors.

From pā rā śaryaḥ and śi lā li, ṇini.

For what is proclaimed by these, it comes respectively for the sūtra of monks and the sūtra of dance, and that too in the domain of reciting and knowing. One who knows or recites the sūtra of monks proclaimed by pā rā śarya is pā rā śā ri ṇaḥ, that is, the monks. By the rule "aṇ ti for the descendant", there is elision again. One who knows or recites the sūtra of dance proclaimed by śi lā lin is śai la li naḥ, that is, the actors.

From ancient sages, for a brāhmaṇa.

When an ancient sage is named, ṇini comes for a brāhmaṇa that he proclaimed, and that too solely for reciting and knowing. One who knows or recites the brāhmaṇa proclaimed by śā ṭyā yan is śā ṭyā ya na niḥ; likewise ai ti re ṣi ṇaḥ, bhāl la bhi naḥ. Why is "ancient" mentioned? What is proclaimed by yajña balka is ya jña bal ka ni, the brāhmaṇas. This itself is pu ra tā by a set form; the word pu rā ṇa and synonyms are not good here. This is the text that serves the brahmin Veda-knowers as the explanation of the secret mantras.

For a ritual manual.

It is said precisely "proclaimed": ṇini comes for a ritual manual proclaimed by that ancient sage. What is proclaimed by paṅga is paiṅ gā, the ritual manual; likewise ā ru ṇa pa rā jiḥ, the ritual manual. Whoever recites or knows the ritual manual of paiṅ ga ṇi is also paiṅ ga niḥ, from the establishment of elision after "proclaimed". It is said precisely "from ancient sages"; otherwise ā śma ra thaḥ, the ritual manual.

From a thar ba ṇa, for a Veda, aṇ.

It comes for what is proclaimed. The Veda proclaimed by Atharvan is ā thar ba ṇaḥ. How is it the Veda of Atharvan? bi śiṣ ṭhaḥ, a nu bā kaḥ: it is assigned to what was proclaimed by him, namely the four, Lord Lion, Guardian of Learning, and the rest. This is not merely for the proclaiming of a Veda in the sense of reciting and knowing; it is stated by way of obtaining.

From that, a single side, tas optionally.

From a third-case ending, tas comes optionally when denoting that from which there is one equal side. That which has a side in common with su dam bā me gha is su dā ma taḥ; sau dā ma nī, the lightning; su dā mi nāḥ, sau dā mi nī: so it should be considered. Likewise he ma ban taḥ, he ma ba tī, gaṅgā; rāṣ ṭa taḥ, rāṣ ṭa yaḥ. Because it is in the class beginning with sva ra, its ending is itself indeclinable.

From u ras, yat.

From a third-case ending, yat comes optionally in the sense of an equal side. Placing the arrow on a side even with the chest is u ra syaṃ; a ras taḥ. By the explanation of the arrangement, aṇ is set aside.

For u pa jñā ta.

aṇ and the rest come as prescribed. What was first understood by Pāṇini is bi nau ba de śe na jñā taṃ; this is an explanation given outside Pāṇini's own time. Likewise kā sa krit snaṃ, gu rū la gha baṃ, ā pi śa laṃ, dus ka ra ṇaṃ.

For what is made.

aṇ and the rest come as prescribed. The text made by Vararuci is bā ra ru caḥ. The text made by Śiva is śe baḥ. The song made by Nārada is nā ra dī yaṃ. The joy made by what is desired is aiṣ ṭa kaḥ; likewise tākṣṇa.

For a name.

For a name, aṇ and the rest come as prescribed in the sense of "made by that". What is made by the makṣika bee is mākṣi kaṃ, honey; likewise gār mu taṃ, sā ra ghaṃ, pait ti kaṃ, ka ṣau draṃ, bhra ma raṃ, bā ra ṭaṃ.

From bā ta pa, añ.

From a third-case ending it comes for what is made, as a name: bā ta paṃ. It is said precisely "for a name"; otherwise bā ta pī yaṃ.

From ku lā la and the rest, buñ.

From a third-case ending it comes for what is made by them: kau lā la kaṃ, bā ru ṭa kaṃ, en dri ya kaṃ, which is knowledge. It is said precisely "for a name"; otherwise kau lā laṃ. The list: ku lā la, ba ru ṭa, caṇ ḍa la, ni ṣā daḥ, kar mā ra sya nā, de ba rā ja, si ran dhīḥ, par ṣa taḥ, bad dhū, ru ru, a na ḍu ha, brā hma ṇa, kum dhā kā raḥ, śva bā ka.

Its; this.

In the sense of "this is its", aṇ and the rest come as prescribed from a sixth-case ending. The property of Upagu is au pa ga baṃ; likewise rāṣ ṭi yaṃ, śās tri yaṃ. It should be stated: "from property, and from villages, countries and men". It comes for these themselves. From a village: śraughnaḥ, rāṣ ṭi yaḥ. From a country: āṅ ga kaḥ. From a man: dai ba dattaḥ, yā jña dattaḥ. To show that it is not to be stated from anything else: ain draṃ, bārh spa tyaṃ, āg ne yaṃ, bā ru ṇaṃ, pai śā caṃ, a su raṃ. Why does it not come here: ta syān ta raṃ, a sya sa mī pa? Because there is no denoting of it.

From ra tha, and from what ends in it, yat.

It comes from a sixth-case ending. The property of a chariot is ra thyaṃ; likewise ca kraṃ, yu gaṃ. It is wanted only for a part of the chariot, not for anything else, because there is no denoting of it. From what ends in it: the property of two chariots is dvi ra thaṃ; likewise tri ra thaṃ. Elision takes place by the rule "elision for dvigu, for the descendant, for worship and the rest". Likewise ba ra ma ra thyaṃ.

From ra tha preceded by bā ha na, añ.

From ra tha it comes for property: ā śva ra thaṃ, rā sa bha ra thaṃ.

Also from bā ha na, a dhārya and pa ri ṣa da.

añ comes in the sense "this is its property": a śva mu pa ka ra ṇaṃ, rā bha saṃ, śriṅ ga laṃ, ā dhvar ya baṃ, pā ri ṣa daṃ. This is the statement.

From ha la and sī ra, ṭhak.

It comes for property from a sixth-case ending, as an exception to aṇ. The plough of this one is hā li kaṃ, the yoke; likewise sai ri kaṃ. The word sī ra means the plough, with dan tya and the rest.

From compounds in the sense of "and", other than deva and asura, for enemies, buñ.

It comes from a sixth-case ending. Crows and owls that have become enemies give kā ko lū ki kā; likewise a śva ma hi ṣi kā, mū ṣi ka mār ju ri kā. The feminine marker belongs to the base. Why is it said "other than deva and asura"? dai bā su raṃ, rak ṣo 'su raṃ.

For a marriage.

From compounds in the sense of "and", buñ comes. The marriage of Atri and bha ra dhvā ja is a ṭi bha ra dhvā ji kā; likewise kutsa ku śi ki kā, phā bha śā laṅ ga ye ni kā.

From na ṭa, for dance, ñya.

It comes: the dance of actors is nā ṭyaṃ. Why is it said "for dance"? na ṭā ṇāṃ gṛ haṃ, the house of the actors.

From chando ga, auk thi ka, yā jñi ka and ba hi ba ca, for dharma, recitation and assembly.

It comes in these three senses for the meanings of chando ga and the rest, as an exception to the application of buñ. Dharma is conduct; recitation is the instruction handed down through the lineage of teachers. The group is the assembly. The dharma, recitation or assembly of the chanda gas is chan da gyaḥ; likewise auk tha gyaḥ, yā jñi gyaḥ, bī hrī cyaḥ. Why is it said "for dharma and the rest"? The house of the chando gas is chando gaṃ.

From ā thar ba ṇi ka, aṇ, with elision of ṭha too.

From the word ā thar ba ṇi ka aṇ comes, and there is elision too. The dharma, recitation or assembly of the followers of Atharvan is ā thar ba ṇaḥ.

From ca ra ṇa, buñ.

It comes for dharma and the rest. The dharma, recitation or assembly of the ka ṭhas is ka ṭha kaḥ; likewise pai bya lā da kaḥ, kā la pa kaḥ. Thus it is said:
"Of these, the first is ha ri dru,
then tum bu ru and cha ga lī,
and u la pa is the fourth:
these are called lā pa kaṃ."

From a gotra, for what is not a disciple of daṇ ḍa mā ṇa va.

From a stem ending in a gotra suffix, from a sixth-case ending, buñ comes for those things, the disciples of daṇ ḍa mā ṇa va excepted. What belongs to the son of Upagu is au pa ga ba kaṃ. Why is it said "not of daṇ ḍa mā ṇa va"? This son of Dakṣa is daṇ ḍa mā ṇa vaḥ, the disciple, or Dakṣa's. The youth for whom the staff is principal is daṇ ḍa mā ṇa va, from the compound of the type śā ka pār thi va. The word "disciple" is mentioned in order to include the disciples of disciples as well.

From rai ba ti ka and the rest, cha.

From gotra endings it comes when denoting property: rai ba ti kī ya, svā pi śī ya. The list: re ba ti kaḥ, svā pi śi, kṣau ma dha nvi, gau ra grī va, au da me ghi, au da bā pi. cha is mentioned to exclude the iñ ending and aṇ.

From kau pañ ji la and ha sti ba da, aṇ.

From these, from a gotra ending, aṇ comes when denoting property, as an exception to buñ for gotra. What belongs to kau piñ ja la is kau piñ ja laṃ; likewise hā sti pa daṃ. aṇ also comes when this same word is stated for the gotra. The descendant of ku biñ ja la is kau piñ ja laḥ; the descendant of Elephant-Foot is hā sti pa daḥ. Making it ṇit after the letter a is also for the sense of dī pa and for excluding a state as if born. Therefore kau piñ ja lī, a man of joy, is established.

For assembly, mark, chant and characteristic, aṇ from añ, yañ and iñ.

From the iñ ending and the yañ ending too, aṇ comes in the senses of assembly and the rest. The assembly of the bi das is bai daḥ, the assembly; bai daḥ, the mark; bai daḥ, the chant; bai daḥ, the characteristic. Likewise from yañ: gār gaḥ, the assembly, the mark or the chant; gār gaṃ, the characteristic. From iñ: rāk ṣaḥ, the assembly, the mark or the chant; dāk ṣaṃ, the characteristic. Why is it said "for assembly and the rest"? bī dā naṃ, the house. It is said precisely "from a gotra"; otherwise bā ta kī yaḥ. The difference between mark and characteristic is the distinction of not necessarily bestowing everything, of what is one's own, and of what is made with reference to oneself.

From śā ka la, buñ too.

From the word śā ka la, aṇ comes for assembly and the rest, and for property; by the word "too", buñ comes as well. One who knows or recites the Veda proclaimed by Śākalya is śā ka lāḥ. Their assembly is śā ka laḥ, śā ka la kaḥ; likewise for mark, chant and characteristic.

The Indian manuscript reads: from he stu ri ṭca bi hi, ṭi too.

From ba hi, from the ending of the word in ti, aṇ comes when denoting property, and from kṛ there is also the augment iṭ: saṃ boḍhuḥ, svaṃ sām ba hi traṃ, āg nī dhraṃ.

For a shelter.

From the word ag ni dha ending in kvip, when denoting a house, rañ comes by a set form. In the base there is no jaś. The shelter of ag nī dha is āg nī dhraṃ, the shelter.

From sa mi dha, for ā dhā na, ṣeṇya.

What is recited above all by this is ā dhā naṃ. From that, from mi dha, ṣeṇa comes. The mantra for the placing of the kindling is pā ni dhe nyeḥ, the mantra; sā mi dhe nī, the verse.

For a modification, aṇ.

A modification is like a changed state of the base. From the sixth-case ending of what denotes it, aṇ comes. The modification of stone is āś maḥ. Where prohibition was obtained by "for aṇ", the ṭi is elided by "modification of stone". Likewise mārt ti kaḥ, bhās ma naḥ. Where elision of ṭi was obtained by "from nas, taddhita", it is prohibited by "from an". It is said precisely "in the remaining sense": establishing aṇ once again is for the purpose of turning back the other remaining suffixes. Therefore ṭhak does not come by "hā la sai ra".

Also for parts of trees, herbs and animals.

aṇ also comes for parts of modifications of trees and the rest: kā rī raṃ, the arrow; kā rī raṃ, the ash. Likewise śaṃ śa paṃ, kha di raṃ. From herbs: maur baṃ, the arrow; maur baṃ, the ash.

Likewise mauṅ ga, gau dhu maṃ. By the word "tree", particular lords of the forest are also included. By the word "herb", creepers and the like are included too. From animals: kā po taṃ byañ ja naṃ, kā po taṃ śak thi. The words "also for parts of trees, herbs and animals" continue to apply up to the completion of the quarter. Therefore, for the trees, herbs, animals and the rest that are about to be explained, mayaṭ and the other suffixes should be understood to come for parts; the others are for modifications only.

From tā la and the rest.

From tā la and the rest, aṇ comes for modifications and parts. tā laṃ is the bow. Otherwise, ta la ma yaṭ. The list: peacock, dhi ṇa, indrā ya dha, cā pa, indra dṛ śa, śyā mā ka, bī yu kṣā, ga be dhu ka. Stating what was already established by the earlier rule is for the purpose of turning back mayaṭ.

From a word meaning gold, for a measure.

From a word meaning gold, when it has become a measure, aṇ comes for the modification, as an exception to mayaṭ. hā ṭa kaḥ, gold; likewise jā ta rū paṃ, kār ṣā pa ṇaṃ, tā pa ni yaṃ. Why is it said "for a measure"? yaṣ ṭiḥ: this is of the nature of gold.

From tā pu and ja ru, ṣuk.

It comes for a modification, after the stem: trā bu ṣaṃ. By this very establishment of ṣuk there is no aṇ. For that very reason there is no prohibition of mayaṭ: tra pu ma yaṃ, ja ru ma yaṃ.

mayaṭ optionally, when not food or clothing.

From the bare base, mayaṭ comes optionally for modifications and parts, food and clothing excepted: a śna ma yaṃ, bhas ma ma yaṃ, ka rī ra ma yaṃ, dur bā ma yaṃ, ka po ta ma yaṃ; or āś ma naṃ, bhās ma naṃ, kā rī raṃ, daur baṃ, kā po taṃ. Why is it said "not clothing and food"? mauṅ gaḥ, the pulse soup; kār bā saṃ, the cloth. For the modification, aṇ itself.

From śa ra and the rest, from vṛddha stems, and from stems of a single vowel.

From a sixth-case ending of śa ra and the rest, and from vṛddha stems and stems of a single vowel too, mayaṭ always comes for modifications and parts: śar ma yaṃ, darba ma yaṃ, ku ḍi ma yaṃ. The list: śa ra, da bha, ku ḍī, tṛ ṇa, so ma, ba lva ja. From a vṛddha stem: ā mra ma yaṃ. From a stem of a single vowel: bāṅ ga yaṃ, taṅ ma yaṃ.

From go and brī hi, for pu rī ṣa and pu ro ḍā śa.

mayaṭ comes. The dung of a cow is gom ma yaṃ; otherwise ga byaṃ. The pu ro ḍā śa cake is brī ni ma yaṃ; otherwise brai haḥ.

From ti la, ya va and piṣ ṭa, when not a name.

From ti la and the rest, mayaṭ comes for modifications and parts when it is not a name: til ma yaṃ, ya ba ma yaṃ, piṣ ṭa ma yaṃ. Why is it said "when not a name"? tai laṃ, yā ba kaṃ, baiṣ ṭa kaḥ, piṣ ṭa kā. yā ba kāṃ comes from the word ya ba, from the aṇ ending, with kan by the rule "from ya ba and the rest".

From a measure, as with "bought".

When a measure is denoted, the suffixes come for a modification just as for "bought". For example, what is bought with a gold coin is naṣ ki kaḥ; likewise the modification of a gold coin is naiṣ kaḥ. Likewise śa ta kiḥ, sā ha sraḥ, dri kaṃ saḥ, dri kaṃ saḥ, tri kaṃ saḥ, dri niṣ kaḥ, dri naiṣ ki kaḥ, dri baiṣ ṭi kaḥ. From a numeral, a year, and a numeral of measure that is not a name, the later member takes vṛddhi by the rule "śā ṇa ku le ja".

From śa mī, ṭlañ.

It comes for modifications and parts, as an exception to aṇ and mayaṭ: śa mī laṃ, the ash; śā mī lī, the ladle.

From uṣ ṭa, buñ.

It comes for modifications and parts: auṣ ṭa kaḥ.

From u ma and ur ṇa, optionally.

buñ comes for modifications and parts: aum kaṃ or au maṃ; au rṇa kaṃ or au rṇaṃ.

From a pa, ṇyat.

It comes optionally for a modification: ā pyaṃ, am ma yaṃ. It is stated because mayaṭ was always obtained by the rule "from śa ra and the rest, from vṛddha stems and from stems of a single vowel".

From ai ṇī and ko śa, ḍhañ.

It comes for modifications and parts: e ṇe yaṃ, kau śa yaṃ, as an exception to aṇ and mayaṭ. How then is it ai ṇa car ma? It comes from the masculine marker.

From piṣ ṭa, for a name, kan.

It comes: biṣ ṭa kaḥ, biṣ ṭa kī.

hai yaṃ ga bī naṃ.

It is a set form for a name and a modification: lightning. From go do ha with khañ, from the state of hi yaṅ gu, hai maṅ ga bi na: fresh butter, that is, butter ripened from yesterday.

From go and pa yas, yat.

It comes for modifications and parts: ga byaṃ, pa ya syaṃ. In every case, whatever holds for the consequence in gor ja and the rest should be established in the domain of mayaṭ too.

From dru.

yat comes for modifications and parts: dra byaṃ.

For a measure, ya.

For the word dru, the suffix ya comes for a modification that is a measure: dru ba yaṃ, a measure.

From kaṃ sī ya and pa ra bya, yañ and añ, with elision of cha and yat too.

From ki śī ya and ba ra śa bya respectively, yañ and añ come, and cha and yat are elided too. What serves the vessel is kāṃ sī yaṃ; the modification of kāṃ sī ya is kāṃ syī. What serves the axe is pā ra śa bya; its modification is pā ra śa baḥ.

For these, one.

When, after a stem ending in a suffix of modification or part, a suffix of modification or part would again be obtained, a restriction is made: for the modification of a modification, and for the part of a part, the suffix comes once only, not a second or a third time. The modification or part of the deodar is dai ba dā ra paṃ; that of kā bit tha is kā pit thaṃ. Its modification or part in turn is dai ba dā ra kaṃ, kā pit tha. How does a single suffix of modification or part, which arose for the base alone, also denote the modification of a modification and the part of a part? Because the modification of a modification is also connected with the root base, and the part also has the root as its whole. For example, the juice is the modification of the sugarcane, just as molasses and sugar crystals are well known to be. Likewise one speaks of Devadatta's arm, hand, finger and joint. Then how is it drau ba yaṃ? Because a word expressing a particular has no power to denote it. When one wishes to express "its property", it comes too.

For a fruit, elision.

The suffix that arose for a modification or part is elided for a fruit. The modification, part or fruit of the emblic myrobalan is ā ma la kaṃ; likewise ku ba laṃ, ba da raṃ. It is the fruit, and a part, of the fruit-bearing tree, just as a shoot is of a plant with twigs. Some say that up to the base, aṇ itself applies to the fruit.

From plakṣa and the rest, aṇ.

aṇ comes when one wishes to express fruit, modification or part; by the force of this establishment it is not elided. pal la kṣaṃ, nai ya gro dhaṃ. The list: pa lakṣa, nya gro dha, iṅ pu dī, ri gu, a śvat tha, ka ka ru, bri ha tī.

From jam bu, optionally.

aṇ comes for the fruit, and elision too: jam ba baṃ, the fruit.

Or elision of jam bu too.

The aṇ made for the fruit from jam bu is optionally elided; this is a particular kind of thing resembling the base. jam buḥ, the jam bu fruit; or jam pa baṃ, the fruit.

From ha ra ti kī and the rest.

There is elision for the fruit: ha rī ta kyāḥ, the fruits; likewise ha rī ta kī, ko śā ta kī. Here it is a thing resembling the gender of the base, and it is not wanted in the word. Hence ha rī ta kyāḥ, the fruits; ha rī ta kī, ko śā ta kī. The list: na ma kha rñja nāḥ, śaṣ kaṇ ḍī, nan drī nā ḍī, dā hī dro tī, śve ta pā kī, ar ju na bā ki, kāl la, dra kṣā, brā kṣā, garg ga ri kā, kaṇ ḍa kā ri kā, śe pha li kā, pha la pā kā.

From what dries up.

There is elision for the fruit: brī ha yaḥ, ya bāḥ, mā ṣāḥ, mu ṣāḥ, muṅ gāḥ.

From flowers and roots, variously.

For these, the suffix of modification or part is elided. The flower of mal li kī is mal li ka; likewise jā tī. The root of bi dā ra is bi dā rī; likewise aṃ śu ma tī, bṛ ha tī. It also fails to occur: pā ṭa lā ni, the flowers; śā lvā ni, the roots. In some cases there are others too: ka dam pa buṣ paṃ, ka rī raṃ, a śo kaṃ.

From a di ti, ya ma, de ti and a ti tya, for the earlier senses, ṇya.

From a di ti and the rest, it comes in the senses stated earlier, as an exception to aṇ and the rest. The descendant of a di ti, or her property, or what she proclaimed, or that of which she is the deity: in all cases a di tyaḥ. Likewise yā myaḥ, de tyaḥ, ā di tyaḥ. How then is it dai tya yaḥ? ḍhak comes from the ending of dī ba.

From what ends in pa ti, not preceded by a śva and the rest, for the earlier senses.

From a stem ending in the word pa ti, not preceded by a śva and the rest, ṇya comes in the earlier senses: bār ta spa tyaṃ, brā jā ba tyaṃ. Why is it said "not preceded by a śva and the rest"? The descendant of Aśvapati is ā śva pa taḥ; likewise ā dhi pa taḥ. The list: ā śva, śa ta, dha na, rāṣ ṭa, ku la, gṛ ha, pa śu, dhā nya, dha nyaḥ, sa bhā, a dhi, se nā, kṣe tra.

From sthā man, a.

From the ending of the word stha ma, aṇ comes in the earlier senses. The hair of this one is u ḍu lo maḥ; his descendants are u ḍu lo māḥ; likewise kha ra lo maḥ. Why the word "plural"? au ḍu lo miḥ: iñ, because it is in the class beginning with ba hu.

From pṛ thi bī, ña and aṇ.

They come in the senses stated earlier: pār thi bāḥ, pār tha bāḥ, pār thī bī.

For ut sa and the rest, añ.

It comes in the earlier senses: aut saḥ, o da pa naḥ. The list: ut sa, a da pā na, bi na da, ma hā na da, ma hā na sa, ma hā prā ṇa, ta ru ṇa, ta la na, and from bas ka ya when there is no compound. In a compound, the descendant of su bas ka ya is su bas ka yiḥ. Further: dhe nuḥ, prak taṃ, ja ga ti, tri rṇu bha, an ṣṭu bha, ja na pa da, bha ra ta, u śī na ra, grīṣ ma, pī lu, ku ṇa, and from u da sthā na for a country. The country drawn from u da sthā na is au da sthā naḥ; otherwise, from the name u da sthā na for a descendant, au da sthā niḥ. Further: bṛ ṣa, bhal la kī ṣā, ra than ta ra, ma dyan ti na, bṛ ha ta, ma ha ta, ma hi ma na, sa tva ta, Kuru pāñ cā la, indra, a ba sā na, uṣ ṇi ha, ka ku bha, su barṇa.

From de ba, yañ too.

From the word de bi, yañ comes in the earlier senses; by the word "too", añ comes as well: dai byaḥ, dai baḥ.

From ba hi sa, ṭī kak too.

It comes in the earlier senses; by the word "too", yañ comes as well. The state of being outside is bā hā kiḥ, the outsider.

From a dvigu, when not for a descendant, elision of ya, ac and the rest.

From a word connected with its object by reason of a dvigu compound, apart from the descendant, the ya and the like made for the earlier senses, and ac and the rest too, are elided. The property of two chariots is dvir thaṃ; likewise tri ra thaṃ. What is prepared on five kapālas is pañ ca ka pā laḥ, the pu ro ḍā śa cake. One who recites or knows two Vedas is dvi be deḥ. Why is it said "from a dvigu"? What arose in the eastern hall is paur ba śā la. It does not come here, from understanding the reason for a dvigu: the confluence of five rivers is pañ ca nā daṃ; what arises from it is pañ ca na daḥ. Why is it said "not for a descendant"? The descendant of two mothers is dvai mā ru raḥ. Why is it said "ya, ac and the rest"? What comes from five gargas is pañ ca garga ru syā. It is said precisely "for the earlier senses"; otherwise one who crosses with two jars is dvai ka la śi kaḥ. Before ji ta, the seven procedures of aṇ and the rest have been explained.

When there is no word ba ti, ḍhak comes from ag ni and ki la for the senses explained earlier and those to be explained. The descendant of fire is āg na yaḥ; likewise kā le yaḥ. What resembles fire in appearance is āg ne yaṃ. That whose deity is fire is ag ne yaḥ. The property of the fire god is ag ne yaṃ; likewise kā le yaṃ. What has fire as its cause, or possesses it, or is born of it, is āg ne yaḥ; likewise kā ye yaḥ. Why is it said "from ba ti"? ag ni bat, ka bat.

From stri and puns, haña and snañ.

Respectively, from what lacks ba ti, they come in the earlier senses, both those already explained and those to be explained: strai ṇaṃ, pauṃ saṃ. It is said precisely "from ba ti"; otherwise stri bat, pum ba ta.

For a state, optionally.

They come for stri and pums: strai ṇaṃ, pauṃs naṃ, or strī tvaṃ.

From go, before ac, yat.

From the word go, when a taddhita beginning with a vowel would follow, yat comes. This one's cow is ga bya. That whose deity is the cow is ga byaṃ. Why is it said "before ac"? gau rā ga taḥ, go rū pyaḥ.

Thus the four, ḍhak and the rest, after ba ti, have been explained.

Here ends the commentary on the fourth chapter of the Eight Thousand, the work on Sādhukīrti's Mañjuśrī Grammar composed by the master Rājadeva, lord of glorious Kaliṅga, master of the land of the bodhisattva.

Homage to the Buddha.

From yat, ṭhak.

For whatever sense lies above the proclamation of yat, ṭhak comes; it should be understood as a general rule.

From "has subdued, has won, digs".

ṭhak comes optionally from an instrument in the sense of winning and the rest. One who has subdued, or has won, or plays with dice is āk ṣi kaḥ; likewise śā lā ki kaḥ. One who digs with a hoe is khā ni tri kaḥ; likewise kaud dha li kaḥ. Why is it said "from an instrument"? de ba datta na ji taṃ. By the explanation of the arrangement, in some cases the word itself is used.

aṅgu lyā kha na ti.

In the sense of "prepared."

In that sense ṭhak comes. What is prepared with curd is dā dhi kaṃ; mā rī ci kaṃ. The splitting of the rule is for the sake of the rule that follows.

From ku lattha and from a stem whose penultimate is k, aṇ.

After ku lattha, and also after a stem whose penultimate letter is k, aṇ comes in the sense of "prepared." What is prepared with ku lattha is kau latthaṃ. Food well prepared with tinti ḍi ka is tainti ḍi kaṃ. Although aṇ is already established by the rule "in the remaining senses," it is mentioned again in order to block the rule that would exclude it.

"Crosses."

ṭhak comes after the word that expresses the means. One who crosses by u ḍu pa is au ḍu pa kaḥ; gau pucchi kaḥ. If one asks how ā śva me dhi ka is formed: it is said of the killing of a brahmin, for here ta ra ti is meant in the sense of merely getting past it.

From nau and from a stem of two vowels, ṭhan.

After the word na u, and after a stem having two vowels, when these express the means, ṭhan comes in the sense of crossing. One who crosses by boat is nā bi kaḥ; gha ṭi kaḥ; pal bi kaḥ; bā hu kaḥ; tī rthi kaḥ. For a woman: nā bi kra, tīrthi kā.

"Goes."

ṭhak comes after the means in what is expressed. "Goes" applies to eating and to going. One who eats with curd is dā dhi kaḥ; śaṅga be ri kaḥ. One who goes by horse is a śvi kiḥ. It says precisely "from the means (ka ra ṇa)," and so we have only sa ha pu tre ṇac ra ti. If one asks how then ghā ṭi kaḥ is formed: it comes about because the sense of the means is intended to be expressed.

From parba and the rest, ṣṭhan.

It comes after the means in the sense of going: parbi kaḥ, parbi kīḥ; a śvi kāṃ, a śvi kī; a ka ṣi kaḥ, a ka ṣi kī. What is called ā ka ṣa is the word for the stone and the rubbing slab used to test gold. One who goes on foot is ba dhi kaḥ, pa di kī; by the rule hi ma ha ti kā śaṣṭhi na la pa da it is the substitute in place of da na. parbba, a śva, a śvattha, ra tha, jā la, byā sa, byu la, pā da, ā ka ṣa.

Optionally from śa ga ṇa.

In the sense of going, ṣṭhan comes optionally: śva ga ṇi kaḥ, śvā ga ṇi kī; śvā ga ṇiḥ, śva ga ṇi kā.

From be ta na and the rest, "lives by."

ṭhak comes after the means. One who lives by be ta na is bai ta ni kaḥ; bā hi kaḥ. be ta naḥ, bā ha, ardha bā ha, bai śa, u pa be śa, pre ṣa ṇa, su kha, śayyā, u pa de śa, u pa bā ha, u pa hasta, u pa sthā na, u pa sthī, bā da, barttā, śakti, u pa ni ṣad, sphi ka, jā la, bā la, dha nu and daṇḍa, and the compound with daṇḍa, in order to include it: dha nuṣkaḥ, daṇḍi kaḥ, dha nu rdāṇḍi kaḥ.

From basna, kra ya and bi kra ya, ṭhan.

It comes in this sense of living by that. One who lives by basna is basni ka; kra yi ka; bi kra yi kaḥ; kra yaḥ.

From ā yu dha, cha as well.

After the word ā yu dha cha comes; and because of the word "as well," ṭhan. One who lives by weapons is ā yu dhi kaḥ, ā yu dhī yaḥ.

From brā ta, khañ.

Those groups of various castes, of unsettled conduct, who live by bodily feats, are brā ta; what is connected with them is also bra ta. In the sense of living by that, khañ comes. One who lives by observance is brā ti'i naḥ.

In the sense of "carries," from utsaṃ ga and the rest.

After utsaṃ and the rest, as the means, ṭhak comes in the sense of carrying. One who carries on the lap is autsaṃ gi kaḥ; au ḍu pi kaḥ; bha ri kaḥ; nā bi kaḥ. utsaṅga, u ḍu pa, bhā ra nau, utta pa, utpu ḍa pi ṭa ka, piṭā kaḥ.

From bhasta and the rest, ṣṭhan.

It comes after the means in the sense of carrying. One who carries by bhastra is bhastri kī; bhasti kī; bha ra ṭi ka, bha ra ḍi kī. bhastrā, bha ra ṭa, bha ra ṇa, śīrṣa bhā ra, aṃ śabhā ra.

Optionally from bi ba bha and bī ba dha.

After a word with the third case ending, ṣṭhan comes in the sense of carrying; in the alternative, ṭhak. One who carries by bi badha is bi ba dhi kaḥ, bi ba dhi kī; bai ba dha kiḥ, bai ba dhi kī. The words bi ba dha and bai ba dha are alike in meaning and are used for gathering together.

From ku ṭi la kā, aṇ.

It comes after the means in the sense of carrying off. What draws off the hunter by a crooked gait is kau kri li kaḥ: a deer. kau ṭi li kī: a courtesan.

From akṣa dyu ta, in the sense of "brought about."

ṭhak comes after the means. What is brought about by playing at dice is ākṣā dyu ti kaṃ: enmity; jaṃ ghā prā ki ti kaṃ. akṣa dyu ta, jaṃ ghā pra hri ta, prā da se da na, ka ṇṭa, ka marṅa na, ga tā pa ga ta, ga mā ga ma, yā tā yā ta.

From ktri and from a bhāva suffix, imap.

After a stem ending in the suffix ktri, and after one ending in the bhāva suffix, imap comes in the sense of "accomplished": kri ti maṃ, baktri maṃ, hi tri maṃ. What is cooked is pā ki maṃ; si ki maṃ, kuṭṭi maṃ. Because ktri is formed to express the bare action (bhāva), it would already be established by the words "from a bhāva suffix"; taking it up separately serves to make the rule obligatory.

From a pa mi tya and ya ci ta, kak and kan.

After the word a pa mi tya and the word yi ca ta, ending in the bhāva suffix kta, kak and kan come respectively in the sense of "obtained." What is obtained by a pa mi tya is ā pa mi tya kaṃ: wealth. What is obtained by yā ci ta is ya ci ta ka: an ornament.

In the sense of "mixed with that."

After the means, in the sense of "made," the words "with that" mean after the third case ending: ṭhak comes. What is mixed with curd is dha dhi kaṃ; bai pba li kaṃ. "Mixed" means made to blend. This formation does not apply to mere being together, and so it does not occur here: sri pe na saṃ sriṣṭa, a vessel.

From curṇa and mudga, ini and aṇ.

They come in the sense of "mixed." What is thoroughly mixed with flours is cu rṇinaḥ a pū pāḥ; mauṅgaḥ, a food.

From la ba ṇā, elision.

The ṭhak made in the sense of "mixed" is elided: la ba ṇa, a vegetable dish; la ba ṇā, a soup.

From au jas, sa has and ambhas, "proceeds."

After these, with the third case ending, ṭhak comes in the sense "proceeds." One who proceeds by vigor is au ja si kaḥ: a hero. sṅa ha si kaḥ: a robber. āmbha si kaḥ: a fish.

From i pa, lo ma and ku la with pra ti and a nu, from aṃ.

After i pa, lo ma and ku la preceded by pra ti and ā nu, having the second case ending, ṭhak comes in the sense "proceeds." One who proceeds pra tī pa is pra tī bi kaḥ; a nrī pi kaḥ; pra ti lo mi kāḥ, a nu lo mi kraḥ; pra ti ku li kaḥ, ā nu kū li kaḥ. It is the object (karman) precisely because it qualifies the action.

From mu kha and par śa after pra ri.

After these, with the second case ending, ṭhak comes in this sense, "proceeds." mu khaṃ mu khaṃ pa ri pa ri mu khaṃ; likewise pa ri pā raśvaṃ. This is by the rule for indeclinables, according to the meaning. One who proceeds facing all around is pā ri mu khi kaḥ. The meaning is: whatever direction the master faces, in that direction he proceeds. Likewise pā ri pārśva kaḥ.

rakṣa ti and añcha ti.

In these senses ṭhak comes after the second case ending. One who guards the assembly is sā mā ji kaḥ; sānni be śi kaḥ. As for gleaning jujubes, ucchi no ti: bā da ri kaḥ; śā ki kaḥ.

From śabda and da rda, "makes."

After these, ṭhak comes in the sense "makes." For "makes sound": śābdi kaḥ. The sound of words is meant, not mere sound. dārad ri ka: ku lā laḥ, a potter.

From pakṣi, matsya and mṛ ga, in the sense of "kills."

After pakṣi and the rest, with the second case ending, ṭhak comes in this sense, "kills": bākṣa kaḥ, mātsyi kaḥ, mārghi kaḥ. Because the meaning is what matters, it also comes after the synonyms of the words specified: śā ku ni kaḥ, śā pha ri kaḥ, ha ro ṇi kaḥ, śau ka ri kaḥ. If one asks why it does not come here, in a jihma hanti and a ni miṣān hanti: because there is no such usage. a jihmā is a kind of wild animal; a ni mi khā are fish.

From pa ri pantha, also in the sense of "stays."

After the word pa ri pantha, ṭhak comes in these senses, "kills" and "stays." One who stays on, or kills on, the pa ri pantha is pā ri pantha kaḥ. A road is bantha. By the rule in the enumeration of the arrangement, "with ba hi too, with the fifth case," it is an indeclinable formation. This very word is turned into pantha by special listing.

From what ends in "path," from a nu pa of pada, and from ākranda, in the sense of "runs": in this sense ṭhak comes after the second case ending.

A path straight as a staff is ma tha, a daṇḍa path. One who runs on it is dāṇḍa mā thi kaḥ; śaulka ma thi kaḥ; pa da bī kaḥ. ba daṃ means foot; "following" it is a nu pa dam, or ga ta. One who runs a nu pa dam is ā nu bā di kaḥ. ākrandanti is here turned into ā kranad. When someone tormented by suffering makes it known, that is crying out, ā kranda: one fit to give refuge. One who runs there is ā kranda kaḥ.

From what ends in pa da, and from pra ti kaṇṭha, ārtha and la lā ma, "takes."

After pa da and utta ra ba da with the second case ending, and after pa ti kaṇṭha and the rest, ṭhak comes in the sense of taking: paurba ba tik, autta ra pa ba di kaḥ, prā ti kaṇṭhi kaḥ, ārthi kaḥ, la lā mi kaḥ. kaṇṭhaṃ kaṇṭhaṃ kaṇṭhaṃ, pra ti pra ti kaṇṭhaṃ: this is by the rule for indeclinables, according to the meaning. Or else it is by the rule "a bhi and prā ti with a characteristic." la lā maṃ is a mark: a banner and a necklace.

In the sense of the blameworthy: "takes."

After a word with the second ending, ṭhak comes: dvai gu ṇi kaḥ, trai gu ṇi kaḥ. One who, having lent out the principal, takes back from it twofold or threefold is a malla, that is, blameworthy; for taking such a return is forbidden in the treatises.

From bṛdhi, from bṛ dha ṣa.

ṭhak is applied. One who takes increase is bārdhu ṣikaḥ. Stating the formation with the word bṛdhu ṣi serves to prevent biddhi from being heard.

From de śai ka da śa and ku śī ra, ṭhaṭ.

After these, ṭhaṭ comes in the sense "takes what is blameworthy." de śai ka da śa expresses the sense of eleven; it is correct by special listing. da śaikā da śi kaḥ. kau śi kā and śa kaḥ. kau śī di kaḥ. A loan grown excessively, whose last sum cannot be repaid, is ku śi da; the very making of such a loan is called kur śī da. One who takes it is a malla, that is, blameworthy.

From dharma and a dharma, "practices."

ṭhak comes after the designating word with the second case ending. One who, by practicing the Dharma, follows it, is dhārmi kaḥ; ā dharmi kaḥ.

From pra ti pa, in the sense of ya ta, ṭhan as well.

ṭhak comes, and because of the word "as well," ṭhan too. For "road by road": prā ti pa ṭhi kaḥ, prā ti pa ṭhi kaḥ.

In the sense of an assembly, in the sense "joins," ṭhak comes after the second case ending. For "joins the assembly": sā mā ji kaḥ, sā mā bā yikaḥ, su mu hi kaḥ, sa mu dā ya kaḥ. "Joins" means that one becomes a part of it.

From pa ri ṣa da, ṇya.

It comes in the sense "joins." For "joins the assembly": pā ri ṣa dyaḥ. By the principle that what is altered in one part is as if it were not other, it comes also after parṣad: pārṣa dyaḥ.

Optionally from se nā.

After the word se na, ṇya comes optionally in the sense "joins": se nyaḥ, se ni kaḥ, se ni kaḥ. se nya takes ṣyañ because it belongs to cā rurbarṇya and the rest.

lā lā ḍi ka and kokku ṭi ka.

These are formed by special listing. One who looks at the forehead is lā lā ḍi kaḥ: said of one who, when he has not attended in time to the work of the master he serves, watches closely to note the sign of anger showing on the forehead itself. One who looks as a fowl does is kaukku ḍī kaḥ. The word kakku ṭī denotes the spot marked by a fowl's foot. The monk who, undistracted, walks with his eyes well restrained toward the spot where his foot will fall is kaukku ṭi kaḥ. Those who are not of that nature but present themselves as such are said to be of hypocritical conduct, or kukku ṭī. One who behaves in that way is kaukku ṭa kaḥ. These are obtained by special listing of a group of meanings.

From par dā ra, su sna ta, mā śabda and the rest, in the senses "goes," "asks" and "says."

The words "and the rest" are joined to each one. After these, with the second case ending, ṭhak comes in each sense respectively. One who goes to another's wife is pā ra dā ri kaḥ. Besides this, only the taking of the teacher's wife is mentioned, and nothing else: pa ra dā ra, gu ru talba. One who asks whether one has bathed well is sau snā ti kaḥ; this denotes only one who utters auspicious words, not others. sau khar rā tri kaḥ, sau kha śa yyi kaḥ. su sna ta su kha rī tri, su kha śiyya. One who says the word mā is mā śabdi kaḥ. One who speaks of permanent sound is kārya śabdi kaḥ. That such words, or the formation with ṭhak, are included comes from their very recital in the group. One who says much is pā bhū ti kaḥ. What expresses the conduct of the naked ascetics is prabhu taṃ. As a qualifier of the action: paryā bhi kaḥ. mā śabda, nitya, śabda, kārya śabda, pra bhū ta, paryāpta.

From ṅas, in the sense of custom.

Here "custom" is meant as conduct; this heading extends as far as the rule on what is proper to the custom endowed with it.

When that is expressed, "from ṅas" means that ṭhak comes after the sixth case ending. The custom of the collectors' enclosure is śaulka śā li kaṃ; ā pa ṇi ka; ā ta ri kaṃ; gaulli kaṃ.

From ri and from ma hiṣa and the rest, aṇ.

After a word ending in the letter ṛ, and also after ma hi ṣā and the rest, aṇ comes in the sense of custom. The custom of the fire-offering priest is hau traṃ; au da gā taṃ; mā hi ṣaṃ; prā jā pa tam. mā hi ṣī, pra jā ba tī, bi li pi kā, ā nu le pi kā, pu ro hi tā, ma ṇi pā lī, a nu cā ra kā, ya ja mān, na ra. Although aṇ is already established by the word ni, the reading of the word na ra serves to prevent ṭhak.

In the case of bai śastri and bai bhra ja ṭi.

After bai śastri and bai bhā ji tṛ, in the sense of custom, aṇ comes, and the iṭ is also elided, by special listing: bai śastraṃ, baibhā ja tra ma.

In the sense of a ba krī.

That by which one buys, that is, holds, for a fixed term, what is collected by the power of exchange: when this is expressed, ṭhak comes after the sixth case ending. The rent of the customs enclosure is śaulka śā li kaḥ; gaulli kaḥ. For it is possible to hold it even while oppressing the world. This is stated for what is not obtained by "from ṅas, in the sense of custom."

From su, in the sense of wares: "his."

"From su" means that after the first case ending, when wares are expressed, ṭhak comes in the sense "his." One whose wares are a pū pa is a pū pi kaḥ; śaṣku li kaḥ; lā ba ṇi kaḥ; mā li kaḥ; śauṇḍi kaḥ; śau lī kaḥ. "Wares" is said of what is to be sold.

From śi śar and the rest, ṣṭhan.

After words for wares, it comes after the first case ending in the sense "his." One whose wares are śa ra is ki śa ri kaḥ, ki śa ri kī; nal dikaḥ, na la di kī. ke śa ra, na la da, stha ga ta, ta ga ta, guggu lu, u śrī ra, ha ri drā, ha ri drū parṇī ba da ra. ki śar and the rest denote fragrant substances.

Optionally from śa lā lu.

After a word for wares, after the first case ending, in the sense of the sixth, ṣṭhan comes optionally. One whose wares are śa lā lu is śa lā lu kaḥ. The word śa lā lu is a fragrant substance.

From the object of a craft.

After the first case ending, ṭhak comes in the sense "his." One whose drum is the object of his craft is mārdaṃ gi kaḥ; bai ṇa bi kaḥ; pāṇ bi kaḥ. A particular knowledge of how to perform music on drums and the like is kau śa laṃ: that is what "craft" denotes. And the action alone is the chief object. Likewise, because of the relation of object and subject, drums and the like themselves are taken as the object. For that very reason the suffix that arises serves to show the designation of the craftsman who lives by that craft.

From maḍḍu ka ddharjha ra, aṇ.

After the first case ending, for the object of a craft, aṇ comes optionally in the sense "his": mūḍḍu ki kāḥ, jhārjha ri kaḥ; māḍḍu kaḥ, jhārjha raḥ.

From words for weapons.

After the first case ending, ṭhak comes in the sense of the sixth. One whose weapon is a sword is ā sī kaḥ; cā kraṃ kaḥ; dhā nuṣkaḥ; pā ra śva ddhi kaḥ.

From śakti and yaṣṭi, īkak.

It comes. One whose weapon is a spear is śāktī kiḥ, śāktī kiḥ; yāṣṭī kāḥ, yāṣṭā kiḥ.

From asti, nāsti and daṣṭa, in the sense "holds."

After āsti and the rest, ṭhak comes in this sense, "he holds it." One who holds that things exist is nāsti kaḥ; nāsti kaḥ. Fortune is the virtue and non-virtue done before; one who holds to it is daiṣṭe kaḥ. It is said to be empty of any other agency of gods or men. Nor is this holding one that falls to an extreme with regard to valid cognition, for the word deṣṭa is taught in the sense of valid cognition.

From words for disposition.

After a word for the object of a disposition, after the first case ending, ṭhak comes in this sense, "this is his." One whose disposition is toward pū pa is ā pū pi kaḥ; pā ru ṣi kaḥ, ā kro śi kaḥ, kā ru ṇikaḥ. Disposition is constant adherence out of regard for gaining the result.

From ccha tra and the rest, aṇ.

After words for dispositions it comes in the sense "his." One whose disposition is like a parasol has the nature of a parasol: ccha traḥ. Just as a parasol protects from heat and the like and becomes a cause of comfort, so a disciple who covers the faults of his teacher and becomes a cause of his comfort has the disposition of a parasol. Likewise śaikṣaḥ, ta pa saḥ, cau raḥ. Others say that here the suffix is ṇa. ccha tra, śakṣī, bhikṣa, dhukṣā, pu rod cur, ud sthā na, kṛ karma, bi śvadhā, ta pas, satya, a nṛ ta, bi śi ka, sthā. The word sthā here is to be taken as the end of a stem preceded by anta, prā and the rest; hence anta thā, u pasthā, su sthā.

From e kā nya and the rest, as an action, and from dha yan bṛtta.

After e kā nya and the rest, as an action, after the first case ending, ṭhak comes in this sense, "his": that is, if e kā nya and the rest apply in the domain of a dhya ya n. One to whom e ka nyat, "one other action," applies in recitation, a mistaken form, an instance of not reading correctly, is e kā nya kiḥ; dri ya nyi kaḥ, e ka granthi kaḥ, e ka rū pi kaḥ. At the time of examination it becomes another action relative to correct reading; the mistaken form itself, which is a not reading, is obtained from the force of usage. If one asks why "in recitation (adhya yan)" is said: it is because of e ka ma nyat said of going astray from this.

From a prior member having many vowels, ṭhan.

After enya and the rest, with a prior member having many vowels, ṭhan comes, if it applies as expressing an action. One to whom twelve other errors apply in recitation is dvā di śā nyi kaḥ; tra yod śānyi kaḥ.

From bhakṣya, in the sense of hi ta.

After the first case ending, ṭhak comes in the sense "his." One for whom pū pa is beneficial is ā pū pi kaḥ; śāṣku li kaḥ.

From what is to be given, in the senses of fixed appointment and full bestowal.

After a word for what is to be given, after the first case ending, ṭhak comes when what is expressed is the understanding of fixed appointment and full bestowal. One for whom the giving of choice food is fixed is ā gra bho ja ni kaḥ; prā tha ma pha li kaḥ, a pū phi kaḥ, śā ṇi kaḥ, maṃ saud ni kaḥ. "Fixed appointment" is the state of being fixed, and that is a qualification of what is to be given.

From od na, ṭhaṭ.

It comes. One for whom the giving of food is fixed is śaulka śā li kaḥ; dau bā ri kaḥ. "Fixed appointment" is said of making this something special.

From a word ending in ā gā ra, ṭhan.

After the seventh case ending it comes in the sense of fixed appointment: de bā gā ri kaḥ, koṣṭha ga ri kaḥ.

From what is a place, in the sense of reciting.

When an improper place or time is expressed, after the seventh case ending, ṭhak comes when reciting is expressed. One who recites in a charnel ground is śam śa ni kaḥ; cā ruṣpa ṭhi kā. From an improper time: cā turad śi kaḥ, ā mā bā syi kaḥ. If one asks why "from an improper place or time" is said: śrughne'ddhī te, pūrbā hṇe'dhī te. Here the places and times at which recitation is forbidden are what is meant by an improper place and time, just as with what must not be eaten and what must not be learned. As it is said:

"One should not recite in a charnel ground,
nor likewise at the village's edge, nor in a cattle pen,
nor while mounted on a tree or a horse,
nor on a donkey, a camel or an elephant.
The eighth day destroys the preceptor;
the fourteenth destroys the student;
the fifteenth destroys learning;
the first day of the month destroys everything.
Therefore avoid them altogether.
Whoever, when the time has been proclaimed,
recites learning at a wrong time
ruins those four: life, learning,
fame and wealth."

This and more appears at length.

From what ends in ka ṭhi na, and from prastār and saṃ sthā na: "deals."

After ka ṭhi na with the seventh case ending, and after prastā ra and saṃ sthā na, ṭhak comes in the sense "deals." One who deals in hard bamboo is pāṃ śa ka ṭhi na kaḥ; cā kra thi ni kaḥ: this designates the ba ru ḍa and the like. prā stā ri kaḥ, sāṃ sthā nī kaḥ.

From ni ka ṭa and the rest: "dwells," in an observance.

After ni kaṭa and the rest, ṭhak comes in the sense of dwelling so as to fulfill an observance. One who dwells in a ni ka ṭa is nai ka ṭi kaḥ. The treatises say that a hermitage is a place to dwell at the distance of an earshot from the village; a monk who conducts himself in this way is designated nai ka ḍi ka. śmā śā ni kaḥ, bārkā mū li kaḥ, ā bya ba kā śi kaḥ, ā bas thi kaḥ; a ba sa thi ka is another form. na kaṭa, śmā śā na, bṛkṣa mū la, a bhya ba kā śa; a ba sa tha completes the forms ending in ṭhak.

Above hi ta, yat.

For whatever sense lies above the proclamation of hi ta, yat comes; this should be understood.

From tīrtha preceded by "same."

In the sense of dwelling, yat comes after the seventh case ending. One who dwells in the same ford is sa tīrthyaḥ: one who has the same teacher. "Same" refers to the side and so on, and its being such. Because, like a riverbank, it is a cause of purification, the ford is the teacher; it denotes the master.

From u da ra, with u, in the sense of cart.

After u da ra preceded by "same," yat comes in the sense of u and cart. One who lay in the same belly is samā no daryaḥ, so daryaḥ. By the option given in the rule "optionally before name, family, belly, form, dharma, place, color, age and lineage," sa mā na is changed to sa.

From yu ga and prā saṅga, from aṃ: "makes."

"From aṃ" means that after the second case ending, after yug and prā saṅga, yat comes in the sense "makes." One who makes a yoke-pair is yu gyaḥ; prā saṅgyaḥ. yu gaṃ is the wood placed on the shoulders for drawing a cart and the like. prā saṅga is the binding of the neck for taming. Therefore it does not occur here: yu gam ba ha ti, that is, together with the king. Or else it means a dispute, and something else: going without hindrance is niprā saṅgaḥ, and it means one who does that.

From ra and from what ends in it.

After the second case ending, yat comes in the sense "makes." One who rides a chariot is rathyaḥ; dvir thyaḥ; par ra ma ra thyaḥ.

From dhu ru, ḍhak as well.

It comes after the second case ending in the sense "makes." Because of the word "as well," yat comes too. One who exerts himself is dhu re yaḥ, dhu ryaḥ. If one asks how the feminine dhu rī comes about: because it belongs to go ra and the rest.

From sarba, utta ra, dakṣa ṇa and pūrba, kha.

After dhu ra preceded by sarba and the rest, kha comes. From "exerts himself in everything": sarba dhu rī ṇāḥ; dakṣi ṇa dhu rī ṇaḥ; pū radhu rī ṇaḥ.

From one having e ka first, optionally elision.

After dhu ra with e ka first, kha comes in the sense "makes," and optionally it is elided. For "exerts himself in one thing": e ka dhu rī ṇiḥ, e ka dhu raḥ.

From śa ka ṭa, aṇ.

In the sense "makes," aṇ comes after the second case ending: śākaṭaḥ.

From ha si ra, ṭhak.

In the sense "makes," ṭhak comes after the second case ending: hā li kaḥ, sai ri kaḥ.

In a name, from jinā.

In a name, in the sense "brings about a womb," after the word jinā, yat comes when girls are expressed; the plural too is by special listing. The word ja nī also means the state of being a ja nī: for "makes ja nī or ja na niṃ," ja nyaḥ. It denotes the friends and servants of a new bride. Since that very word would there be turned into ja mā tri, it is done in this way.

dhe nu ṣyā and gārda pa tya.

In a name, by special listing: a cow or a buffalo that stays without being tied is dha nu ṣyā. Yat and ṣuk are by special listing. What is joined with the householder is gārad pa tyaḥ: a particular fire. By special listing, it denotes the householder who performs sacrifice.

In the sense "pierces," from what is not the instrument.

After the second case ending, yat comes in the sense of piercing. What pierces the feet is pā dyāḥ: gravel. u ra syeḥ: thorns. If one asks why "not by the instrument" is said: ri suṃ bi dhya ti śasgyu, cośaktyā; cau rā na bi dhya ti dha nu ṣā.

From dhan and gaṇ, in the sense "obtains."

After the object of the second case, yat comes in the sense of piercing. One who obtains wealth is dha nyaḥ; gaṇyaḥ. If one asks why "obtains" is said: tṛ naṃ, water.

From anna, ṇa.

After the second case ending it comes in the sense "obtains." For "obtains food": ānnaḥ.

From ba śa, in the sense "goes."

After ba śa with the second case ending, yat comes in the sense of going. One who has gone under control is baśyaḥ. The meaning is: in order to accomplish the winning of the desired company of women in all births.

From su, from pa da, in the sense "the place where something is seen."

After the first case ending, after pa da, yat comes in the sense of the place where something is seen. That in which footprints are seen is padyaḥ: mud. It designates what is neither too wet nor too dry.

From mū la, from utsa ṭa: "of this."

After mū with the first case ending, yat comes in the sense "this is fit to be pulled out." What is pulled out by the root is mū lyāḥ: muṅga beans. mu lyāḥ: sā li rice. The meaning is that the poor and the excellent can be properly judged only by pulling them out by the root.

From laṭā, in the sense "not a name," in a name.

After mū la with the third case ending, yat comes in the sense "not a name," in a name. What is "not a name" with respect to the root is mūlyāṃ: that which arises from the root as its cause, such as money, cloth and the like.

It denotes the price in money that arises from what is to be sold at a surplus. Because it is a surplus, it arises from the root. It is precisely ā nā myaṃ: ṇyat by special listing. If one asks why "in a name" is said: mū le na nā myaṃ, a tree.

Also from "equal in age."

After mū la with the third case ending, yat comes in the sense of "equal in age." One equal in age is ba yā yyaḥ. One equal in price is mū lyaḥ: a cloth. It says precisely "in a name": ba ya sā tu lyo ri buḥ.

From nau, tu lā, biṣ and dharma, in the senses "to be ferried across," "to be measured," "to be killed" and "to be obtained."

After nau and the rest, yat comes in the senses "to be ferried across" and the rest. What can be ferried across by boat is na byaḥ: water. What is measured with a balance, that is, determined, is tū lyaṃ. If one asks how gyi ri tu lya is formed: an elephant. Seeing and determining: it is a determining by measuring things as alike in their qualities. What deserves to be killed by poison is bi ṣyaḥ: an enemy. What is to be obtained by Dharma is dharmyaṃ: a happy destiny.

From sī tā and what ends in it, in the sense "goes along with."

Yat comes after the third case ending. What goes along with the furrow is sī tyaṃ: a field. dvi sī tyaṃ, tri sī tyaṃ.

From pa thi, dharma, artha and nyā ya.

After the third case ending, yat comes in the sense "goes along with": pa thyaṃ, dharmyaṃ, arthyaṃ, nyā yyaṃ. Since "in a name" continues to apply, it is a medicine, by going along the path of action, and not along the road to a village or the like.

Produced by meter.

Yat comes after the third case ending. A work produced by meter, by the Veda, or by a method of composition, a sūtra or a poem, is cchanda syaṃ. The word for "wish" is used because it proceeds by desire: so says Victorious Sunlight.

From u ras, aṇ as well.

After the word u ras with the third case ending, yat comes; and because of the word "as well," aṇ too. au ra saḥ: a son. u ra syaḥ denotes a son. Because "in a name" governs, it also means a disciple. u ra sā means having become the heart: he is said to be formed and engendered by that very heart, in a distinctive form.

From hṛ da ya, with ṅas, in the sense "pleasing."

After the word hṛ da ya with the sixth case ending, when "pleasing" is expressed, yat comes. What pleases the heart is hṛ dyaḥ.

Also in the sense of binding, for a mantra.

After hṛ da with the sixth case ending, yat comes. What binds the heart is hṛ dyaḥ: it denotes the mantra by which one brings another under control.

From yat ja na, in the senses of doing and of speech.

After the sixth case ending, yat comes. The doing of what is wished, or the doing of a thing, is ma tyaḥ. The speech of people is ja nyaḥ.

From ha la and what ends in it, in the sense of reviling.

After the sixth case ending, yat comes. The state or the doing of ploughing with a plough is ha lyaḥ; dvi ha lyaḥ; tri ha lyaḥ.

"From ṅi" means that after the seventh case ending, yat comes in the sense "good at." One good at work is karma ṇyaḥ; śarṇyaḥ. One good in the assembly hall is sa bhyaḥ. One good at what is common is sā ma nyaḥ: bra hma ṇaḥ. "Good" means virtuous or beneficial.

From pra ta ja na and the rest, kha.

It comes after the seventh case ending in the sense "good at"; it is an exception to yat. One good toward each person born is prā ti jā nī naḥ: the meaning is good toward each one who is being born and has been born. bai śva ja nī naḥ. pa ti ja na, bi śva ja na, ma hā ja na, sarba ja na, saṃ yu ga, i daṃ yu ga, pa raṃ ku la, a mu ṣya ku la.

From bhakta, ṇa.

It comes after the seventh case ending in the sense "good at." What is good as food is bhāktaḥ: śī li, large-grained rice.

From pa ri ṣad, ṇya.

It comes after the seventh case ending in the sense "good at," and ṇa as well: pā ri ṣa dyaḥ, pā ri ṣa daḥ. As before, pārṣi dyaḥ, bārṣa daḥ.

From ka thā and the rest, ṭhak.

It comes after the seventh case ending in the sense "good at": ka thī kaḥ, bai ka thi kaḥ. ka thāḥ, bi ka thā, bi śva ka thā, bitaṇḍā, ja na bā daḥ, bṛtti saṃ gra ha, gu ṇa, ga ṇā, ā yu be da, kulmā ṣa, ikṣu, sakṣu, a pū la, mā sau da na, bai ṇu saṃ ghā ta, pra bāṃ sa, u pa bā sa, bī bās.

From pa thya, ti thi, ba sa ti and sva pa ti, ḍhañ.

It comes after the seventh case ending in the sense "good at." What is good on the road is pā the yaṃ; a ti the yaṃ; pā sa te yaṃ; svā pa te yaṃ.

Above pā ri khā, cha.

For whatever senses lie above the proclamation of pa ri khā, cha should be understood to come.

From ut and from go and the rest, yat.

After words ending in the letter ū, and after go and the rest, yat comes in the domain of cha. What is beneficial for a peg is śaṃ ka byaṃ: wood. pi ca byaḥ: cotton. sa naṅga byaṃ: sa naṅga is leather; it denotes the leather of one who makes a living by shoes. ca ra byaḥ: large-grained rice. pakta byāḥ: barley. ka maṇḍa la byāḥ: earth. From go and the rest: ga bya, a grove. ha bi ṣaḥ: large-grained rice. go ha bi ṣaḥ; the eighth; barhi ṣaḥ; yu ga me dhā; śu as well. And na bha for nā bi: na bhyaḥ, wood. Where yat comes by the rule "from a part of the body," there is no change to na bha. From śva na, when truth is expressed, there is lengthening: śu nya, śū nyaṃ. And the n of udha sa: ū dha nyaḥ, a well. akṣa ra, da ra be da, slu da, bi śa. Although yat is already established above for a ca, go is mentioned again to indicate the rule for what ends in it: su ga byaṃ, prā ti ga byaṃ, pa ra ma ga byaṃ.

From kamba la, in a name.

Yat comes: kamba lyaṃ denotes eight palas of wool. If one asks why "in a name" is said: kamba lī yāḥ, ba la.

Optionally from ha bi ra yū ba and the rest.

After words denoting ha biḥ, and after yā ba and the rest, in the domain of cha, yat comes optionally: pu ro ḍā syāḥ, large-grained rice; pu ro ḍā sā yāḥ. After the word ha bi the former is constant: a mi kṣyaṃ, a mī kṣī ya. yū pyaṃ: wood. yū pī yaṃ, dī pī yaṃ, taṇḍu lī yaṃ. yū pa taṇḍa la, a pū pa, dvī pa, pī tha ka, a bha lu ṣa, a byo ṣa, kaṇṭhi, mu sa la, karṇa, beṣṭa ka, śa ka ṭa, argka la, sthū ṇa, pūrbya, a śva pa ṭa.

Also from modifications of food.

When a modification of food is expressed, yat comes optionally: suryāḥ, su rī yāḥ, large-grained rice. The reading of the word ā pū pa in the group of words denoting modifications serves to indicate the rule for what ends in it. Hence, for that: yā bā pā pyaṃ, ya bā pū pī yaṃ, pa dī pyi yaṃ.

In the sense "beneficial," from the fourth case.

After the fourth case ending, in the sense "beneficial," cha and yat come according to rule. What is beneficial for calves is batsī laḥ: milk.

ka ra bhī yaḥ, ba ṭa byaḥ; ga byaṃ: a grove. a pū byaṃ, a pū pī ya.

Not from rā ja, ā cārya, brāhma ṇa and bṛṣṇā.

After rā ja and the rest, cha does not come in the sense "beneficial": rājñe hi taṃ, ā cārya ya hi taṃ, brahma ṇā ya hi taṃ.

For parts of the body, yat.

It comes after the fourth case ending in the sense "beneficial." What is beneficial for the teeth is dantyaḥ: sesame. nā bhyaḥ, cākṣu ṣyaṃ.

From kha la, ya ba, mā ṣa, ti la, bṛ ṣa and brahma ra tha.

After the fourth case ending, yat comes in the sense "beneficial." What is beneficial for the threshing floor is kha lyaṃ; ya byaṃ, mā ṣyaṃ, ti lyaṃ, kriṣṇa ti lyaṃ, bṛṣyaṃ, brahma ṇya. What is beneficial for the sake of a chariot is ra thyāḥ.

For pra to lī, a ja and a bhi, thyan.

It comes after the fourth case ending in the sense "beneficial." What is beneficial for goats or sheep is a ja thyā.

For ārma and for what ends in bho ga, kha.

After the fourth case ending, kha comes in the sense "beneficial." What is beneficial for oneself is ārma nī naṃ. What is beneficial for a mother's enjoyment is mā tri bho gī ṇaḥ; bho gaḥ is śa rī raṃ. rā ja bho gī ni, ā cārya bho gī ni. Because these belong to kṣu ghna and the rest, n is not changed to ṇ.

From ji na after pañca, bi śva and the rest, in a karmadhāraya compound.

After the word ji na with pañca or bi śva first, ending in the fourth case, kha comes in the sense "beneficial." What is beneficial for those born of the five is pañca nī niḥ; bi śva ja nī naḥ. If one asks why "in a karmadhāraya" is said: one born of various things is bi śva ja naḥ; what is beneficial for him, or beneficial for the birth of various beings, is bi śva ja nī yaḥ.

From sarba.

After ja na following it, with the fourth case ending, kha comes in the sense "beneficial." What is beneficial for those born of all is sarba ja nī yaḥ. It says precisely "in a karmadhāraya": sarba ja ni kaḥ.

From ma ha ta, ṭhañ as well.

After ja na following the word ma hat or sarba, with the fourth case ending, ṭhañ comes in the sense "beneficial": mā hā ja ni kaḥ, sarba ja ni kaḥ. It says precisely "in a karmadhāraya": mā hā ja nī yaḥ, sarba ji nī yaḥ.

From sarbā, ṇa optionally.

After the word sarba with the fourth case ending, ṇa comes optionally in the sense "beneficial." What is beneficial for the sake of all is sārbaḥ, sarbā yaḥ. Mentioning sarba again serves to exclude ja na.

From mā ṇa ba and ca ra ka, khañ.

It comes after the fourth case ending in the sense "beneficial": mā ṇa bī naṃ, cā ra kī ṇaṃ.

From the product, in the sense of the material cause.

After a word expressing the product, with the fourth case ending, when the material cause is expressed, cha and yat come according to rule. The woods that are beneficial for charcoal are aṅga rī yā ṇiḥ; prā pā dī yāḥ: bricks; pi ca byaḥ: cotton. If one asks why "material cause" is said: u da kā ya kū paḥ. If one asks why "from the product" is said: by mentioning it one makes the rule understood. If one asks why "after the fourth case ending" is said: saktu nī dhā nāḥ. If one asks why it does not come here, in mū tra ya kalpa te ya bā gūḥ: because the word is dependent on another. For it is not a product, since it is a sentence into which a following word enters. If one asks how then it is that sentence: it is said that this is because it can be connected with the product. For that very reason this is not a case of it: sāndhya ba ha bi kaṃ, aṃ tyā ma nantiḥ. If one asks how then pu trī ya ti is used for "desires a son": it comes from the product itself, there from the two senses of the suffix. If one asks how then it is not the material cause of ya ba gū: it is not; the material cause of ya ba gū in a single instance is the wet substance that turns into it. Whatever non-product brings something about within a single continuum is its material cause.

From carma na, añ.

When a product whose material cause is leather is expressed, añ comes. What is beneficial for the sake of a ba ḍha is bā ḍhaṃ: leather. What is beneficial for a strap is bra traṃ: leather.

From ṛ ṣa bha and u pā nā ha, ña.

It comes after the fourth case ending in the sense "beneficial for a material cause": ārṣa bhyaḥ, a calf; au pā na hyaṃ, leather.

cha di pa li: ḍhañ.

After these, with the fourth case ending, ḍhañ comes in the sense "beneficial for a material cause." The grasses for covering the hand are chā dī ṣe yaṃ; leather. bā la yāṃ: large-grained rice.

From u pa dhi.

Ḍhañ comes in its own sense; by the splitting of the rule it applies to u pa dhi itself: au pa dhi yaṃ. In "u pa dhi ya te, u pa yā dhi ya teḥ," the u pa dhā is a part of a chariot.

From su, in the sense "possible as its basis, as material cause."

"From su" means that after the first case ending cha and yat come according to rule; that is, in the sense of being possible as the basis of the word with the first case ending, and as its material cause, it comes here from pra sā da. pra sā dī yaḥ: a place. prā sā dāḥ: it comes here. prā sā dī yaṃ: wood. Likewise pa ra śa byaṃ: this. ca ra byāḥ: large-grained rice. a ga ra byaṃ: a grove. If one asks why "possible" is mentioned: prā sā daḥ, it is here; prā sā dāḥ, this. If one asks why "material cause" is mentioned: prā sā daḥ, this will become something that increases. Because the product is extensive, this is not possible even for many.

From pā ri ṣa, ḍhañ.

After the word pa ri khā, in the sense of being possible as its basis, as material cause, ḍhañ comes after the first case ending: pā ri khe yaḥ, a place; pa rī khe yī, soil.

Homage to the Buddha.

Above ba ti, ṭhañ.

For whatever senses lie above the proclamation of ba ti, ṭhañ is to be understood.

From a word with a numeral first, when it is not elided.

Henceforth, for the senses above ba ti, the suffix formed from a particular base comes after a word that ends in it and begins with a numeral, provided that the ending is not elided. For "engages in support": pā rā ya ṇi kaḥ, tau ra ya ṇi kaḥ, cāndra ya ṇi kaḥ, dvai pa ra ya ṇi kaḥ. If one asks why "when it is not elided" is said: what is bought with two winnowing baskets is dvi sūrbyai ṇa krī tra, dvi saurbya kiṃ. Even though it begins with a numeral, añ does not come again after the word dvi sūrbya. If one asks why "with a numeral first" is said: pa ra ma pā rā ya nāṃ bartta ya tiḥ. Since it is taken up generally, there is no restriction; hence pa ra ma saurbi kaḥ, dvi saurbyi kaḥ.

Up to the end of arha ti, for those that are not particular bases.

The formations that follow are to be understood as applying up to the end of arha ti, if the senses are not connected with particular bases.

From ka sa and arda, ṭhaṭ.

It comes up to the end of "deserves": kaṃ si kaḥ, kaṃ sa kī; ardha ka, ardhi kī. kārṣā pa ṇa becomes pa ti optionally.

Up to the end of arha ti, ṭhaṭ and the change into pra ti come optionally: kārṣā pa ṇi kaṃ, kārṣā pa ṇi kā; pra tik 'i, pra ti kī.

From sūrbba, añ.

It comes optionally up to the end of "deserves": saurppa, saurppo kī.

From śa ta mā na, bim śa ti ka, sa ha sra and bas na, always aṇ.

It comes up to the end of "deserves": śā ta mā na, baiṃ śa ti kaḥ, sā ha sram, bā sa naṃ.

From śa ta alone, ṭhan and yat; but not for that.

From śa ta alone, ṭhan and yat come, but not when that very hundred is what is expressed: śatyaḥ, śa ti kaḥ. If one asks why "alone" is said: for two and a hundred, dvi śa ti; when something is bought with that, dvi śa ti kaṃ. If one asks why "not for that" is said: that whose measure is a hundred, as when one says "he reads what consists of a hundred topics," is śa ta kaṃ: the set of topics. Here śa taṃ itself is the sense of the suffix, for the word śa ta is shown to apply also to what is counted. Elsewhere it remains śa ta itself: a bull bought for a hundred. śa taṃ, śa taṃ, śata kaṃ.

From ka ti, and from numerals not ending in ti or śa ta, kan.

After ka ti, and after numerals other than those ending in ti and those ending in śa ta, kan comes. What is bought for how many is ka ti kaḥ; pañca kaḥ, ba hu kaḥ, ga na kaḥ. If one asks why "not from those ending in ti and śata" is said: sāpta ti kaḥ, ṣāṣṭi kaḥ, pāñcā śatkaḥ. ka ti is mentioned because, ending in ti, it would fall under the prohibition.

After ba tu, iṭ optionally.

After a word ending in ba tu, the augment iṭ optionally comes before kan: tā ba ri kaḥ, tā batkaḥ; yā ba ti kaḥ, yā batkaṃ; ki ya ti kaḥ, ka yitkaḥ; i ya ti kaḥ, i yatkaḥ.

From triṃ śat and biṃ śati.

For these, kaṇ comes up to the end of "deserves": triṃ śatkaḥ, biṃ śatkaḥ.

When not a name, ḍvun.

It comes for those just explained: triṃ śa kaḥ, biṃ śa kaḥ. For these the ti is elided by the rule "before ḍi ta, of biṃ śa ta." If one asks why "when not a name" is said: triṃ śatkaḥ, biṃ śatkiḥ.

After a dvigu compound, elision.

What is made for a dvigu compound is elided up to the end of "deserves": dvi sūrbyaḥ, adhyarha sūrbyaḥ, arha pañca ma sārbya. If one asks why "after a dvigu" is said: saurbyaḥ, e kā da śa kaḥ. If one asks what the qualification "made" is for: what is bought with two baskets is dvi saurbyaṃ kaṃ; because of the word "numeral," vṛddhi applies not to the first word but to the later one. It says precisely "not a name": pāñca lau hi ti kaṃ, pañca kā pā li kaṃ; because these belong to a nu śa ti ka and the rest, vṛddhi applies to both words.

Optionally from kārṣā pa ṇa sa hasra, su barṇa and śa ta mā na.

After a dvigu ending in kārṣā pa ṇa and the rest, elision optionally takes place up to the end of "deserves": dvi kārṣā pa ṇaṃ, dvi kārṣa pa ṇi kaṃ; a dhyardha kārṣā pa ṇaṃ, a dhyārdha kārṣā pa ṇi kaṃ; dvi sā has svaṃ, dvi sā ha sraṃ a dhyardha sā ha sraṃ; dvi su barṇaṃ dvi sau barṇi kaṃ. The word su barṇa applies to a measure: a dhyardha su barṇaṃ, a dhyardha sau barṇṇi kaṃ; dvi śa ta mā naṃ, dvi śā ti māṃ naṃ; a dhyardha śa ta mā naṃ, a dhyardha śā ta mā naṃ.

From niṣka and bisti after dvi, tri and ba hu.

Elision takes place optionally: dvi bistaṃ, dvi baisti kaṃ; tri naṣka, tri naiṣkaṃ; ba hu niṣki kaṃ, ba hu naiṣki kaṃ; dvi bi staṃ, dvi bai sti kaṃ; tri bi staṃ, tri baisti kaṃ; ba hu bi staṃ, ba hu bai staṃ kaṃ.

From biṅśa ti ka, always kha.

After a dvigu ending in śa ti ka, in the domain where kan is elided, kha always comes: dvi biṃ śa ti kī naṃ, a dhyardha śa ti kī naṃ.

Also from what ends in khā rī and kā ka ṇī, ikan.

After a dvigu ending in khā rī or kā ka ṇi, and, because of the word "also," after these alone too, ikan comes: dvi khā rī kaṃ, a dhyardha khā rī kaṃ dvi kā ka ṇī kaṃ. After these alone too: khā rī kam, kā ka ṇāṃ ka. Some read kā ka nī in place of kā ka ṇi.

From pa ṇa, pā da and ma sa, yat.

After a dvigu ending in pa ṇa and the rest, yat comes up to the end of "deserves": dvi pa ṇyāṃ, a dhyardha pa ṇyaṃ; dvi mā syaṃ, a dhyardha pā dyaṃ; dvi mā syaṃ, a dhyardha mā syaṃ. Because it is not a limb of a living being, there is no change into ba da.

Optionally from śa ta.

After a dvigu ending in śa ta, yat comes in the domain of elision: dvi śa tyaṃ, a dhyardha śa tyaṃ; dvi śa taṃ, a dhyardha śa taṃ. In the alternative with ṭhañ, it is elided.

From śā ṇa.

After a dvigu ending in śā ṇa, yat comes optionally in the domain of elision: dvi śā ṇyaṃ, dvi śā ṇaṃ; a dyardha śaṇyaṃ, a dhyardha śa ṇaṃ.

From dvi and ti, aṇ as well.

After śā ṇa preceded by dvi or ti, aṇ also comes, and yat too: dvi śā ṇyaṃ, dvai śā ṇaṃ; tri śā ṇyaṃ, trai śā ṇaṃ; dvi śā ṇaṃ, tri śā naṃ.

From ṭā, in the sense "bought," from a word for price.

After a word expressing price, with the third case ending, ṭhak comes according to rule in this sense, "bought." What is bought for seventy is sāpta ti kaṃ; a śī ti kaṃ, kāṃ si kaṃ, ārdhi ka, sarbya, sāh sraḥ, pañca kaḥ. If one asks why "from a word for price" is said: de ba datte na kri taṃ, pa ṇi nā kri taṃ. Because the commentary implicitly states a particular number, it does not come after the dual or the plural: prasthā bhyaṃ krī taṃ, prasthaiḥ krī taṃ. Where there is the capacity, it does come: dvi kaṃ, tri kaṃ, pañca kaṃ. What is bought with beans is mauṅga kaṃ; since it does not come for what is bought with a single bean, plurality is understood throughout.

From ṅas, in the sense of sowing.

After the sixth case ending, in the sense of sowing, ṭhañ comes according to rule up to the end of "deserves." Where a prastha measure is sown is prasthī kaṃ: a field. pā tri kaṃ, dro ṇi kaṃ, kaṃ si kaṃ, paurbyaḥ, khā rī kaṃ. Here it is "sowing" because seeds are sown.

From bā ta, bitte, śleṣma and panni pā ta, in the senses of allaying and aggravating.

After the sixth case ending, ṭhañ comes. What allays or aggravates wind is bā ti kaṃ; bait kaṃ, śleṣi kaṃ, sānni pā ti kaṃ. Allaying belongs to healing; aggravating is a cause of disturbance.

From a word for the occasion, in the senses of connection and portent.

After the sixth case ending, ṭhañ comes up to the end of "deserves." What is the occasion, as connection or as portent, for reaching a hundred is śa ti kaḥ, śa tyaḥ; sāṃ gra mi ka, ulkā pā tāḥ. Connection is relation. Portent is a change that signals the virtue and non-virtue of living beings. If one asks why "connection and portent" is said: śa ta sya ni mittaṃ de ba dattaḥ.

From a stem of two vowels, other than numerals, measures, and a śva and the rest, yat.

After a stem of two vowels with the sixth case ending, excepting numerals, measures, and a śva and the rest, yat comes in the senses of connection and portent; it is an exception to ṭhañ and the rest. What is the occasion, as connection or as portent, of wealth is dha nyaḥ; sarggyaḥ, ā yu ṣyaḥ, ya śa syaḥ, pa ya syaḥ. If one asks why numerals and the rest are excepted: pañca kaḥ, prāsthi kaḥ, a śvi kaḥ, aśmi kaḥ. a śva, aśman, gaṇ, ūrṇṇa, u mā bhaṅgā, barṣā, ba su. If one asks how ga byaḥ comes about for what is the occasion, as connection or as portent, of cattle: it is because yat is formed by the rule "from ga, from a ca."

From brahma barca sa.

After the sixth case ending, yat comes. What is the occasion, as connection or as portent, of brahma barca is brahma barca syeḥ.

From pu tra, cha as well.

After the sixth case ending cha comes, and yat too. What is the occasion, as connection or as portent, of a son is pu trī yaḥ, pu tryaḥ. This is stated because by "from a stem of two vowels" yat alone would always be obtained.

From pṛ thi bī and sarba bhū mi, in the sense of "lord," aṇ as well.

After pṛ thi bī and sarba bhū mi, when "lord" is expressed, aṇ comes; and because of the word "as well," also for the cause of connection and portent. The lord of the earth is pārthi paḥ, sārbba bhau maḥ. Likewise for the cause of connection and portent.

From ṅi, in the sense "known."

After pṛ thi bī and sarba bhū mi with the seventh case ending, aṇ comes in the sense "known." One known on earth is pārthi baḥ, sārbba bhū maḥ. For kaṃ pa and the rest, ṭhañ and the rest do not come, because of their relation to a particular base.

From lo ka and sa lo ka.

After the seventh case ending, ṭhañ comes in the sense "known." One known in the world is lau ki kaḥ, sārba lau ki kaḥ.

From su, for interest, revenue, profit, toll and gift, in the sense "to be given to this" or "for the sake of this."

In the senses of that to which, or for the sake of which, something is to be given, after the first case ending, in the senses of interest and the rest, ṭhañ comes according to rule up to the end of "deserves." For that on which, for the sake of five, interest, revenue, profit, toll or gift is to be given: pañca kaḥ, sapta kaḥ, sa ha sraḥ, śa dyaḥ, śa ti kaḥ, prāsthi kaḥ, kaṃ si kaḥ, saurbyaḥ. If one asks why "interest and the rest" is said: pañca mu lya ma mai dā ya ste. What is to be given over and above the wealth taken as the principal of a loan is interest. What accrues in villages and the like held by the king is revenue. Goods over and above the price obtained for what is sold are profit. What is to be given as levied by the king's guards is toll. What is given separately, accruing for the sake of that, is utko ca, that is, a gift.

From pū ra ṇa and ardha, ṭhan.

After a word expressing an ordinal, and after the word ardha, with the first case ending, ṭhan comes in these senses, "to be given to this" and "for the sake of this"; it is an exception to ṭhañ and ṭhaṭ, that is, if the word with the first case ending is interest or the like. Interest and the like to be given to this for the sake of the fifth: pañca mi kaḥ, sabta mi kaḥ, dvi tī yi kaḥ, tri tī yi kaḥ, a rṅhi kaḥ.

From bhā ga, yat as well.

After bhā ga with the first case ending, yat comes in these senses, "to be given to this" and "for the sake of this," and ṭhañ too, if it is quite other than interest and the rest. What is to be given to this for the sake of a share is bhā gyaḥ, bhā gi kaḥ, bhā gi kaḥ.

From a word measuring the size of this; when not elided.

After the first case ending, when the measure of something is expressed, ṭhañ comes according to rule in the sense "of this," up to the end of "deserves." That whose measure is measured by a prastha is prāsthi kaḥ: grain. khā ra śa ti kaḥ. That whose measure, its living, is fully measured by sixty is ṣāṣṭi kaḥ. Likewise, that whose measure is fully measured by five is pañca kaḥ: a Saṅgha. aṣṭa kaḥ: the sūtra of Pāṇini. sapta kaṃ: that of Rājāśrī. aṣṭā kaṃ: that of Sādhukīrti. That whose measure is a nature spreading forth from five is pañca kaḥ: recitation. pañca kā bya, in the plural. Its measure is five, taken as a body, with distinct limbs. The general is the sense of the base; its particular, which possesses the general, is the sense of the suffix. biṅśa ti, triṃ śadta, ca tvā ri śat, pañcā śat, ṣṭi, sapta ti, a śī ti, na ba ti, śa ta and the rest, arranged in this way, are also by special listing: for dvau pañca, measured by these, by the suffix ti and the elision of ṭi. In order: for dvau da śa, by the suffix ti and the substitute biṅ śa, biṅ śa ti. From dva yod śa, by the suffix śat and the change into triṃm, triṃ śat. Likewise, by the change into ca tvā rim, ca tvā riṃ śat. By the change into pañca, pañcā śat. From ṣaṭ da śa, by the suffix ti and the change into ṣaṣ, ṣa ṣṭi. Likewise, by the change into saptaṅ, sapta tiḥ. By the form a śī, a śī ti. By the form na ba, na ba ti. From da śa da śa, by the suffix tan and the form śa, śa taṃ. By the words "and the rest" one should understand: da śa śa ta is sa ha sraṃ; da śa sa ha sre is a yu tan; da śa yu ta is lakṣaṃ; and so on. If one asks why "also when not elided" is said: that whose measure of living is measured by sixty-two is dva ṣṛ ṣṭi kaḥ; here, by the rule "after a dvigu," it is not elided.

pañcad and da śa da, optionally, for a group.

For these, making tu ba the suffix, by special listing: pañcad, a group; pañca kaḥ. da śa da, a group; da śa kaḥ.

For a stoma, ḍaṭ.

After the first case ending, when a measure is expressed and a stoma is designated, ḍaṭ comes: pañca da śaḥ, a stoma; pañca da śī, a series. Likewise biṃ śaḥ. Mentioning the stoma, although "group" is already present, serves to indicate the rule for the general case of living beings. And this is measured by number itself, not by the prastha and the like; therefore it does not come from that rule.

From triṅ śaṭ and ca tvā riṃ śat, in a name of brahmins, ḍaṇ.

After these, with the first case ending, in this sense, "brahmins," ḍaṇ comes in a name. Those whose measure is the reading of thirty are trīṃ śā ni: brahmins. cā tvā riṃ śā ni. If one asks why "in a name" is said: triṃ śātkaḥ, by the rule "for triṃ śat and biṃ śa ta, kan"; cā tvā riṃ śatkaḥ.

"Above ba ti, ṭhañ": the remainder of the Veda and those brahmins as well. It is stated for some, not for all the senses.

From bhṛ ti, basna and aṃ śa.

After the first case ending, in this sense, "his," ṭhañ and the suffixes up to "deserves" come according to rule, that is, if the word with the first case ending is bhṛ ti or one of the rest. One whose wage, or whose share, is five kārṣa pa na is pañca kaḥ; prasthī kaḥ. A wage is the price of work. basnaṃ is the price of what is set out. aṃ śa is a share.

In the sense pa ci ti, from dro ṇa, from am, aṇ as well.

"From am" means that after the second case ending, after the word dro ṇa, aṇ comes in the sense "cooks," and because of the word "as well," ṭhañ too. It is a measure. One who cooks it is drau ṇaḥ, drau ṇi kāḥ, drau ṇi kī: a brahmin; a woman. bi kled naṃ pā kaḥ.

sambha ba ti and a ba ha ra ti as well.

In these senses, "holds," "consumes" and "cooks," after the second case ending, ṭhañ and the suffixes up to "deserves" come according to rule. What holds, consumes or cooks a prastha is prāsthī kaḥ; drau ṇi kaḥ, sā ha sraḥ. "Holding" is when large-grained rice and the like, taken as a whole, exceed the measure. "Consuming" is gathering in.

From bā dra, a ci ta and ā dha ka, kha optionally.

After the second case ending it comes in the senses "holds" and the rest. What holds, consumes or cooks a vessel is prā tī ṇā, prā tī kī; ā ci tī nāḥ, a ci tī kīḥ; a ḍha kī nāḥ, ā ḍha kī.

After a dvigu compound, ṭhaṭ as well.

After a dvigu ending in pā tra and the rest, ṭhañ comes in the senses "holds" and the rest, and kha too, optionally. What holds, consumes or cooks in two vessels is dvi pā trī kī, dvi pā trī ṇa, dvi pā trī. By the rule "after a measure, kā la basti, a ci ta, kampa lya and saṃ khya, when the suffix is elided," dip comes: dvyā ci ta kī, dvyā ci tī nā, dvyā ci tī; dvyā ḍha ki kī, dvya ḍha kī, nā dvyā kī. Although the two suffixes are established by the word "as well," the continuation of the word "or" serves to make clear the nature of the elision of ṭhañ.

Optionally from ku li ji.

After a dvigu ending in ku li jā, ṭha comes optionally in the senses "holds" and the rest, and kha too, optionally. What holds, consumes or cooks what is born of two families is dvai ku li ji kī, dvi ku li jī nā, dvi ku li jā, dvai ku li ji kī. With the continuation of the word "or," bai ka ra ṇaḥ: its purpose is the option for ṭha. Hence cā rū pyaṃ samba dya te.

From bha ra after baṃ śa and the rest, in the senses of carrying a load, conveying and bringing.

After bha ra following baṃ śa and the rest, ṭhañ comes in the senses of carrying and the rest. One who carries, conveys or brings a load of bamboo is bā śa bhā ri kaḥ; kau tra ja bhā ri kaḥ. If one asks why "after baṃ śa and the rest" is said: dha nyaḥ bhā raṃ ba ha ti. Or else it is formed from the load that comes from baṃ śa and the rest: baṃ śaṃ haṃ ra ti, kau tri ji kaḥ. If one asks why "from bha ra" is said: baṃ śaṃ haṃ ra ti. baṃ śa, ku ṭa ja, balba ja, mū la, sthu na, akṣa, aṣma na, ikṣu, kha tvā.

From dra bya and basna, kan and ṭhañ.

They come in the senses of carrying and the rest, as is known. One who carries, conveys or brings goods is dra bya kaḥ; basna kaḥ. "Carrying" is causing something to reach a place, or else robbing. "Conveying" is lifting up and conveying. "Bringing" is producing.

arha ti.

In the sense "deserves," after the second case ending, ṭhañ and the suffixes up to "deserves" come according to rule. One who deserves clothing is bā stri kaḥ; śa tyaḥ, śa ti kaḥ, pañca kaḥ. pūrba kā as well: they come precisely in these senses above bā ti. One who deserves fire is āgne yaḥ.

From ccha da and the rest, "constantly."

After ccha da and the rest, ṭhañ comes in this sense, "constantly deserves." One who constantly deserves to be cut is cchai di kaḥ; bhai di kaḥ. cchai da, bhai da, dro ṇa, nartta, dro ha, karṣa, sa sra yo ga bi pra yo ga.

bi rā ga becomes bi raṅga as well.

After the word ba rī ga with the second case ending, ṭhañ comes in the sense "deserves," and it is also changed into bi raṅ ga. For "constantly deserves dispassion": bai raṅga kaḥ, a monk.

From śīrṣa ccha da, yat as well.

Yat comes, and ṭhañ too. For "constantly deserves to have his head cut off": śairṣa ccha di kaḥ, śīrṣa ccha dyaḥ.

From yajña, gha.

It comes. For "deserves a sacrifice, or the act of sacrifice": yajña yaḥ.

From pā tra, gha as well.

Yat comes, and gha too. For "deserves an alms bowl": pā tryaḥ, pā tri yaḥ.

From daṇḍa and the rest.

After the second case ending, yat comes in the sense "deserves." For "deserves punishment": daṇṭyaḥ; ba dhyaḥ, arghyaḥ. daṇḍa, mu su la, argha, ma rgyu parkaḥ, i bha, me dhā, ma gha, u da kā, yu ga, ba dha.

From ka ḍaṃ ga ra, dakṣa ṇā and sthā lī bi la, cha as well.

After the second case ending, yat comes in the sense "deserves," and cha too. For "deserves straw": ka ḍaṃ ga rī yaḥ, a cow; ka ḍaṃ garya. dakṣa ṇī yaḥ, a monk; dakṣi ṇyaḥ. sthī lī bi lī yaḥ, large-grained rice; stha lī bi lyā. ka ḍaṃ ro mā sā di nāṃ sṭhū la bu ṣa sta marha ti: because of a dīptāgni; or else nairguṇya.

From ṛ tvi ja, khañ.

After the word ṛ tvik with the second case ending, khañ comes in the sense "deserves." For "deserves to be offered worship in season": īrti jī naḥ, a sacrificer.

śā li na and kau pi na, in the senses of one whose brow and head are turned away, and of what is not to be done.

These are by special listing. One placed among people who, with brow and head turned away, cannot stay there and is fit only to go inside is śā lī naḥ. That which, being something not to be done, deserves to be strongly forbidden because it is blameworthy, and so deserves to be put down a well, is kau pa'i na. By "deserving to be forbidden," a certain part of the body is also meant; not covering it is also blameworthy.

The explanation of the section ending with "deserves" is complete.

From pā rā ya ṇa, tu rā ya ṇa and cāndrā ya ṇa, "engages in."

After the second case ending, ṭhañ comes in the sense "engages in." One who engages in, that is, recites, the "support" (pārāyaṇa) is pā rā ya ṇi kaḥ: a student. One who engages in the tu rā ya ṇa sacrifice is tau rā ya ṇi kaḥ: a sacrificer. One who engages in the cāndrā ya observance is cāndrā ya ṇi kaḥ: an ascetic. Because verbal roots have many meanings, all these senses are included.

From saṃ śa ya, in the sense "has fallen into."

After the word saṃ śa ya with the second case ending, ṭhañ comes in the sense "has fallen into." One who has fallen into doubt is sāṃ śa yi kaḥ.

From yo ja na, in the sense "goes," ṭhañ comes. One who goes a yojana is yau ja ni kaḥ: it denotes precisely one who is able to go a yojana every day.

Also from kro śa ending in śa ta, in the sense "fit to be approached."

Also from kro śa ending in śa ta, besides the sense of going a yojana, ṭhañ comes in the sense "fit to be approached." One who goes a hundred yojanas, or who is fit to be approached from that distance, is yau ja ni śa ti kaḥ, and likewise krau śa śa ti kaḥ. The one fit to be approached is an individually noble one.

From pa tha, ṣi kan.

After the word pa tha with the second case ending, ṣi kan comes in the sense of going. One who goes along the path is pa thi kaḥ, feminine pa thi kī.

In the sense "without interruption," ṇa, and also the change into pantha.

In the sense of going without interruption, ṇa comes after pa tha with the second case ending, and the word is also changed into pantha: pāntha, panthā.

From pa tha preceded by a ja, śaṃ ku, utta ra, pa ri jaṃ ga la and kāntā ra, in the sense "taken by it."

After the word pa tha preceded by a ja and the rest, with the third case ending, ṭhañ comes in the sense "goes," and also in the sense "taken by it." One who goes by taking the goats' path is a ja pa thi kaḥ. A path like a peg is śa ku pa tha, and so one who goes by it is śā ku pa thi kaḥ. By the northern path, autta ra pa thi kaḥ. By the covered path, bā ri ba thi kaḥ. By the jaṃ ga la path, jī ga la pa thi kaḥ. By the wilderness path, kāntā ra pa thikaḥ.

Also when preceded by sthā la.

After the word ba thi with the third case ending, ṭhañ comes in the sense "taken by" or "goes." What is taken by, or one who goes by, the stha la pa tha, the land route, is sthā la pa thi kaḥ.

From ma dhu ka and ma rī ca, aṇ.

After stha la pa tha, aṇ comes when ma dhu kaṃ or ma rī ca is meant. What is carried by the stha la pa tha is sthā la pa thaṃ: maṅhu kaṃ, ma rī ci. ma dhu kaṃ is sweetwood, a sweet substance. The commentator on bhā ri ṣa says: "After stha la and bā ri, aṇ for ma dhu ka and ma rī ca."

From a word for time, in the sense "completed."

After a word denoting time with the third case ending, ṭhañ comes in the sense "completed." What is completed in a day is ā hni kaṃ; in a month, mā si kaṃ; in a year, sāmbatsa ri kaṃ. The words "from a word for time" carry on into the rules that follow.

From the fourth case ending, in the senses "wage" and "specially requested."

ṭhañ comes after a word for time. A wage, or a special request, made for the sake of a month is mā si kaḥ; for a year, sāmbatsa ri kaḥ. A wage is what is bought by work. A special request is one that comes with an act of honor.

From aṃ, in the senses "has come to be" and "is to be."

After the second case ending, ṭhañ comes in the senses "has come to be" and "is to be." What has come to be, or is to be, for a month is mā si kaḥ. From here on, for nirbṛtta and the rest, that is, for the five senses, the procedure is to be understood as it applies: after the third, fourth and second case endings, from a word for time.

From mā sa, in the sense of age, yat and kha.

These come in the sense "has come to be." One who has lived a month is mā syaḥ or mā sī kaḥ: a baby boy. Why "in the sense of age"? Because otherwise we get mā si kaḥ, since the bare procedure for "has come to be" carries no aspect of age.

Preceded by a numeral, yat.

After mā sa preceded by a numeral, yat comes when age is to be made known. One who has lived two months is dvi mā syaḥ.

From ṣa ṣa, also ṇyat, optionally.

After mā sa preceded by ṣaṭ, ṇyat comes optionally when age is to be made known. Because of the word "also," yat comes optionally too: ṣaṇma syaḥ, ṣaṇmā si kaḥ, ṣaṇmā syaḥ.

Otherwise, ṭhañ as well.

Other than in the sense of age, after mas preceded by ṣaṭ, ṭhan comes in the senses "completed" and the rest, and nyat as well. What is completed in six months, or is a wage or special request for six months, or has come to be or is to be for six months, is ṣanmā sa syaḥ.

From sā mā, kha.

After sa mā, kha comes in the five senses "completed" and the rest. For what is completed in a year, a wage or special request for the sake of a sa mī, and what has come to be or is to be for a sā mā: sa mī naḥ, prā pā daḥ.

Preceded by a numeral, optionally.

After sa mā preceded by a numeral, kha comes optionally in the five senses: dvi sa mī naḥ, dvai sa mi kaḥ.

From ra tvi ya ha naḥ and sambatsa ra.

After a word ending in rā tri and the rest, preceded by a numeral, kha comes optionally in the senses "completed" and the rest, and in part ṭhañ: dva rī trī ṇaḥ, dvai rā tri kaḥ, dvyā hī naḥ, dvai ya hni kaḥ, dvi sambatsa rī ṇa, and, with strengthening of the final member, dvi sāmbatsa ri kaḥ.

From barṣa, also elision.

After barṣa preceded by a numeral, kha comes optionally in the five senses, and elision too is optional: dvi barṣī ṇaḥ, dvi bārṣi kaḥ. The final member is strengthened by the rule "of barṣa, when not in the future": dvi barṣaḥ, prā pā daḥ. For "is to be," dai bārṣa kaḥ, trai barṣi kaḥ.

For a living being.

After barṣa preceded by a numeral, when a living being is meant, only elision takes place from here on, since it is laid down again: dvi barṣaḥ, tri barṣaḥ: a young dog.

ṣaṣṭi ka: this is a set form for the sense "ripens in sixty days," and it is a name. So it does not overextend to mung beans, muṅga, and the like. ṣaṣṭi kaḥ is a kind of grain.

From ṭā, in the senses "easily done," "to be done," "to be gained," and "to be fully mastered."

"From ṭā" means that after a word for time with the third case ending, ṭhañ comes in the senses "easily done" and the rest. What is easily done, or to be done, or to be gained, or to be fully mastered in a month is mā si kaḥ; in a year, sāmbatsa ri kaḥ. What can be mastered completely is what is "to be fully mastered."

"That," in the sense "of this," for religious discipline.

"That" means that after a word for time with the second case ending, ṭhañ comes in the sense of the sixth case, "of this," when religious discipline is to be made known. One whose religious discipline lasts a month is mā si kaḥ: a practitioner of religious discipline. Likewise ārdha mā si kaḥ and sāṃ batsa ri kaḥ. Or else, after "that" with the first case ending, ṭhañ comes in the sense of the sixth case, "of this," when the discipline itself is named. The religious discipline of this month is mā si kaṃ: the religious discipline itself. Likewise ā ho rā tri kaṃ.

From ṅas, after ma hā nā min and the rest.

ṭhañ comes for religious discipline. The religious discipline of the ma hā nā min verses is mā hā nā mni kaṃ; likewise ā di tya pra ti kaṃ and gau dā ni kaṃ.

From aṃ, "practices."

After ma hā nā min and the rest with the second case ending, ṭhañ comes in the sense "practices." The observance of the ma hā nā min verses is expressed by that very word. For one who practices the ma hā nā min verses: ma hā nā min kaḥ; likewise ā di tya bṛ ti kaḥ and gau dā ni kāḥ.

From de ba brat and the rest, ḍi ni.

After the second case ending, ḍi ni comes in the sense "practices." For one who practices the observance of the gods: de ba bra tī; likewise ti la bra tī and a banta ra dikṣi.

From aṣṭā ca tvā raṃ śad, also ḍun.

After the second case ending, ḍu na comes, and ḍi ni as well. For one who practices an observance for forty-eight years: aṣṭā ca tvā riṃ śa kaḥ, aṣṭā ca tvā riṃ śī.

From cā turmā sya, also deletion of ya.

What takes place in the four months is cā turmā sya.

From that, after the second case ending, ḍun comes in the sense "practices," and ḍa ni as well. When it is attached, ya is also deleted. One who practices an observance in the four months is cā turmā si kaḥ, cā turmā si kaḥ, cā turmā sī. This is the form of cā turmā.

From ṅas, after yajña, in the sense "fee."

After a word that names a sacrifice, with the sixth case ending, ṭhañ comes in the sense of a fee. The fee for the fire sacrifice is āgni ṣṭo mi kā; likewise ba ja pū yi kī, dvai bā ja pi yi kī, pāñcau da ni kī.

From ṅi, in the sense "to be given."

"From ṅi" means that after the seventh case ending, ṭhañ comes in the sense "to be given at a sacrifice." What is to be given at the fire sacrifice is āldisgoāgniṣṭo mi kaṃ; likewise rā ja sū yi kaṃ, bā ja pe yi kaṃ, pāñcau ni kaṃ. By this rule the form is held to be established for the fee and for the other senses as well.

Also for "to be done," as for "occurring."

After a word denoting time with the seventh case ending, when what is to be given is named, and also for what is to be done, ṭhañ and the rest come just as they do for "occurring." For example, what occurs in a month is mā si kaṃ; likewise sāmba tā rī kaṃ, hai minta kaṃ, bā santaṃ, prā bṛ ṣa ṇyaṃ, śā ra daṃ. The same holds for what is to be given and what is to be done.

From byuṣṭa and the rest, aṇ.

After the seventh case ending, it comes for what is to be done and what is to be given. For what is to be done or given at daybreak, bai yuṣṭaṃ, naityaṃ. The list runs: byuṣṭa nidya, niṣkra ma ṇa, ni bi śa na, pra be śa na, tīrtha, saṃ krā ma, sa ghā ta, pra bā sa, u pa bā sa, agni pa da, pī lu, mu la.

From ya thā ka thā ca, after ṭa, ṇa.

After ya thā ka thā ca with the third case ending, aṇ also comes in the senses "to be given" and "to be done." What is given or done any which way is yā thā ka, thā cā: a quality. The word ya thā ka thā is indeclinable, and it is used in general for what is done without care.

In the sense "adorns."

It comes in the senses "to be given" and "to be done": what is to be given or done by hand is hastyaṃ. In the sense "adorns," ṭhañ comes after the third case ending. What is adorned by ear wrappings is kārṇa baṣṭa ki kaṃ: the face. bāstra yu ga la li kaṃ: the body.

From karma and be śa, yat.

It comes after the third case ending in the sense "adorns." One adorned by deeds is karmma ṇyaṃ: a hero. One adorned by stage costume is bai śyā: a courtesan. Some say the word bai śā means "with the body."

From santrā pa and the rest, after the fourth case ending, in the sense pra bha bati.

After santā pa and the rest with the fourth case ending, ṭhañ comes in the sense of pra bha ba ti, "is capable of": sarba pā ya pra bha ba ti sa marthaḥ, santa pi kaḥ. The list runs: saṃ gra ma, sannā ha, saṃ yo ga, saṃ pa rā ya, saṃ be śaḥ, nirgho ṣa, ni sargga u pa sa rgga pa bā sa, sambā da na. It comes also from the words for barley flour, meat and rice when they are taken apart: mā si kaṃ, au da ni kam, saktu maṃ, sau da ni kaṃ.

From yo ga, also yat.

After the word yo ga with the fourth case ending, yat comes in the sense of pra bha ba ti. One who is capable of yoga is yo gyaḥ or yo gi kaḥ.

From karman, u kan.

It comes after the fourth case ending. What is capable of action is kārmu kaṃ: a bow.

From su, after sa ma ya, in the sense "has been reached by this."

After the word sa ma ya with the first case ending, ṭhañ comes in the sense of the sixth case, "of this." If it has reached its time, it is sā ma yi kaṃ.

From ṛ tu in particular, aṇ.

It comes in the sense "has been reached by this." What has reached its season is ārtta bam: a crop. That for which the clothing has drawn near is au pa bastram. That for which eating has fully arrived is pra śi traṃ bhaktaṃ.

From ka la, also yat.

It comes in the sense "has been reached by this." What has reached its time is kā lyaṃ: the cold.

From pra kraṣṭi.

After kā la with the first case ending, ṭhañ comes in the sense "drawn out by this." What is drawn out by its time is kā li ka: a loan.

From pra yo ja na.

After the first case ending, ṭhañ comes in the sense "this is the purpose of this." That whose purpose is the festival of Indra is aindram hi kaṃ; likewise mā ga li kaṃ and autsa bi kaṃ.

From e ka gā ra, for a thief.

After the word e kā gā ra, in the sense "this is the purpose of this," ṭhañ comes only when a thief is meant. One whose purpose is a single house is e kā gā ra ma re kaḥ: a thief. Why "for a thief"? Otherwise: e ma gā ra pra yo ja na ma sya.

Monk. From a kā la, ṭhañ as well.

In the sense "that is the purpose of this," ṭhañ comes; ṭhañ as well. That whose purpose is a short time, that is, untimely lightning, is ā gā li kī. It means lightning that perishes as soon as it arises. For that very reason, in this set form, the word for a short time signifies "untimely."

From śraddhā and the rest, aṇ.

It comes in the sense "that is the purpose of this." That whose purpose is faith, meaning full trust in the world beyond, as when one applies oneself with faith to the topknot rite, is cau ḍaṃ. Those who see aṇ applied within the scope of "that is the purpose of this" should understand the application to śraddhā and the rest in the same way.

From bai śā kha and ā ṣā ḍha, for a peg and a staff.

In the sense "that is the purpose of this," aṇ comes. What has this as its purpose is bai śā kha: a peg. ā ṣā ḍhaḥ: the staff of those who keep vows. Why "for a staff and a peg"? Otherwise: bai śā khi kaḥ, ā ṣā ḍha kaḥ.

From utthā pa na and the rest, cha.

It comes in the sense "that is the purpose of this." That whose purpose is raising up is utthā pa nī yaḥ; likewise u pasthā pa nī yaṃ. The list runs: utthā pa na, u pa sthā pa na, a nu pra ba ca na, pra be śa na u nu be śa na, a nu ro ha ṇa, ā ro ha ṇa, ā rambha ṇa, ā bha ra ṇa, bi śi, bu ri, pa di, ro hi, sa mā pi.

From a base other than a, together with a preceding word.

In the sense "that is the purpose of this," cha comes. That whose purpose is entering a house is gṛ ha pra be śa nāṃ yaṃ; likewise pra pā pū ra ṇī yaḥ, a śva pra ba da nī yaṃ, pra bā dā ba ro ha ṇī yaṃ, byā ka ra ṇa sa ma ṇī ya.

From svargga and the rest, yat.

It comes in the sense "that is the purpose of this": svarggya, dharmya, ya śa syaṃ, ā yu ṣyaṃ, kā myaṃ, dha nyaṃ.

From pu ṇyāhā ba ca naṃ and the rest, elision.

In the sense "that is the purpose of this," ṭhañ is elided: puṇyā hā ba ca naṃ, śānti, bā ca naṃ. That is, the ṭhañ ending is barred for these acts.

In the sense of comparison, ba ti.

When a thing is compared with a standard of comparison by which it is well established, ti comes after that standard, by implication for the thing compared. Like a brahmin is brahma bat: a kṣatriya. Like a bird is kha ga bat: a sage who travels through the sky. Like a conch is śaṃ kha bat: a person. Sound is impermanent, gha ṭa bat, like a pot. ku be ra bat: one who owns a pleasure grove.

You are to be looked upon like a god: de ba bat. You practice religious discipline like a sage: mu ni bat. One who makes gifts to the śūdra caste as a brahmin does: brāhma bat. One who stands firm like a mountain: parba ta bat. Some hold that it applies to the fifth case as well. A woman whose form is like a man's: puṃ bat. One who moves on water as on dry land: stha la bat.

For the state, after ṅas, to and tal.

"From this, for the suffixes that express it": this refers to the state. The ground on which a word is applied is a property, such as the class, within its own meaning. When that is to be expressed, to and tal come after the sixth case ending. The state of a cow is go tvāṃ or go tā; this expresses the class, which is the ground for applying the word go. The state of a white cloth is śu kal tvaṃ; śu kla tva is the state of a quality. When the state of the quality white is śukla tvaṃ or śukla ta, then white is the state of the class of that quality. pā ca ka tvaṃ, daṇḍi tvaṃ, bi ṣā ṇi tvaṃ, rva ja phu ru ṣa tvaṃ: here the relation to an action and the like is itself the state. de ba datta tvaṃ, candra tvaṃ: here the particular condition of that individual is the universal. ā kā śa tvaṃ and ā bhā ba tvaṃ are universals applied figuratively. For a single thing, a distinguishing ground of application other than that is to be conceived, and it too is not different from what it distinguishes.

After na ña, when the meaning is not another's, nañ.

When a word after na ña does not apply to another meaning, only tve and tal come for the state. One without debt is a nṛ ṇaṃ, and its state is i nṛ ṇa tvāṃ or a nṛ ṇa tā. Likewise a dharma tvaṃ, a dharma tā, a pṛ thu tvāṃ, a pṛ thu tā, a dṛ ḍha tvaṃ, a dṛ ḍha tā. Why "when the meaning is not another's"? The state of a person who has no debt is ā nṛ ṇyāṃ; likewise ā dharmyaṃ.

From ca tu ra, saṃ ga ta, la ba ṇa, ba ha, bu dha, ka ra and ra sa, optionally.

After ca tu ra and the rest preceded by nañ, tva and tal come optionally for the state, when the meaning is not another's: ā ca turyyaṃ, sāmma tyaṃ, ā ba la ṇyaṃ, ā pa jyaṃ, ā budhyaṃ, ā ka ryaṃ, ā ra syaṃ. In part, ā ca tu ra tvaṃ, ā ca tu ra tā, and so on.

From pṛ thu and the rest, i man.

After pṛ thu and the rest, i man comes optionally in the sense "its state." The state of being broad is pṛ thi mā, pṛ thu tvāṃ, pṛ thu tvāṃ, pārtha baṃ; likewise mra di mā, mṛ du tvaṃ, mārdu baṃ. The list runs: pṛ thu, mṛ du, pa ṭu, ta nu, la ghu, sā dhu, u ru, gu ru, ā śu, ba hu, ba hū la, ma ha ta, candra, khaṇḍḍa, a kañci na, bā la, ho ḍa, batsa, pāk, kanda, svā du, ṛ ju, hra sva, dīrgha, kaṣi pra, kṣu dra, pṛ ya.

From barṇa, dri ḍha and the rest, also ṣyañ.

From words for particular colors, and from dṛ ḍha and the rest, i man comes optionally in the sense "its state," and ṣyañ as well. From color words: śauklya, śukli mā, śukla tvaṃ, śukla tā. dārdhtyaṃ, dṛ dhi mā, dṛ dha tvaṃ, dṛ ḍha tā. The letter ṣa serves the purpose of dī pa: au ci tī, ya thā kā mī, bai dagdhī, sā ma gri, maittri, a nu pūrbī. The list runs: dṛ ḍha, bṛ ḍha, pa ri bṛ ḍha, bhṛ śa, kṛ śa, cu kra a mla, la ba ṇa, ma dhū ra, ā mra, śī ta, uṣṇa, ja ḍa, ba dhi ra, mū ka, mūrba, paṇḍi ta.

From yā ta, lā bha and śā ra da after bi.

In the sense "its state," ṣyañ comes, and i maṇ as well. One who reckons this as contradictory is bī yā taḥ, and its state is be yā tyaṃ or bi yā ti mā. Likewise bai lā bhya, bi lā bhi mā, bai śā ra dyaṃ, bi śā ra di mā.

Also after sam, from ti ma nas.

After saṃ and bi, for the following words ma ti and ma nas, ṣyañ comes in the sense "its state," and i man as well. The state of right understanding is sāmma tyaṃ; likewise bai ma tyaṃ, samma ti mā, bi ma ti mā, sāṃ ma na syaṃ, saṃ ma ni mā, bi ma ni mā.

From words expressing qualities and from brāhma ṇa and the rest, also for action.

To state a quality is to express a quality. After such words, and after brāhma ṇa and the rest, ṣyañ comes for the state and for action, and i man as well. Action here means an act of intention. For the state: jā ḍhaṃ, ja ḍi mā, ja ḍa tvaṃ, ja ḍa tvā, mo ḍhyaṃ, ba dhi ryaṃ, brāhma ṇyaṃ. The list runs: brāhma, niṣma la, bī li śa, duṣpu ru ṣa, rā ja na, ga du la, dā yā da, biṣasti, bi śa sya.

From ca turbarṇa and the rest, in their own sense.

Words with the first case ending, in their own sense: after words with the first case ending, ṣya ña comes. The four castes themselves are cā tu rbarṇṇya ca tu ra śra myaṃ; likewise trai ka syaṃ, trai ka lyaṃ, dvai rū pyaṃ, cā tu rū pyaṃ, trai gu ṇyaṃ, ṣāḍgu ṇyaṃ. The army itself is sai nyaṃ.
