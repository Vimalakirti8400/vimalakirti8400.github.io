The Praise in One Hundred and Fifty Verses (Śatapañcāśatkanāmastotra)

The Praise Called the One Hundred and Fifty, known in Sanskrit as the Śatapañcāśatka and in Tibetan as Brgya lnga bcu pa zhes bya ba'i bstod pa, is a devotional hymn in verse addressed to the Buddha and traditionally attributed to the Indian poet Mātṛceṭa. Opening with homage to the Three Jewels, it extols the Buddha as the one in whom every fault is absent and every good quality is complete, and it argues that anyone with understanding should take refuge in him alone, praise and honor him, and live according to his teaching. In verses of deep reverence, it reflects on the rare good fortune of encountering the Dharma, using the classic image of a turtle surfacing through a yoke adrift on the ocean, and goes on to celebrate the perfection of the Buddha's body, speech, and mind. The work is Toh 1147 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the collection of praises, or hymns of devotion, that open the canonical commentarial literature.

In the language of India: śatapañcaśatikanāmasyastotra
In the language of Tibet: The Praise Called the One Hundred and Fifty.

Homage to the Three Jewels.

In whom all faults whatsoever
Are utterly and entirely absent,
And in whom, in every respect,
All good qualities abide:

If one has a mind at all,
It is fitting to go for refuge to him alone,
To praise him, to honor him,
And to abide in his teaching.

The Omniscient One possesses every quality,
And these have no deficiency;
Faults, together with their habitual tendencies,
The sole Protector does not have.

In the activity of the Blessed One (the Buddha),
Of his body, his speech and his mind,
Not even those hostile to him
Can find a fault that accords with the Dharma.

Like a turtle that puts its neck
Through the hole of a yoke adrift on the ocean,
I have gained this human state,
Joined with the great festival of the true Dharma.

Bound up with impermanence,
And uneasy at the opening it may give to karma,
Why should I not, with this very tongue of mine,
Take hold of what is of real worth?

Though I know that the Sage's good qualities
Are beyond all count, even in their range,
Out of regard for my own good
I shall speak with care of a portion of them.

Your qualities have no limit in number,
And your power has no limit;
Your wondrous deeds are immeasurable.
Homage to you, Self-Arisen One!

Since there is no end to saying "this many,"
What could one say, "they are like this"?
Yet, thinking it merit to speak of your qualities,
I have grown bold.

This is the first chapter, the introduction to the praise.

Casting aside the hesitating thought,
"Shall I act or shall I not?"
Saying, "These beings are in misery,"
You yourself took on the task.

You do good though no one asks;
You love though there is no reason;
You are a friend to those you have never met,
A kinsman to those with no tie to you.

Since you gave away even your own flesh,
What need is there to speak of other things?
Doer of good, you gave
Even your life to those who begged for it.

With your own body and your own life,
Hundreds of times over,
You ransomed the bodies and lives
Of creatures seized by executioners.

Not out of dread of the lower realms,
Nor to secure some destiny you desired,
But solely with a pure intention,
You made moral discipline your habit.

Always shunning what is crooked,
Always keeping to the straight and steadfast,
Through your wholly pure deeds
You became the one and only support.

When you were tormented by many sufferings,
With a virtuous mind
You stood firm against the afflictions
And showed love to afflicted people.

The joy you felt when, for the sake of others,
You gave up your body and your life,
Is not found even in creatures
Saved from the slaughter.

Not once only, but many times,
Though you were hacked to pieces, you gave no thought to the pain,
And even toward the beings who acted as your executioners,
O Lord, you felt compassion.

That precious mind of yours,
The seed of perfect awakening,
You alone, O hero, know to its core;
Other people cannot even approach it.

Knowing that without the work of austerity
The state so hard to win is not attained,
For that reason, with no regard even for yourself,
You greatly increased your diligence.

In striving after excellence
You never once grew slack;
Therefore, in your excellences,
No order of earlier and later can be told apart.

Because your mind was always
Bound up with great compassion,
You were not attached even to the perfect bliss
Of meditative concentration and its fruits.

Happiness not shared with others,
However splendid it may be,
For one of noble conduct like you
Brings harm rather than help.

From what was mixed you took up only good sayings,
A faultless essence, nothing else,
And kept them in your mind;
Ill-spoken words you shunned entirely, like poison.

Knowing them to be a precious essence,
You bought good sayings at the price of your life;
In birth after birth
You showed your heroism for the sake of awakening.

Thus, for three countless eons,
As you strove in this way,
With diligence alone as your companion,
You attained the unsurpassed state.

This is the second chapter, the praise of the cause.

You did not envy those above you,
You did not disdain those below,
You did not compete with your equals;
And so you became foremost in the world.

You were devoted to the causes of good qualities,
Not to their fruits;
By that very right practice
Your qualities reached their utmost limit.

By your good conduct you so built yourself up
That even the dust of your feet
Becomes a field
In which merit grows.

Weakening faults and pulling them out by the root,
Increasing qualities and refining them,
By that excellent way of proceeding
You attained the supreme accomplishment above all others.

So thoroughly, in every respect,
Did you destroy the faults
That in your own mindstream not even a remnant
Of their habitual tendencies remains.

So abundantly, gathering and gathering,
Did you accomplish good qualities in yourself
That in no one else is there
Even a likeness of them.

All the things of the world that might serve as comparisons
Are hindered and obscured,
Temporary and limited,
And very hard to come by.

Your unsurpassed qualities
Have no hindrance and no obstruction;
Since they are stable and unbroken,
How could there be anything to compare them with?

When one considers the expanse of your mind,
Which has neither bottom nor further shore,
Even the depth of the ocean
Plainly appears like the hoofprint of a cow.

When one reflects on your steadfastness,
Which nothing at all can shake,
Even the steadiness of the earth
Seems as slight as the tip of a śirīṣa flower's filament.

O Sage, beside the light of your wisdom,
Which destroys the dimness of ignorance,
Even the sun
Does not count so much as a firefly.

Beside the purity of your speech, your mind and your conduct,
The pure autumn moon,
And the purity of water and of the sky,
Seem as though they were stained.

Whatever is excellent in the world,
Though all of it be brought forward, falls short of this;
For afflicted, worldly qualities
Are no match for the qualities of a buddha.

The precious Dharma, by attaining which
You became supreme, that alone
May be set against you in excellence;
It and you alone are fit to be compared.

Whatever little is said of you
By matching you in general terms with something else
Is no more than the speaker's whim,
A mere device, and idle talk.

This is the praise of the incomparable, the third chapter.

Your victory over Māra,
And your making all beings faithful:
When I reflect on your very nature,
I see nothing in these that seems great.

To overcome the many
Who strive to assail you out of hatred
Is a trifling matter for your patience, grounded in excellence,
And no great wonder.

But that on the very night
On which you subdued the māras
You abandoned the mighty afflictions,
That indeed is marvelous.

That the sun with its thousand garlands of rays
Dispels the darkness,
And that you, O Hero, have conquered the non-Buddhists (tīrthikas):
What is wonderful in that?

Free of attachment, you tamed the attached;
Free of aversion, the hateful;
Free of delusion, the deluded:
By these three you always tamed those three.

Though you highly praise the true Dharma
And censure what is not the true Dharma,
Toward the noble and the base
You have neither attachment nor anger.

If your mind is not moved
To fondness for arhats
Or to anger toward the non-Buddhists,
What is there to say in praise of you?

You are not attached even to good qualities,
Nor partial even toward the virtuous.
Oh, how wondrously clear and utterly pure
Is the strength of your mind!

Since your senses are perfectly serene
And never at any time decline,
Your ever-serene mind
Seems as if directly perceived.

Since it is proclaimed by your sublime nature,
Your well-spoken words and your good conduct,
The purity of your understanding and intentions
Is known even among children.

This is the praise of the marvelous, the fourth chapter.

Calm, yet delightful;
Blazing, yet doing no harm;
Gentle, yet of great majesty:
Whom does this body of yours not make faithful?

One who has seen your body a hundred times,
And one who sees it for the first time:
To both alike
It brings delight to the eyes.

Because your body is so beautiful,
By its nature one never tires of looking;
Every time it is seen,
It brings forth joy again and again.

Since the qualities of the support
And the qualities it supports suit one another,
Your body and your qualities
Possess supreme perfection.

Apart from your body, blazing
With the excellent major and minor marks,
In what other could these
Qualities of a Tathāgata rest so well?

Your body seems to say to the qualities it supports,
"I am of great glory,"
And those qualities in turn
Seem to say, "It has set us well in place."

This is the praise of the body, the fifth chapter.

All these beings without distinction
Are bound by the afflictions;
To free beings from their afflictions
You were long bound by compassion.

Shall I first bow to you,
Or to that great compassion
By which you, knowing its faults,
Remained so long in saṃsāra?

That you, who gave up the bliss of solitude,
Should dwell amid the crowd:
It seems your compassion, its time having come,
Seized an opening and brought this about.

As a nāga is drawn from a lake by a spell,
So your compassion drew you
From the peaceful forest dwelling to the edge of the villages
For the sake of those to be tamed.

Though you abide in supreme peace,
Because compassion was foremost in you,
You made even the musician cast down his art
At your feet.

Your miracles, your lion's roar,
And your proclaiming of your own qualities:
These, in you who have no self-seeking,
Were displayed by your compassion.

Excellent in serving the good of others,
Yet having no tender care for its own,
Protector, toward you alone
Compassion showed no compassion.

Thus at times that steadfast compassion,
In order to accomplish the good of others,
Cast you out to the directions a hundred times
Like a torma offering.

Though it harms you in this way,
You do not count it at fault;
And so it seems that it acts
Solely in harmony with your wishes.

This is the praise of the mind, the sixth chapter.

Fine in its words and great in its meaning,
True and pleasing to the ear,
Endowed with meaning both profound and clear,
Both concise and elaborated:
Such is your speech. Hearing it,
Who, even among your enemies,
Would not become certain,
"This is one who knows all"?

Most of it is pleasing through and through;
A little is pleasing only by your art.
Yet because your speech accomplishes its purpose,
All of it is nothing but well spoken.

When your speech is examined,
Whatever is gentle, whatever harsh,
And whatever has something of both,
All of it becomes of a single taste.

How wonderful! Masterful indeed
Is the craftsmanship of utterly pure deeds,
By which such a vessel as this
For precious words was fashioned.

Your face is most lovely to behold,
and from it come these words of yours,
pleasant to hear,
like nectar dripping from the moon.

Your speech is like a rain cloud
that settles the dust of attachment.
Because it drives off the serpent of aversion,
it is like a garuḍa.

Because again and again it destroys
the darkness of ignorance, it is like the sun.
Because it shatters the mountain of pride,
it is like the weapon of Śakra (Indra).

Because it sees the truth, it does not deceive;
because it is free of fault, it is consistent;
because it is well composed, it is easy to grasp:
your speech has these three excellences.

At first your words
captivate the minds of those who hear them;
then, when they are reflected on,
they clear away both the dust and the darkness.

They comfort the destitute
and alarm the heedless;
because they stir the comfortable to weariness with the world,
your words reach each person in a fitting way.

They give joy to the wise,
increase the understanding of the middling,
and destroy the darkness of the lowest;
so this speech benefits all people.

Your speech separates beings from wrong views
and leads them close to nirvāṇa.
It dispels faults
and pours down a rain of good qualities.

Your mind is unobstructed toward all things,
and your attention is always present to all;
therefore every declaration you make
deceives no one at all.

Because it is never spoken in the wrong place,
at the wrong time,
or to an unfit vessel,
your speech bears fruit, like well-directed diligence.

The Praise of Speech, the seventh chapter.

A single way, easy in its means,
well connected and free of fault,
good in the beginning, the middle and the end:
no one else has a teaching like yours.

If, clinging to views they alone hold dear,
the childish revile
even such a doctrine as yours,
there is no enemy like views.

When one considers the many sufferings
you endured for the sake of other beings,
one would have to abide in your teaching
even if it were a poor one.

How much more so, then, with your teaching,
which speaks of what helps and brings help!
Why would one not practice it
as though one's own head were ablaze?

Through your teaching all four of these excellences are gained:
independence,
the bliss of awakening and peace,
and the praise of your qualities.

O Hero, this teaching of yours
terrifies all the non-Buddhists (tīrthikas),
fills Māra with dismay,
and lets gods and humans breathe freely.

The command of the Lord of Death,
which holds sway over the great lands of the three realms,
unobstructed and irreversible,
is overpowered by your teaching.

One who understands the way of your teaching
may remain even for an eon if he wishes,
and he goes with joy and gladness
to the place where Māra holds no power.

The division into three stages,
respectful listening,
reflection on the meaning of scripture, and meditation,
is found nowhere but in your teaching.

O Sage, supreme among beings,
what greater suffering could there be
than to show disrespect toward this teaching of yours,
so thoroughly pervaded by excellence?

The Praise of the Teaching, the eighth chapter.

Hearing of you, enthusiasm arises;
seeing you, faith is born;
hearing your words, one is gladdened;
your teaching brings complete liberation.

Your heirs bring contentment;
your spreading delights all creatures;
when you set forth, you bring benefit;
when you turn back, decline follows.

Proclaiming you takes away wrongdoing;
remembering you brings gladness;
seeking you gives rise to understanding;
fully knowing you brings purity.

Approaching you brings glory;
attending on you, understanding becomes supreme;
relying on you makes one free of fear;
honoring you brings happiness.

Pure through perfect moral discipline,
clear through perfect meditative concentration,
undisturbed through perfect wisdom,
you are a great lake of merit.

Your body is a jewel worth beholding,
your good words a jewel worth hearing,
your Dharma a jewel worth practicing;
so you are a mine of jewels.

You are an island for those swept off by the flood,
a protector for the wounded,
a refuge for those tormented by fear of existence,
a guide for those who seek liberation.

Because your discipline is pure, you are the supreme vessel;
you are a fertile field, rich in fruit;
because you benefit all living beings,
you are also the best of friends.

Because you help, you are a dear friend;
because your company is pleasant, you are beloved;
because you are at peace, you are wholly pleasing;
possessing every good quality, you are worthy of worship.

Because you are without wrongdoing, you are agreeable to the mind;
because your body and speech are fine, you bring delight;
because you accomplish every aim, your glory is vast;
as the excellent ground of good qualities, you are auspicious.

The Praise of Aspiration, the ninth chapter.

You urge on those who lag behind;
you put a stop to those who rob and plunder;
you set the crooked straight;
you spur on those who move slowly.

The tamed you set upon the path;
toward the wild you remain in equanimity;
therefore you are the unsurpassed and supreme
charioteer who tames beings.

You are tender toward the wretched,
glad to help those who live in ease,
and compassionate toward those in want;
you wish to benefit all.

You are loving even toward enemies,
you help those who have fallen,
and you are kind to the violent;
you are a wondrously noble one.

If parents are held as gurus
because they help us,
then you, who help far more,
what need is there to say that you are a guru?

Because you did not look to your own aims,
you became as though your own enemy;
and for those who dwell in darkness at the edge of the abyss,
you have become like a protecting fence.

To bring benefit in both worlds
and to pass beyond the world,
you made the light of wisdom shine
upon a world that had fallen into darkness.

In the enjoyment of sense objects,
the ways of gods and humans differ,
but since the enjoyment of the Dharma is shared by both,
with you there is no distinction.

Blessed One, with teachings
that suffer no flaw of birth, age, caste,
country or time,
you have bestowed benefit on all these.

Even to your hearers (śrāvakas), free from existence
and holding nothing in wonder,
the gods, palms joined, offer their aspirations
and honor them as great marvels.

How wonderful! The Buddha has appeared in splendor,
having become a canopy over saṃsāra;
through him, human beings too
have become what the gods aspire to be.

The Praise of Entering the Path, the tenth chapter.

Weariness and the loss of comfort,
keeping company with wicked people,
hardships and the press of crowds:
these faults you regarded as if they were virtues.
With a mind free of attachment
you strove for the welfare of beings.
Blessed One, how marvelous is this,
the very buddhahood of the buddhas!

You ate even ordinary food,
and at times you also fasted.
You walked even on perilous paths,
and you slept even on rough ground.
Protector, though you are the lord,
out of love for those to be trained
you changed your bearing, now forceful, now gentle,
and you changed your speech and your dress.

Though everyone, gladly, for their own ends,
pressed about you as though you were their servant,
Protector, never was there a time
when you lacked mastery of yourself.
Whoever urged you to anything, wherever,
you did not refuse their bidding,
yet never did it come about
that you strayed from your own excellent practice.

As earnestly as you work
to help those who do you harm,
so earnestly do people not help
even those who help them.
To a harmful enemy
you are a friend who brings benefit;
in one who always seeks out faults
you seek out only virtues.

Where you were invited
to food laced with poison and to fire,
you went there with affection,
and for you it turned to nectar.
With patience you subdued those who spoke harshly;
those who bore malice, with blessings;
those who slandered you, with truth;
and hatred you subdued with loving-kindness.

Even the many natures of people,
ingrained through habit since beginningless time,
you transformed in a single instant,
turning them back from the lower realms.

This is the eleventh chapter, the praise of difficult deeds.

You made the harsh gentle,
the stingy generous,
and the fierce tame:
this is your skill in means.
Nanda's senses brought to calm,
the one swollen with pride made to bow,
and Aṅgulimāla too made peaceful:
who would not be filled with wonder?

Satisfied by the taste of your Dharma,
those who rely on you give up beds of gold
and lie down contentedly
on beds of strewn grass.
Because you know the right time and disposition,
sometimes, even when asked, you do not speak,
while to others who are eager you teach,
and you even go to them to explain.

Like dye on a stainless cloth,
first, with talks on giving and the like,
you gladden the mind,
and afterward you teach the Dharma.
To draw afflicted beings
out of the depths of dreadful saṃsāra,
there is no means and no conduct
that you have not employed.

With many guises of body,
and with speech in each one's own tongue,
you acted, in this way and that,
for the varied dispositions of those to be trained.
And all of it was pure,
free of contradiction, worthy of offering,
majestic, and for gods and humans
nothing but benefit and honor.

For most people, speech and action
do not agree well,
but in you, who speak the truth,
the two are otherwise.
By your utter purity of self
you became a glorious field for beings;
a field of such a kind as this
is nowhere found in the three worlds.
In fearful, beginningless existence,
for the good of all beings,
long since wholly fallen into ruin,
you strove in this way.

This is the twelfth chapter, the praise of skill.

I do not think there is any practice
by which your kindness could be repaid.
Even those people who have passed into nirvāṇa
have not repaid it.
Since those who abide in your Dharma
work only for their own aim,
what repayment have they made
to you, who wore yourself out for their sake?

You are the one who keeps watch, wakeful,
over the mindstreams of sleeping beings;
for those who have become heedless
you are a good friend who is heedful.
You explained how to destroy the afflictions,
you revealed the deceits of Māra,
you declared the dreadful nature of saṃsāra,
and you showed the direction free of fear.

Of what one who is compassionate and intent on benefit
ought to do for beings,
what else is there, tell me,
that you have not done?
If these qualities could be
handed over to another,
you would surely give them to all,
even to Devadatta.

Therefore, instructing beings,
you, protector of beings,
declared with certainty that here
no one acts on another's behalf.
Out of love for the worlds,
you taught the true Dharma on earth for a long time,
and you brought forth many excellent disciples
able to benefit the three worlds.

You personally trained the multitude of disciples,
up to Subhadra, the very last.
Now what debt to beings
could still remain for you?
For by the vajra-like samādhi
you ground your body like sesame seeds,
and even at the very end
you did not let your hard practice lapse.

"This form body and these teachings
were for others' sake alone":
for the world that finds this hard to believe,
you showed your passing into nirvāṇa.
Therefore you entrusted the whole dharmakāya
to the foremost of your heirs,
broke up your form body like sesame seeds,
and passed completely into nirvāṇa.

How wondrous your abiding, how wondrous your conduct,
how wondrous your virtues, how wondrous your form!
Among the qualities of the Buddha
there is none that is not a great marvel.
You bring benefit, you are lovely to behold,
your body, speech and activity are peaceful,
yet see the dreadful delusion
of those who bitterly hate even you!

Ocean of merit, treasury of jewels,
source of virtues, mass of Dharma:
it is right to pay homage
even to those beings who bow to you.
Protector, though your virtues are not exhausted,
my own strength has run dry,
and so, because I am afraid,
I stop, as one who holds his breath.

Your own essential nature
is measureless and inconceivable,
inexpressible and beyond number;
surely you alone know it.

Just as a great lake, though its waters are not drained,
fully soothes the torment of people,
so, though I have voiced no more than a fraction of your virtues,
I am supremely glad at heart.
Through the fruit that arises from this virtuous deed,
undertaken with confidence born of faith in the Sage,
may the minds of beings, churned by the swirling red storm of wrong thoughts,
come to rest in their natural state.

This is the thirteenth chapter, the praise of being free of debt.

The Praise Called the One Hundred and Fifty, composed by the master, the great scholar, the brahmin Aśvaghoṣa, is complete.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

In the verse on the human state, the line rendered "uneasy at the opening it may give to karma" is obscure; ཀླགས is read as གླགས, "opportunity, opening." In the verse comparing worldly things with the Buddha's qualities, རྟོགས་མ་མཆིས is read as ཐོགས་མ་མཆིས, "without obstruction," which matches the "hindered and obscured" of the preceding verse.  "You made even the musician cast down his art at your feet" is read as an allusion to the Buddha's taming of the gandharva musician Supriya, whose pride in his lute-playing he overcame. The Tibetan wording is compressed.

In the verse on dwelling amid the crowd, the phrase rendered "its time having come" is uncertain. It may instead mean that compassion "overstepped its time."

In the verse beginning "Most of it is pleasing," the line rendered "A little is pleasing only by your art" is uncertain. It probably contrasts a few deliberately corrective words with the generally sweet ones.  In the ninth chapter, the line rendered "Your heirs bring contentment" reads literally "by your sons (or heirs) one is gladdened." In a series otherwise about the Buddha himself, the Indian original may have referred to his birth, but the Tibetan as written has "heirs," and that has been kept. In the last verse of the tenth chapter, the phrase rendered "having become a canopy over saṃsāra" is obscure in the Tibetan; the rendering follows the most natural reading of the words. "The dust and the darkness" in the seventh chapter likely corresponds to rajas and tamas, passion and dullness.  The colophon names the author as the brahmin Aśvaghoṣa. This hymn is generally attributed to Mātṛceṭa, and Tibetan tradition often identifies the two.

"The one swollen with pride" may be a proper name: the brahmin Mānastabdha, whom the Buddha humbled. It is translated descriptively because the identification is not certain.

The words rendered "strewn grass" (beds contrasted with beds of gold), "do not agree well" (of speech and action), and "my strength" and "holds his breath" in the closing verse involve uncommon or doubtful wording, and these renderings follow the evident sense of the verses.

Here dharmakāya, entrusted to the Buddha's heirs, refers to the body of his teaching.
