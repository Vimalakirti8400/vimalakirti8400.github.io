The Noble One Hundred and Eight Names of Sarvanīvaraṇaviṣkambhin, Together with the Dhāraṇī-Mantra, known in Tibetan as Phakpa Dribpa Tamche Nampar Selwé Tsen Gya Tsa Gyepa Zung Ngak Dang Chepa, is a short scriptural work devoted to the bodhisattva whose Sanskrit name, Sarvanīvaraṇaviṣkambhin, means "Remover of All Obscurations." It opens with homage to this great being and a brief mantra, after which the Tathāgata Vairocana stretches out his right hand, praises the bodhisattva for bringing forth his dhāraṇī-mantra, and explains the benefits it brings to beings. Vairocana says that anyone who hears it gains roots of virtue as vast as if planted before countless buddhas, and becomes free of obscurations simply by having it reach their ears. The work is Toh 880 in the Kangyur, where it belongs among the dhāraṇī texts, the class of scriptures centered on sacred formulas and the recitation of names for protection, purification, and spiritual merit.

The Noble One Hundred and Eight Names of Sarvanīvaraṇaviṣkambhin, Together with the Dhāraṇī-Mantra

Homage to the bodhisattva, the great being Sarvanīvaraṇaviṣkambhin.

oṃ amṛta bindu ja ba mahā su kha svāhā

Then the Blessed One, the Tathāgata Vairocana, stretched out his right hand, gladdened the bodhisattva, the great being Sarvanīvaraṇaviṣkambhin, and said: "You who possess a host of precious qualities, by bringing forth this dhāraṇī-mantra of yours you produce great happiness for sentient beings. Excellent, excellent!

"O son of the Victors, whoever hears this dhāraṇī-mantra will come to possess roots of virtue produced in the presence of as many tathāgatas as there are grains of sand in ten Ganges rivers. Whoever has it reach their ears will also become free from obscurations. If, before a stupa containing relics, on the eighth or the fifteenth day of the waxing fortnight, one fasts and keeps the poṣadha observance, makes offerings of perfumes, incense, lamps, flowers, garlands, ointments and so forth, circumambulates and recites it, one will obtain whatever excellent thing one wishes, and will be freed from all evil deeds, from suffering, and from all fears. If one makes offerings of perfumes and the like to the bodhisattva Sarvanīvaraṇaviṣkambhin and recites it, he will appear in one's dreams, fulfill all one's aspirations, and dispel all afflictions and suffering."

Then Great Vajra Flower Crown, together with the Lord of the Three Worlds, rose from his seat in the midst of that great circle of the assembly, bowed to the great bodhisattva Sarvanīvaraṇaviṣkambhin, and praised him with one hundred and eight names:

The speaker is introduced as "Great Vajra Flower Crown, together with the Lord of the Three Worlds." This is uncertain. The Tibetan may name one figure or two. The first element (with thod, "crown, headdress" or "skull") could not be securely identified with a Sanskrit name, so it is translated literally.

In the twenty-second stanza, the line rendered "holding the vajra weapon" reads irregularly in the Tibetan (mtshon pa snams, likely for mtshon bsnams, "holding a weapon"). The evident reading is translated.

In the seventh stanza, lha chen is rendered by its Sanskrit form Mahādeva, an epithet of Śiva.  The phrase rendered "the maṇḍala of great splendor and light" (Tibetan 'byor chen snang ba) may allude to Vairocana, the "Illuminator," whose maṇḍala frames the text. The reading is uncertain.

"Keeping to a fixed place" (Tibetan nges par gnas byas) could also mean "remaining steadfast" in the practice.

"Possesses the great tantra" (Tibetan rgyud chen ldan) could also be read as "of great lineage" or "of a great mental continuum."
