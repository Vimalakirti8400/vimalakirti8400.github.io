The Noble Teaching on the Perfection of Wisdom Requested by Suvikrāntavikrama—known in Sanskrit as the Ārya Suvikrāntavikrama Paripṛcchā Prajñāpāramitā Nirdeśa and in Tibetan as ’Phags pa rab kyi rtsal gyis rnam par gnon pas zhus pa shes rab kyi pha rol tu phyin pa bstan pa—is a Mahāyāna sūtra belonging to the vast Prajñāpāramitā, or Perfection of Wisdom, literature. Set at Vulture Peak's Bamboo Grove near Rājagṛha, it opens with the Buddha surrounded by a great assembly of monks and bodhisattvas, before the bodhisattva Suvikrāntavikrama comes forward to pose questions that occasion the Buddha's teaching on the profound nature of wisdom's perfection. As is typical of this genre, the text likely explores themes of emptiness, non-attachment, and the bodhisattva path through dialogue between the Buddha and the questioning bodhisattva. This work is catalogued as Toh 14 in the Kangyur, placing it among the canonical discourses attributed directly to the Buddha, and it forms part of the broader Prajñāpāramitā section that constitutes one of the foundational bodies of Mahāyāna scripture.

Homage to all buddhas, bodhisattvas, and noble hearers.

Thus did I hear. At one time the Blessed One was dwelling at Rājagṛha, in the Karanda Bamboo Grove, together with a great saṅgha of twelve hundred and fifty monks, and with bodhisattva great beings, immeasurable and innumerable, who had come together from various buddha realms, obstructed only by a single more birth. At that time the Blessed One, surrounded and faced by many hundreds of thousands of retinue members, was teaching the Dharma.

Then the bodhisattva great being named Suvikrāntavikrama, who had gathered in that very assembly and was seated there, rose from his seat, draped his upper robe over one shoulder, placed his right knee on the ground, and, bowing with palms joined toward the place where the Blessed One was, spoke thus to the Blessed One: "If, having asked, the Blessed One grants me the opportunity to have my question answered, I would like to ask the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly awakened Buddha, about a certain matter."

When he had spoken thus, the Blessed One said this to the bodhisattva great being Suvikrāntavikrama: "Suvikrāntavikrama, ask the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly awakened Buddha, whatever you wish, and I shall gladden your mind by answering whatever you ask."

When this had been said, the bodhisattva great being Suvikrāntavikrama spoke thus to the Blessed One: "Blessed One, as to what is called 'the perfection of wisdom, the perfection of wisdom' — Blessed One, to what extent is it called the perfection of wisdom of the bodhisattva great beings? Blessed One, how does a bodhisattva great being engage in the perfection of wisdom? Blessed One, how does the bodhisattva great being who engages in the perfection of wisdom bring the cultivation of the perfection of wisdom to full completion? Blessed One, how is it that when the bodhisattva great being cultivates the perfection of wisdom, the wicked Māra finds no opportunity, and moreover comes to understand all the deeds of Māra? Blessed One, abiding in what manner within the perfection of wisdom does the bodhisattva great being swiftly bring the qualities of omniscience to full completion?"

When this had been said, the Blessed One spoke thus to the bodhisattva great being Suvikrāntavikrama: "Suvikrāntavikrama, for the sake of the bodhisattva great beings, and in order that you may benefit many people, bring happiness to many people, show compassion for the world, and act for the sake, benefit, and happiness of the great multitude of gods and humans, and because you wish to illuminate this for the bodhisattva great beings of the present and of the future, you have entered upon this — it is excellent, excellent that you ask the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly awakened Buddha, about the perfection of wisdom!"







Not-knowing, too, should not be called gnosis. If not-knowing were to be called gnosis, then for that reason all ordinary childish beings would become possessors of gnosis. This overcoming through supreme power is the thorough knowing of reality exactly as it is; therefore it is called freedom. Apart from this freedom from not-knowing and freedom from knowing, nothing else whatsoever is apprehended. Since even wisdom itself is not apprehended, what need is there to speak of the not-knowing that is liberated from what has been exhausted? Therefore it is called "knowledge of exhaustion" — yet it is not as the words themselves suggest. For that which is knowledge of exhaustion has no verbal designation that can be applied to it at all; nevertheless, it is taught in this way as "ignorance exhausted" and "knowledge of exhaustion."

Suvikrāntavikrāmin, one who through examination comprehends that all phenomena are both inexhaustible and yet the object of the knowledge of exhaustion is free from the knowledge of exhaustion, and subsequently attains the limit of what has no exhaustion. This limitlessness is the limit of nirvāṇa — yet it is not as the words themselves suggest. All phenomena are the limit of nirvāṇa, which is to say, the limitless. The cutting off of all limits is called "the limit of nirvāṇa" — yet it is not as the words themselves suggest. The limit of nirvāṇa is not to be expressed as the cutting off of all conventional designations.

Suvikrāntavikrāmin, this teaching of the expanse of nirvāṇa is likewise not as it is taught in words. Because it genuinely transcends every kind of teaching, the expanse of nirvāṇa cannot be taught; that cutting off of all teaching is what is called "the expanse of nirvāṇa." What is called "the expanse of nirvāṇa" applies to the teaching of the definitive realization of the wisdom that transcends the world. Suvikrāntavikrāmin, it is taught that this expanse of nirvāṇa does not abide in any object and does not abide in any direction.

Ānanda, it is like this. For example, when the nāga king of the ocean becomes greatly joyful, at that time he sends down a great stream of rain from his own abode. Those nāgas who dwell in that abode, receiving that stream of rain, become joyful, and by that stream of rain they know well-being. Those who are his sons likewise bear that stream of rain, and by that stream of rain they attain supreme joy. Why is this? Ānanda, it is because other nāgas cannot bear that stream of rain; other nāgas do not know well-being through that stream of rain, and they do not become joyful through those streams of rain.

Ānanda, in just the same way, those who are especially attached to the Dharma jewel of the Tathāgata, and those foremost sons of the Tathāgata, the bodhisattva great beings, who have created roots of virtue, whose intention is vast, and who have arisen definitively from the way of the profound Dharma—these can bear this great rain of the way of the Tathāgata's Dharma. Because the reality of all phenomena cannot be shown, and because the knowing and seeing of all phenomena is not truly established, the perfection of wisdom is not truly established. Because becoming cool, peace, and utter peace are not truly established, the perfection of wisdom is not truly established. Because the ripening of sentient beings is not truly established, the perfection of wisdom is not truly established. Because the perfection of marks, the utterly pure buddha-field, the powers of a buddha, the fearlessnesses, and the eighteen unshared qualities of a buddha are not truly established, the perfection of wisdom is not truly established. Because nirvāṇa is not truly established, the perfection of wisdom is not truly established. Because all phenomena, virtuous and non-virtuous, up to and including all of them, are not truly established, the perfection of wisdom is not truly established. Likewise this should be applied extensively to all things.

Śāriputra, it is like this: space, for example, has no form, cannot be shown, has no substance, and is not truly established. Likewise the perfection of wisdom also has no form, cannot be shown, has no substance, and is not truly established.

Śāriputra, it is like this: a rainbow, for example, appears with a variety of colors everywhere, yet not a single color in it is truly established; none can be apprehended. Likewise, although the perfection of wisdom is present as a variety of teachings, the intrinsic nature of that teaching cannot be apprehended.

Śāriputra, it is like this: never before has anyone seen five fingers truly established in space. Śāriputra, likewise, never before has anyone seen the intrinsic nature of the perfection of wisdom as truly established.
