Praise in Eight Verses (Padāṣṭakastotra)

The Praise in Eight Verses, whose Sanskrit title is given in the translation as Padasya Aṣṭakastotra, is a short devotional hymn of homage in eight stanzas. After an opening salutation to Mañjuśrī in youthful form, it extols the Buddha through vivid similes: a face like the full moon, a voice like a lion's roar, the strength of an elephant, and a mind as steadfast as the king of mountains and as deep as the ocean. It goes on to celebrate his compassionate gaze upon gods, nāgas, yakṣas and humans, and his golden body adorned with the major marks, bowing in reverence to him as the Sugata. The text is Toh 1143 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the collection of praises, the class of hymns composed by Indian masters in honor of the Buddha and other awakened figures.

In the language of India: padasya aṣṭakastotraṃ
In the language of Tibet: Praise in Eight Verses

Homage to Mañjuśrī in youthful form.

Your face is like the full moon, your eyes like those of a noble bull,
your voice like a lion's roar, your strength like an elephant's,
your mind steadfast as the king of mountains and deep as the ocean:
master of mind, teacher of the lords of gods, I bow to you.

With three eyes, with fine eyes, with eyes as wide as the lotus,
you gaze upon yakṣas, nāgas and humans alike;
your long eyes grant liberation;
you who see with a thousand eyes, I bow to you.

Many supreme gods bow their heads to you;
your body blazes like refined gold, a body worthy of offerings,
a body adorned with the beauty of the excellent major marks:
to that Sugata I bow my head.

Your wisdom is vast, stainless as gold;
your understanding is renowned, your mind that of the learned;
O Buddha, your eyes are stainless as the sky:
hero of unwavering mind, to you I bow.

Supreme among speakers, you speak pleasing words full of meaning;
you speak the true meaning, you speak the meaning of the discipline;
lord of speech, your words cannot be shaken by opponents:
Victor who taught the four truths, I bow to you.

Not trained by another, you train others for the sake of their training;
you train them through purity, through abandoning the afflictions and the rest;
the Sage has acted well through his great conduct:
protector with even and beautiful teeth, I bow to you.

Your body is beautiful from immeasurable merit amassed,
a body blazing with a maṇḍala of light a fathom wide,
a body stainless like purified and refined gold,
a body freed from existence: Victor, I bow to you.

Your feet bear the supreme mark of the wheel upon their soles,
your feet are reverently bowed to by the heads of the lords of gods,
your beautiful feet are well adorned with webbed toes:
to your feet, marked with the śrīvatsa and the svastika, I bow.

The Praise in Eight Verses, composed by the layman Anantadeva, is complete.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The author's name is rendered as Anantadeva, the usual Sanskrit equivalent of the Tibetan; this identification is a reconstruction. In the second verse the phrase rendered "yakṣas, nāgas and humans alike" is terse in the Tibetan and could also be read as "gazing in a way unlike yakṣas and nāgas"; the reading chosen seems the more natural sense. In the sixth verse, "not trained by another" takes the Tibetan to mean that the Buddha attained his discipline without a teacher; it could also be read as "not training in any other way."
