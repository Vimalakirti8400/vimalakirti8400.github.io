An Instruction on the Six-Limbed Yoga (Ṣaḍaṅgayogopadeśa)

An Instruction on the Six-Limbed Yoga, known in Sanskrit as the Ṣaḍaṅgayoga Upadeśa and in Tibetan as Yenlak Druk gi Jorwé Mengak, is a brief verse manual of tantric meditation attributed to the Indian master Celupa and translated into Tibetan by the paṇḍita Mantraśrī and the translator Ra Chörab. Addressed to a practitioner who has received the four empowerments and seeks the state of great Vajradhara, it sets out the six-limbed yoga associated with the Kālacakra tradition: withdrawal, meditative concentration, breath control, retention, recollection and concentration, describing for each the posture, gaze and practice, the signs that arise, and the purpose it serves in uniting bliss and emptiness. It closes by promising buddhahood in this very life, or else through the six yogas of the intermediate state, and notes that it is an instruction for those free of desire. The work is Toh 1374 in the Tengyur, where it belongs among the commentarial and practice literature on the tantras.

In the language of India: ṣaḍaṅgayoga upadeśa. In the language of Tibet: An Instruction on the Six-Limbed Yoga.

Homage to glorious Great Bliss.

I bow to the body of great bliss,
And I shall explain the instruction on the six yogas.
The practitioner who has received the four empowerments,
In order to accomplish the state of great Vajradhara,
In a solitary place, upon a soft cushion,
Abiding as the chosen deity, should meditate on the six yogas.

First, withdrawal (pratyāhāra):
At night first, and by day afterward,
In a place without openings, beneath a high roof,
Straighten Mount Meru and gather in the four continents;
Turn sun and moon upward, and stir the banner of the tongue;
By binding the vajra, subdue the ocean;
Strike the great wind from above downward.
This is called the activity of the body,
And by it the winds are bound within.
Adopt the gaze of the great uṣṇīṣa.
Abiding in the pride of the deity,
Intent upon space, gaze into space.
The five signs, beginning with smoke, arise,
And one will come to realize emptiness.
By day, within, gaze to the west and the east;
One should not meditate at midday.
Binding the activity of the body as before,
Gaze without concepts into outer space.
The six signs, beginning with what is like a blaze, arise,
And signs such as awareness soaring in the sky.
When one is absorbed in withdrawal,
One is blessed by all the mantras.

To make that emptiness stable,
Meditate on meditative concentration (dhyāna), as before:
With the eyes gazing into space, meditate on emptiness.
By growing habituated to it, wisdom,
Thought and analysis, joy and bliss arise.
As its signs, the five superknowledges come forth.
When one is absorbed in meditative concentration,
One will obtain the five superknowledges,
And it becomes the cause of one-pointed awareness and the rest.
These two are virtuous in the beginning,
And serve as the support for seeing emptiness.

To seal emptiness with bliss,
Through breath control (prāṇāyāma), gather the winds under control.
Adopt the gaze of Amṛtakuṇḍalin.
Drawing the breath into the central channel,
Undertake the vajra recitation.
When by this bliss has fully arisen,
Practice the yoga of blazing caṇḍālī.
If it has not fully arisen, unite with a mudrā.
Body and mind are then sure to become great bliss.
One sees the heirs of the buddhas and the ḍākinīs,
And exhortations and prophecies will come.
There come to be nine, beginning with entering the vase.
Through breath control, with certainty,
One beholds the mind of awakening and the rest.

To make that stable, meditate on retention (dhāraṇā):
At the hubs of the four cakras,
Insert the winds and the rest inseparably, and meditate.
Signs such as lightness of the body arise,
And there come to be nine, beginning with abiding in the vase.
Through the power of constant retention,
Vajrasattva truly enters.
These two are virtuous in the middle.

To make bliss and emptiness inseparable and stable,
The meditation on recollection (anusmṛti) is this:
Meditate on bliss and emptiness made stable in the four places.
When one is thoroughly habituated, the view
Of the buddhas abides stable, without dispersing.
Light radiates from the body, and one sees the enjoyment and emanation bodies.
When one is absorbed in recollection,
A maṇḍala of light will arise.
Know the bodies, such as the traceless one.

To make that stable, meditate on concentration (samādhi):
In that very inseparability of bliss and emptiness
One should become thoroughly habituated.
Everything arises as bliss and emptiness and so forth,
And there come to be nine, beginning with spontaneous presence.
Merely by gaining mastery over concentration,
All obscurations will be cleansed away.
These two are virtuous at the end.

By growing habituated to conduct in this,
One attains buddhahood in this very life.
If it is not attained, meditate on the six yogas of the intermediate state.
This is the instruction for those free of desire;
Know that the six yogas for those with desire are found elsewhere.
By the virtue of having done this, may I and others
Attain the state of great Vajradhara.

The Instruction on the Six-Limbed Yoga, composed by Celupa, is complete. It was translated by the paṇḍita Mantraśrī and the translator Ra Chörab.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The homage reads "dpal mad," which makes no sense as written; it has been taken as "glorious" (dpal), the extra syllable being a probable slip. The bodily images in the posture verses (Mount Meru for the spine, the four continents for the limbs, sun and moon for the eyes, the "banner of the tongue," the bound vajra subduing the "ocean") are coded yogic instructions and are translated literally. In the line on daytime practice, "nang du" is rendered "within"; it may stand for "nangs," "in the morning," in keeping with the Kālacakra practice of gazing west in the morning and east in the afternoon with one's back to the sun. In "de rtag mngon shes lnga," "rtag" (always) is read as "rtags" (signs), which the context requires. "Kun 'dar" is the central channel (avadhūtī). The pairs "these two" refer to the six yogas grouped as withdrawal and dhyāna, prāṇāyāma and dhāraṇā, anusmṛti and samādhi.
