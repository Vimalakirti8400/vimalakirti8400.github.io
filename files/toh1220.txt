The Pith Instruction of Glorious Hevajra Like the Tip of a Lamp Flame (Śrīhevajrapradīpaśūlopamāvavādaka-nāma)

The Instruction of Glorious Hevajra Like the Tip of a Lamp Flame, known in Sanskrit as Śrī Hevajra Pradīpa Śūlopama Avavādaka and in Tibetan by a title beginning Dpal gyi rdo rje'i mar me'i rtse mo lta bu'i, is a short tantric pith instruction on the practice of Hevajra. Opening with homage to Hevajra and to the goddess Nairātmyā, the author presents teachings received from the master Nairātmyavajra and describes them as joining empowerment with practical instruction. The text addresses a yogin who has received the Hevajra empowerment, keeps the tantric commitments, and aspires to the state of the great Vajradhara, guiding him through preparations such as finding a suitable place, balancing the body's constitution and elements, and settling into a relaxed posture before meditation. It is Toh 1220 in the Tengyur, where it belongs among the commentarial and practice works of the tantra section associated with the Hevajra cycle.

In the language of India: śrī hevajra prādīpa śūlopama bābādaka nāma
In the language of Tibet: The Instruction of Glorious Vajra Like the Tip of a Lamp Flame.

Homage to glorious Hevajra.

Having paid homage to Nairātmyā,
Who has become the summit of the essence of wisdom,
From the venerable Nairātmyavajra
I shall explain the pith instruction of Hevajra,
Like the tip of a lamp flame,
That joins empowerment with instruction.

A yogin who has the empowerment of glorious Hevajra, keeps the commitments, and wishes to accomplish the great Vajradhara first seeks out an excellent place and the other conditions. He then brings the constitution and the elements of the body into balance. With the constitution thus wholly at ease, he relaxes his body on a comfortable seat and lies down at ease. Through its various accumulations of meditative concentration, this is the nature of the path of accumulation.

Then, when the first light of dawn appears, he rises well. He properly puts on clean clothes, brings the warmth of the constitution and the elements into harmony, and performs the rites of bathing and of waking. Then he sits properly on a well-cushioned seat and properly balances the body. The mind too is brought into balance. Because what has been done up to this point applies one to meditative concentration, it is the nature of the path of joining.

Then one visualizes oneself as glorious Hevajra with one face and two arms, holding a five-pronged vajra in the right hand and a skull cup (kapāla) in the left. Then one visualizes a blue hūṃ on a sun and a moon at one's own heart. Light rays radiate outward from it. They burn away the conceptual thoughts of the whole outer world that is the container and of all the female beings of the inner world that is its contents. As the rays gather back, one visualizes the mother Nairātmyā, the wisdom aspect, as the nature of the container world. In the same way all male beings are burned away. As the rays gather back, one visualizes oneself as glorious Heruka, the nature of the world of the contents, and so others are purified.

Then one visualizes oṃ between a moon and a sun at the mother's heart. By its light the mother herself melts into light, and one visualizes her dissolving into the father's heart. Seeing this very meaning, never seen before, is the nature of the path of seeing.

Then, by the light of hūṃ between the sun and moon at the father's heart, the father himself melts into light. One visualizes only the sun above, which is method. The mother is the moon below, which is wisdom. One visualizes that from the seed syllable hūṃ between the sun and moon come the limb letters, the eighty consonants, with their heads turned downward. In the same way one visualizes the minor-limb letters, the thirty-two vowels, with their heads turned upward. The syllable hūṃ is visualized as method and wisdom inseparable. The sun and moon are the size of a pea. The hūṃ is the size of a white mustard seed. The vowels and consonants are the size of the tip of a hair. Meditating on the drop in this way is the nature of the path of meditation.

Then light rays come forth from that hūṃ. One visualizes the consonants melting into light and dissolving into the vowels, and the vowels also melting and dissolving into the moon. In the same way one visualizes the sun dissolving into the moon, and the moon dissolving into the hooked stroke at the foot of the syllable. The hūṃ too is gradually let go. Meditating in stages like this is the subtle yoga. Then one fixes the mind on a drop of nectar, white and glistening, the size of the tip of a hair. Then one fixes it on the nāda, a hair's length, the size of the finest particle. Then one fixes the mind on mind alone. Because the relinquishing of things has been carried to its end, this is the nature of the path of culmination.

Then one meditates on the mind as luminosity. Here the container and its contents are of the nature of father and mother. Father and mother are of the nature of the drop. The drop is of the nature of the subtle, and the subtle is of the nature of mind. Mind is, from the very beginning, of the nature of emptiness, free of all extremes such as beginning and end. To meditate on it in this way is the great Vajradhara, the nature of the result of the five paths.

These stages above should each be held for a year or for a month, according to the yogin's particular inclination, so that the mind becomes stable. After that there are also those who train in the father and mother and meditate in stages. There are also those who meditate on both stages complete within a single session, and some who enter the higher stage all at once. One should therefore recognize each person's mind and give the instruction accordingly.

The faults are two: dullness and agitation. The method for clearing away dullness is to uplift the mind, call to mind the wisdom of the Buddha's omniscience, and then meditate again. Sleepiness is cleared away by sprinkling water on the face or by anointing it, and then one meditates again. If one is agitated, one should stir up weariness toward that very object, the suffering of saṃsāra, and meditate again. If even that does not calm it, one should shift the focus away from that object, direct the mind onto mind itself, and meditate.

There are four sessions. While the mind is still weak, the length of the session should be measured with a stick. One should meditate in short sessions with long breaks between them. When the mind has grown a little stronger, the length of the session should be measured with a water clock. In the same way, once the mind has grown stronger, experience has been found and the mind has gained strength, the boundaries of the session should be measured by shifts, by watches, by days and nights, and by years and months. One should meditate for long periods and keep the breaks short.

As for the signs, first a nonconceptual state of the constitution arises. Then old illnesses leave one's body and new ones do not arise. One turns away naturally from bodily misdeeds and engages skillfully in virtue. Bodily signs such as trembling appear.

One who is especially devoted to outer yoga should make offerings, praises and torma offerings before each of the four sessions, and then meditate.

Received from the lineage of gurus,
Joining empowerment with instruction,
I have composed this instruction, the Tip of the Lamp Flame.
By whatever virtue is gained through it,
May all wandering sentient beings
Attain the luminosity of emptiness.

This completes the instruction of glorious Hevajra like the tip of a lamp flame, composed by the master Saroruhavajra. It was translated and edited by the Indian preceptor Gayādhara and the lama Śākya Yeshé.

The Tibetan title reads "Glorious Vajra," while the colophon has "Glorious Hevajra." The title has been translated as it stands. "From the venerable Nairātmyavajra" renders "zhal snga nas," which may name the author's teacher or may be an honorific way of addressing the deity. The text gives eighty consonants and thirty-two vowels, and these numbers are kept as written. "Does not calm it" reads "ma zhin" as "ma zhi na," which is an evident slip. "Shifts" (pho ba) probably refers to the shifts of the breath between the nostrils used as units of time. "Anointing" renders "bsgos pa," which could also mean rubbing or smearing the face.
