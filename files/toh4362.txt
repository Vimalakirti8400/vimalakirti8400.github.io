Memorandum on the Enumerations of Dharmas

The Memorandum on the Enumerations of Dharmas, in Tibetan Chos kyi rnam grangs kyi brjed byang, is a compact handbook of Buddhist doctrinal categories, Toh 4362 in the Tengyur. Its colophon credits its composition to the great translator Paltsek Rakṣita and others, which makes it one of the rare works of early Tibetan authorship preserved in the Tengyur rather than a translation from an Indian original. As its opening explains, it gathers in brief the condensed meaning of many texts, such as the sūtras, the treatises, the Perfection of Wisdom in One Hundred Thousand Lines and the Yogācāra works, so that those unable to hear many teachings can grasp the meaning of scripture with little trouble. Moving through numbered lists in the manner of an Abhidharma glossary, it explains such topics as the aggregates, elements and sense bases, the twenty-two faculties, the six causes, four conditions and five results, the eight sufferings, the eight unfavorable states, the sixteen aspects of the Four Noble Truths, and the four meditative concentrations of the Great Vehicle, often illustrating them with vivid everyday examples.

Herein is contained the Memorandum on the Enumerations of Dharmas.

The Memorandum on the Enumerations of Dharmas.

Homage to the entire assembly of noble ones.

This work called the Memorandum on the Enumerations of Dharmas gathers, in brief and in well-turned words, the condensed meaning drawn from many different texts, such as the sūtras, the treatises, the Perfection of Wisdom in One Hundred Thousand Lines, and the Yogācāra. It is taught so that persons who are unable to hear many teachings may understand the meaning of the scriptures with little trouble, and so that they may become skilled in the characteristics by which they take apart their own bodies.

Now, the passage beginning "What is the aggregate of form?" teaches the characteristics of the aggregates, the elements and the sense sources as they appear in the Yogācāra. It teaches the meaning of "which, among the five aggregates, is the aggregate of form?"

On the words "Whatever form there is, all of it is the four great elements and what has the four great elements as its cause": "Whatever form there is, all of it" means all that is designated as form, however much there may be: long, short, round and square form; blue, yellow and so on; past, future and present form; near, far, fine, coarse and so on. The four great elements are the four: fire, water, earth and wind. "What has the four great elements as its cause" means what is born from those elements, or what has come about from them; that is, the forms that have arisen from that cause. These are the five faculties, the eye and the rest, and the five objects of those faculties, from visible form up to the tangible, making ten; non-informative form, making eleven; and the four great elements. Thus, when the aggregate of form is analyzed, there are in general fifteen.

Form is of three kinds: the demonstrable and obstructive, the non-demonstrable and obstructive, and the neither demonstrable nor obstructive. Among these, color, shape and so on, which have the character of the four great elements, are both demonstrable and obstructive. The five faculties, the eye and the rest, together with sound, smell, taste and the tangible, are non-demonstrable and obstructive. Non-informative form is neither demonstrable nor obstructive. It is posited as form because it arises from causes that possess form, namely informative form and the four meditative concentrations. Such are the characteristics of the aggregate of form.

The passage "What is the aggregate of feeling? It is the three kinds of experience" is stated. To experience the three, pleasure, suffering, and what is neither pleasure nor suffering, is called feeling. Here, "Pleasure is that which, when it ceases, one wishes to meet with again" means that when pleasure arises with body and mind at ease, one does not wish to be parted from it, and even when parted from it, one wishes to meet with it again. "Suffering is that which, when it arises, one wishes to be parted from" means that when some harm arises to body or mind, one does not wish to keep company with it, and so one wishes to be parted from it. "What is neither suffering nor pleasure is that which, when it arises, one wishes for neither" means that since there is nothing harming body or mind, there is no wish to be parted from it; and since there is nothing that brings special pleasure or benefit to body or mind, there is no wish to keep company with it or meet with it either. This is what "one wishes for neither" means.

The passage "What is the aggregate of perception? It is the grasping of signs in objects, which is of three kinds: small, great and immeasurable" is stated. Objects are the phenomena that serve as objects of focus, such as visible forms and sounds. Grasping signs refers to the many different particulars of color and shape. The small is that which focuses on the inferior desire realm. The great is that which focuses on the form realm, from the first meditative concentration up to the fourth. The immeasurable is that which focuses on the formless realm, from the sphere of infinite space up to the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception. These are the three.

On "What is the aggregate of formations? It is the mental phenomena other than feeling and perception": since everything included in the aggregates, form and the rest, arises from the coming together of many causes and conditions, all of it may indeed be called formations; but here the term refers to the formations that are mental factors. Since feeling and perception were already taught above as aggregates of their own, they are set aside, and the term refers to those that come after them, from contact up to investigation.

The passage "Among these, what are the five that are omnipresent?" is stated, and from there the fifty-one formations associated with mind and the fourteen not associated with mind are taught one by one. Omnipresent means that they appear and operate within all states, the virtuous, the unvirtuous and the indeterminate.

"Contact is the discernment that comes from the meeting of the three" means this: from the meeting of the three, object, faculty and consciousness, the faculty too is altered in accordance with how the object is, pleasant and so on; consciousness discerns this and so gives rise to feeling. Therefore it is called contact.

"Attention is the engagement of the mind" means that it directs the mind toward the object to be focused on and turns it to face that object; therefore it is called the engagement of the mind.

"Feeling is the experiencing of the ripened result of the virtuous, the unvirtuous and the indeterminate" means that it experiences the three results of virtuous, unvirtuous and indeterminate actions: pleasure, suffering, and what is neither.

"Perception is the distinguishing of the particulars in the objects of consciousness" means that it clarifies and discerns, each on its own, the particulars of the different objects of focus of the consciousnesses of the five doors.

"Intention is the mental action that forms the mind toward good qualities, faults, and what is neither" means this: virtuous action is called good qualities, unvirtuous action is called faults, and indeterminate action is called neither. Intention rouses the mind and directs it toward these three kinds of action; for, just as a magnet makes iron move, it makes the mind engage its object of focus.

The passage "What are the five that are restricted to particular objects? Aspiration is the wish for a desired thing" is stated. Unlike the omnipresent ones, these do not operate in every state, virtuous, unvirtuous and indeterminate; they operate only on whatever is their own object, and so they are called restricted to particular objects. Whatever has become the object of the faculties and of consciousness is called a desired thing. To seek and pursue it in order to bring it into direct experience is called wishing, because one aspires toward it.

On "Resolve is the holding of an ascertained thing exactly as it has been ascertained": ascertainment applies only to the object of one's resolve, not to other objects on which one has not resolved. Moreover, to have examined something by way of direct perception and inference, and to have faith in the scriptural authority of the sūtras and treatises without doubt, is called ascertainment. Alternatively, to hold that the specific and general characteristics of phenomena are not permanent and not pleasant, but impermanent and so on, is called ascertainment.

On "Mindfulness is the non-forgetting of a familiar thing; it is the mind's expression": when one has previously met with something, experienced it, and kept company with it again and again, it is called familiar. Not forgetting a thing familiar in that way, and recalling it clearly, as though one were putting it into words, is called the mind's expression.

On "Meditative absorption is the one-pointedness of mind on a thing to be examined": the thing to be examined should be understood as the very reality that has not come about and not arisen, or else as the Four Truths, each taken one by one and again as fourfold. One-pointedness of mind is like twisting tight the tip of a thread and putting it through the eye of a needle: to fix the mind on the object of meditation without letting it stray elsewhere is called one-pointed meditative absorption.

On "Wisdom is the thorough discrimination of those very things; it is born of correct reasoning, of incorrect reasoning, or otherwise": wisdom is of three kinds, the wisdom of hearing, of reflection and of meditation. Further, that which enters into the teaching of the Buddha is called born of correct reasoning. That which enters into the teaching of the non-Buddhists (tīrthikas) is called born of incorrect reasoning. That which, though taught by neither, is by nature greatly perceptive is called otherwise. "The thorough discrimination of those very things" refers to the very object of meditative absorption mentioned above. Knowledge born of correct reasoning ascertains without error the specific and general characteristics of phenomena: that ultimately there is no arising and no ceasing, and that conventionally earth has the characteristic of hardness, water of wetness, and so on. Knowledge born of incorrect reasoning imagines and meditates that what is without self has a self, which is like seeing horns grow. Wisdom of the other kind is so called because, without being trained by anyone, one is naturally sharp of mind and skilled in all activities, such as work to be done and speech.

The passage "What are the eleven virtuous ones? Faith is conviction and clarity of mind regarding action, results, the truths and the Jewels" is stated. Action is virtuous, unvirtuous and indeterminate. Results are what that action produces: pleasure, suffering, and what is neither. The truths are four: the truth of suffering, of origin, of cessation and of the path; faith is to be convinced that these exist. The Jewels are three. The Buddha is perfect in realization and in abandonment. The Dharma is the path that brings liberation from all suffering. The Saṅgha, engaged in abandoning the afflictions, is the field of merit. Knowing these qualities of theirs, to have a mind clear with confidence is called faith.

The passage "Shame is the avoidance of wrongdoing with oneself or the Dharma as the standard" is stated. Taking oneself as the standard means refraining from wrong because one thinks: "I hold the way of life of one of noble standing, such as a renunciant or a brahmin, so these unworthy evil deeds are not something for me to do." Taking the Dharma as the standard means refraining because one thinks: "If I commit evil deeds, sinful and unvirtuous actions, their result will be that I fall into the three lower realms and meet with every kind of suffering, and that will not do." Wrongdoing is something one cannot bear to mention or speak of in words, something not even fit to be uttered, an object of reproach; to fear such a thing is called avoidance.

"Embarrassment is the avoidance of wrongdoing with the world as the standard" means this: thinking, "If I commit these things that are not mine to do, such as taking life, others will reproach and censure me, and it will be bad if I become known for these faults," one fears this and refrains; that is called avoidance.

On "Non-attachment is the antidote to attachment; it is disenchantment and not grasping": the objects of attachment are the body and possessions. Their antidotes, the antidotes of the three, attachment, aversion and delusion, are the unattractive, loving-kindness and dependent origination, applied one to each in order. Disenchantment and not grasping means that, knowing that the end of enjoying and dwelling in the body and possessions is the arising and increase of every kind of suffering, such as birth, aging, sickness and death, one gives them up and does not engage in them.

The passage "Non-aversion is the antidote to aversion; it is loving-kindness" is stated. The objects of aversion are enemies, poisons and weapons. The thought "I shall destroy, ruin and annihilate them" is called aversion, and simply not acting with such intention and application is also called its antidote. Here, however, since aversion does not arise in one who has a mind of loving-kindness toward all sentient beings, like the love for a cherished child, loving-kindness is the antidote, and so it is called non-aversion.

The passage "Non-delusion is the antidote to delusion; it is the correct understanding of what is correct" is stated. To view phenomena as existing as dharmas and persons, as real things, and to conceive them in the inverted ways, as permanent, pleasant, self and pure, is called conception. Its antidote is non-inverted wisdom accompanied by diligence and meditative absorption, which abandons the inversion of conceiving dharmas, persons and so on as existent and knows them as empty, selfless and so on; this is called the correct understanding of what is correct.

The passage "Diligence is the antidote to laziness; it is the mind's enthusiasm for virtue" is stated. Laziness is of two kinds: applying oneself to what is inferior, and indolence.

Those who perform unvirtuous actions such as taking life may exert themselves with great effort, yet that is not diligence; it is called the laziness of applying oneself to what is inferior. Lying down, lounging and the like are called the laziness of indolence, whose characteristic is not engaging in listening to the Dharma, reflecting, meditating and so forth. Its antidote is this: whatever belongs to the side of virtue one undertakes, from listening and reflecting up to the grounds and the perfections, to strive at it without interruption and without regard for life and body is the diligence that is enthusiasm for virtue.

The passage "Pliancy is the antidote to dysfunction; it is the serviceability of body and mind" is stated. When the body wants to lie down and lounge, is stiff, uncomfortable and sluggish, and the mind has fallen under the sway of slackness and excitement, or of dullness and torpor, so that it is unserviceable, that is called dysfunction, because through it one goes into saṃsāra and the states of the lower realms. Its antidote is for the body to be light and at ease, without weariness and sluggishness, without discouragement and fatigue, and for the mind to remain clear and one-pointed, free of slackness and excitement, of dullness and agitation. When body and mind have become like this, it is called serviceability and pliancy.

The passage "Heedfulness is the antidote to heedlessness; it is, while abiding in those from non-attachment up to diligence, to abandon unvirtuous phenomena and also to cultivate the virtuous phenomena that are their antidote" is stated. Heedfulness means that, with the three, non-attachment, non-aversion and non-delusion, as its cause, and established in diligence, one does not let the mind run loose among unvirtuous phenomena but turns it back; that is the abandoning of the unvirtuous. Cultivating virtuous phenomena means this: the worldly and the world-transcending virtues, such as those of the stage of conduct through aspiration and of the first ground and the rest, are the antidotes; to familiarize the mind with them again and again is called cultivation.

The passage "Equanimity is, while abiding in those very ones from non-attachment up to diligence, the attaining of evenness of mind, of the mind's resting naturally, and of the mind's spontaneity, by which afflicted phenomena are cleared away and one abides free of affliction" is stated. Of the three kinds of equanimity, this is the equanimity of formations. Here too, as above, the three, non-attachment and the rest, are the cause of equanimity, and diligence is its basis. Abiding in that, the beginning, middle and end of equanimity come about. At the beginning, the moment one has found freedom from slackness and excitement is called evenness of mind. Then, abiding while still wary that slackness and excitement may return is called the mind's resting naturally. Then, having meditated for a long time and become habituated, abiding with no slackness and excitement arising and with no wariness is called spontaneity. In a person who has attained equanimity of this nature, a mind of attachment and the like cannot arise; that it does not arise is called the clearing away of the afflictions. And when one abides thus in meditative concentration with a pure mind, that very state is also abiding free of affliction.

The passage "Harmlessness is the antidote to harmfulness; it is compassion" is stated. To inflict all kinds of harm, such as killing, threatening, beating and striking, is harmfulness. Its antidote is compassion. For when one has it, one gives up even one's own life and body for the sake of sentient beings without regret; what need, then, to speak of not doing harm? It is because one does not mistreat sentient beings.

"What are the six afflictions?" These are attachment, anger, pride, ignorance, view and doubt.

On "Attachment is clinging and strong attachment to the five appropriated aggregates": the five, from the defiled aggregate of form through the defiled aggregates of feeling, perception, formations and consciousness, arise from the four appropriations as their cause: desire, views, moral discipline and ascetic observances, and the assertion of a self. They are therefore designated the appropriated aggregates, just as a fire that arises from grass is called a grass fire. Being unable in the present time to part from those aggregates is clinging. Wishing to take up the five aggregates in other lifetimes is strong attachment. For just as oil, once it has soaked into cloth, is hard to remove, so attachment to the five qualities of sense desire and to one's own aggregates is hard to be parted from and abandon, even with great effort in listening, reflecting, meditating and so on.

"Anger is malice toward sentient beings" means the intention to inflict all kinds of harm, such as beating and killing, on others such as enemies. Because one is by nature harsh of mind and full of malice, one always abides in suffering and never touches happiness.

On "What is pride? It is of seven kinds": all seven kinds of pride alike have this characteristic, that, viewing the aggregates of one's own body as self and mine, one thinks, "I am superior to others," and so is without respect and haughty toward everyone. Among their varieties, pride is this: in comparison with those who are lesser in ability, looks, lineage, wealth and so on, one may indeed be greater, yet on that very basis one thinks, "I am the greater." Great pride is this: when oneself and another are alike and equal in wealth and everything else, one thinks, "Beyond that, I am greater in some few respects, such as moral discipline." Pride beyond pride is this: although one is lesser than others in lineage and so on, one thinks, "I am greater than others." The pride of thinking "I am" is so called because, although in the appropriated aggregates there is no real self or mine, the mind presumes and is inflated, thinking that a real self and mine exist. Manifest pride is this: although one has not attained the state of a noble one or the special meditative concentrations, on the strength of having a mere trace of mental serviceability one thinks, "I have attained the ground of a noble one." The pride of thinking oneself slightly less is this: although between oneself and another the other is greater by far, one does not take it so, but thinks, "Between us there is merely a slight difference in greatness." Wrong pride is this: although one is full of all kinds of faults and has not even the slightest good quality, one is inflated and haughty, thinking, "I possess every good quality, such as moral discipline."

On "Ignorance is not knowing action, results, the truths and the Jewels; it is either innate or imputed": action is virtuous, unvirtuous and indeterminate. Results are pleasure, suffering, and what is neither. The truths are the four: the truth of suffering, of origin, of cessation and of the path. The Jewels are the Buddha, the Dharma and the Saṅgha; these are the three. Not knowing whether these lack good qualities or possess them is ignorance. Not knowing, by one's very nature, that they exist or that they possess good qualities is innate ignorance. Not knowing them through the mistaken and erroneous teaching of others is called imputed ignorance.

Of the five views, the view of the perishing collection is this: those five aggregates perish moment by moment and are a collection in which many substances have been gathered; although there is no real self and nothing real that is mine, one conceives that there is a real self and something real that is mine. The view that holds to extremes is the view of the two, permanence and annihilation: either one holds that the self is like a snake that sheds its skin, or one holds that it utterly ceases to be, just as a fire dies out when the wood is used up. Wrong view is denial: saying that none of these exist, action, results, the truths, the Jewels, this world and the next, and so on. Holding views as supreme is this: the three views mentioned above, together with the aggregates of the body that are the basis of those views, are held to be excellent and foremost, as in "This alone is good," and one thinks that there is nothing beyond it. Holding moral discipline and ascetic observances as supreme is to think that by practicing a moral discipline that abandons the seven faults arising from body and speech, or the observances of the dog and the cow, or plucking out and shaving the hair and the like, one will become freed and liberated.

"Doubt is being of two minds about the truths and so on" means that one is divided in mind, thinking, "Do action, results, the truths and the Jewels exist or not? And if they do exist, do they have good qualities or not?" One finds no certainty, and so does not enter upon the side of virtue.

The passage "What are the twenty secondary afflictions? They are wrath, resentment, concealment, spite, envy, miserliness, deceit, dissimulation, self-satisfaction, harmfulness, shamelessness, lack of embarrassment, torpor, excitement, lack of faith, laziness, heedlessness, forgetfulness, distraction and non-vigilance; these are the twenty" is stated, and they are explained one by one below.

Of these, on "Wrath, which is based on immediate harm, is mental malice": it does not concern objects of wrath, such as enemies, that are past or future and out of sight; to think, toward those that are present and directly before one, "I shall do them wrong and harm them" is called wrath and malice.

"Resentment is holding on to it like a knot" means thinking about an enemy or the like who has harmed oneself or one's own circle, such as one's relatives, "Someday I will not let them go without having harmed them."

"Concealment is hiding one's own wrongdoing" means this: to have done evil, unvirtuous deeds that should not be done is called wrongdoing. It is so called because, when a spiritual friend says, "By doing these deeds that ought not to be done, you have incurred fault; confess these faults," one is unwilling to confess, saying, "I have no fault."

On "Spite is stinging with harsh words": to dig up the faults and hidden failings of others and speak fierce words is called harsh words. Because by them one cuts others to the heart, it is called stinging.

"Envy is the inner agitation of mind at the good fortune of others" means this: seeing the gains and honors of others and their good qualities such as learning and moral discipline, through attachment to these, aversion arises and the mind cannot bear it; this is called envy.

"Miserliness is what is opposed to generosity; it is mental clinging" means this: because attachment predominates, one is attached to the necessities of life and cannot bear to let them go. Being unable to make offerings to the Jewels and to give to the needy and so on is called being opposed to generosity. Gathering and hoarding even all one's useless and shabby belongings is called mental clinging.

"Deceit is showing what is not true in order to mislead others" means that, out of desire for profit, honor and gain, one feigns and puts on a show of possessing good qualities even though one has none.

"Dissimulation is crookedness of mind that has seized on a means of concealing one's faults" means this: fearing that others will recognize one's faults, such as feigning out of a desire for gain and honor, one speaks evasively, throwing one's talk to the wind, and makes both words and meaning roundabout. This is called the crooked means of concealing one's faults.

On "Self-satisfaction is the exhilaration of attachment to one's own good fortune; it is the mind's being taken over": good fortune means lineage, intelligence, wealth, ability, looks and the like. The exhilaration of attachment to it is the feeling of ease and the gladness of mind that come from having that good fortune. Being taken over means that the mind becomes giddy over one's wealth and the rest, one thinks, "These alone are enough," and does nothing else on the side of virtue; self-satisfaction refers to just this.

"Harmfulness is mistreating sentient beings" means this: because one lacks compassion and kindness, beating them with slaps, fists and the like, binding them, and inflicting all kinds of harsh treatment such as coercion is called mistreatment.

"Shamelessness is not avoiding wrongdoing with oneself as the standard" means this: being under the sway of predominant attachment, aversion and delusion is shamelessness. Wrongdoing is evil, unvirtuous action. Not avoiding it with oneself as the standard means engaging in evil deeds without thinking, "I am a son of good family, or a renunciant who has gone forth, so doing deeds like this is not for me."

On "Lack of embarrassment is not avoiding wrongdoing with others as the standard": not avoiding it with others as the standard is so called because, when one does the evil, unvirtuous deeds that ought not to be done, one has no fear of others' punishment and the like, of their censure and reproach, or of disgrace.

"Torpor is the unserviceability of the mind; it is sluggishness" means that, because delusion predominates, at the time of listening and reflecting the mind is unserviceable, since one lacks a mind that is clear with the distinctive quality of wisdom.

"Excitement is the non-pacification of the mind" means this: because attachment predominates, one follows after attractive signs, such as the play and laughter of former times, and is distracted, so that at the time of meditation it interrupts calm abiding.

On "Lack of faith is non-conviction and non-clarity of mind regarding action, results, the truths and the Jewels; it is the factor opposed to faith": action is virtuous, unvirtuous and indeterminate. Results are pleasure, suffering, and what is neither. The truths are the truth of suffering, of origin, of cessation and of the path; these are four. The Jewels are the three: the Buddha, the Dharma and the Saṅgha. Not being convinced that these exist, and not having a mind clear with confidence that they have good qualities, is meant, because without faith there is no application to the side of virtue.

"Laziness is the mind's lack of enthusiasm for virtue; it is the factor opposed to diligence" means this: one does not engage in the things on the side of virtue, such as listening, reflecting and meditating, but gives oneself over to the comfort of lying down, reclining and lounging; and because delusion predominates, one makes no effort at virtue. It is therefore called opposed to diligence and lack of enthusiasm.

"Heedlessness is, through attachment, aversion and laziness, not guarding the mind from the afflictions and not cultivating virtue" means this: with the three, attachment, aversion and delusion, as cause, and established in laziness, to fall under the sway of these four is heedlessness. To let the mind run loose after those four, attachment and the rest, and not turn it back, is not guarding the mind. Because one does not familiarize oneself again and again with the side of virtue, non-attachment and the rest, it is called not cultivating.

On "Forgetfulness is afflicted mindfulness; it is unclarity regarding virtue": mindfulness that is familiar with attachment and the like is afflicted. Because one has forgotten the correct instructions and has no mindfulness of the side of virtue, it is called unclarity and forgetfulness.

On "Distraction is the scattering of the mind toward the five qualities of sense desire, through a share of attachment, aversion and delusion": distraction is of six kinds: natural distraction, external distraction, internal distraction, the distraction of signs, the distraction of dysfunction, and the distraction of attention. Of these, natural distraction is the consciousnesses of the five doors, because by their very nature they face outward and so are unable to settle inwardly in equipoise. External distraction is when, while in meditative equipoise, one abandons the object of meditative absorption and the mental consciousness goes off to the five qualities of sense desire. Internal distraction is dullness, excitement and savoring while in meditative equipoise, because these three faults do away with meditative absorption. The distraction of signs is so called because one has no intention of seeking liberation but seeks profit and fame. One exerts oneself merely out of a wish for the sign of being spoken of by others as "a venerable meditator"; for this, although it is exertion in virtue, bears no fruit of real benefit. The distraction of dysfunction is attention accompanied by grasping at "I", grasping at "mine" and grasping at the thought "I am." Although one may wish from the heart for the qualities of meditative absorption, under the sway of the pride that thinks "I am a meditator" one praises oneself and belittles others. In fact, both the latent tendency of pride, which has not been abandoned and has grown, and the manifest pride that has arisen from it are called dysfunction, because through them virtue becomes stained. The distraction of attention is this: those of the Great Vehicle lineage may become weary of the suffering of saṃsāra and unable to bear it; if they then turn their attention to the Śrāvaka Vehicle, they fall away from the Great Vehicle. That is why it is called the scattering of the mind.

"Non-vigilance is wisdom associated with the afflictions; by it one engages in the conduct of body, speech and mind without vigilance" means this: although there is wisdom, it is mixed with afflictions such as attachment and ignorance, and so it is called associated with the afflictions. It is so called because through it one goes about coming and going, speaking, thinking and the like carelessly, without hitting the mark.

"Four others are also variable" refers to regret and sleep, and to coarse examination and fine investigation. These are not fixed to one side, as the virtuous and the unvirtuous are; they occur within all, the virtuous, the unvirtuous and the indeterminate. When they occur within the virtuous, they are other than unvirtuous; when they occur within the unvirtuous, they become other than virtuous. Therefore they are called variable.

"Regret is remorse of mind" means this: regret is of many kinds. With respect to the virtuous, the unvirtuous and the indeterminate, there is regret that thinks, "It was wrong to have done it," and regret that thinks, "It was wrong not to have done it." Thus, remorse at the thought "It was wrong to have done virtue" is unvirtuous regret. Remorse at the thought "I did not do virtue" is called virtuous regret. Apply this in the same way, correspondingly, to the unvirtuous and the indeterminate.

"Sleep is the contraction of the mind without control over its engagement" means this: the consciousnesses of the six doors focusing on outer objects is called engagement. The causes of sleep are weakness and fatigue, heaviness of body, and attending to darkness. Through being drowsy, consciousness is unable to engage its objects, and one is unable even to hold the body upright; it collapses inward and every activity slips away. That is its characteristic. Further, if one lies down to sleep while doing virtue and it is interrupted, that is unvirtuous sleep. If one lies down to sleep while doing evil and its continuity is cut, that is virtuous sleep. If one is in the midst of painting or sewing and it is interrupted and one stops, that is indeterminate. Alternatively, the three kinds may also be applied to whatever virtue or evil one does in dreams.

What is investigation? On the passage "Investigation is a mental discourse that searches; it is a particular kind of volition and of wisdom, namely the coarseness of mind": the mindfulness, in the mind of mental consciousness, of an object that is being sought is called investigation and mental discourse. That investigation is moreover of two kinds, worldly and supramundane. Worldly investigation is that mind accompanied by volition. Supramundane investigation arises from being accompanied by wisdom. Having searched, "What is this?", to know roughly, merely in general and approximately, "This is a pot," or "This is a cloth," is called the coarseness of mind.

What is analysis? On the passage "It is a mental discourse that examines in detail; likewise, it is the subtlety of mind": the rest is as above. "Subtlety of mind" means that one analyzes the very thing that was earlier examined coarsely, merely as "a pot." It is like determining in detail whether the pot is good or bad, fired or unfired, and its color and shape.

On the passage "What are the fourteen formations not associated with mind?": the word "not associated" refers to the mind not moving at such times as non-perception, cessation, fainting, and deep sleep. Further, just as, at the time of doing virtue, a nonvirtuous mind does not arise, and at the time of doing something indeterminate such as painting or sewing, a mind exclusively of doing virtue or of doing evil does not arise, this too is called "not associated." Apply the same to the others as well.

What are they? They are acquisition, the absorption of non-perception, the absorption of cessation, non-perception, the life faculty, group homogeneity, birth, aging, duration, impermanence, the collection of names, the collection of phrases, the collection of letters, the state of an ordinary being, and others of a like kind.

"Acquisition is obtaining and possession" means this: if one performs any action at all, whether a virtuous, a nonvirtuous, or an indeterminate action, then the first bringing into existence, from its previous nonexistence, of the imprint of whatever action was done is called obtaining. From then on, its remaining together with that, inseparable from it, is called possession.

On the passage "The absorption of non-perception is that cessation of unstable minds and mental factors which comes about, in one who is free from attachment to Śubhakṛtsna but not to what lies above, through attention preceded by the perception of release": in order to attain Great Fruit, the abode at the summit of the three abodes of the fourth meditative concentration, one blocks the consciousnesses of the six doors and the perceptions accompanying them, and that abiding is called the absorption of non-perception. Having abandoned without remainder the faults of the third meditative concentration is called being free from attachment to Śubhakṛtsna. Because the abandonment of the faults of the fourth meditative concentration has not been carried through to the end, it is called "not to what lies above." To think that that abode is liberation, final nirvāṇa, is the perception of release. To think, "I shall attain it," is the attention preceded by that. Mind is the six consciousnesses. Mental factors are perception and the rest; because these sometimes arise and sometimes do not arise, they are called unstable, and the term refers to their cessation.

The passage "The absorption of cessation is that cessation of unstable minds and mental factors, and also of some of the stable ones, which comes about, in one who is free from attachment to the sphere of nothingness and has moved upward from the peak of existence, through attention preceded by the perception of peaceful abiding" distinguishes it from the preceding one in four ways, namely by nature, by person, by ground, and by attention. Here, non-perception stops six consciousnesses, while absorption in cessation stops seven consciousnesses, so they differ by nature. As to person, they are distinguished by the one being entered by noble ones from the non-returner upward and the other by ordinary beings. As to ground, they are distinguished by the sphere of nothingness and Śubhakṛtsna. As to attention, they differ in that one thinks of final release and the other thinks, "For a while I shall abide in peace." Here, the sphere of nothingness is the third abode of the formless absorptions, and being free from attachment to it is being free from its afflictions. Having set about abandoning the afflictions of neither perception nor non-perception is called having moved upward from the peak of existence. To think, "This abiding, in which that has not been carried through to the end, is not final nirvāṇa, yet since it is blissful like it, I shall abide in it for a while," is called being preceded by the perception of peaceful abiding and by attention. Mind and mental factors are as above. The stable ones refers to the afflicted mind and its retinue, and the term refers to the stopping of seven consciousnesses.

On the passage "Non-perception is the result of the absorption of non-perception; it is that cessation of unstable minds and mental factors in those born among the gods who are non-perceiving beings": this is the result of the absorption in non-perception described above. Having been born among the non-perceiving gods, in one portion of the Great Fruit gods, at first there is perception. Then, through the momentum of what went before, perception grows subtler and subtler until it ceases, and one abides as if in a special meditative concentration. The six consciousnesses have ceased.

On the passage "The life faculty is the fixed duration of the abiding of formations, projected by previous karma into the group homogeneities": not dying for as long as one's lifespan, whether long or short, is called the life faculty. Gods being alike as gods, down to ants, beetles, and the like being alike within their different kinds, is group homogeneity. Having been born through the power of previous karma, virtuous and evil, with particular lifespans ranging from a hundred thousand years at most to mere instants at least, whatever years, months, and number of days each can last according to its own lot is called the fixed duration of the abiding of formations.

"Group homogeneity is the similarity of the bodies of sentient beings" refers to those born in the five paths of existence, humans, gods, animals, and the rest, each being alike within its own kind. "Birth is the arising of formations in a group homogeneity from their not having arisen" means that coming about anew, from previous nonexistence, within any kind of sentient being at all, such as a god or a human, is birth.

"Aging is the continuum of those formations becoming otherwise in this way" refers to those five aggregates changing, from the first, the embryonic stage of jelly and so on, into one continuum after another, until in the end they have become white haired, wrinkled, and so forth. "Duration is the continuity of the continuum of those formations in this way" means that once those aggregates of formations have arisen, the remaining of their momentary continuum in succession for as long as one has not died is called duration. "Impermanence is the cutting off of the continuity of those formations" means that the momentary continuum of the aggregates is cut off, one's life passes on, and the aggregates are destroyed.

"The collection of names is the designations for the essential nature of phenomena" means that a single phenomenon is labeled with a name by means of different linguistic conventions, and by that the meaning is understood. It is as when one says "fire" and "water," or in the language of India "agni" and "pāni," and one thereby understands that fire is what burns and water is what moistens. Alternatively, when a single name that has been assigned is written out in letters, the gathering together of many letters is called the collection of names. On "designations for the essential nature": the capacity to perform each its own function, such as heat and moisture, is called the essential nature. When names such as "fire" and "water" are assigned as designations, these are called designations. It is not otherwise, for if the name itself were the object, then saying "fire" would burn one's mouth.

On "The collection of phrases is the designations for the particular features of the essential nature of phenomena": labeling with names such as "fire" and "water" is called the collection of names. Saying, "That water is moreover impermanent, suffering, empty, and selfless," is called the collection of phrases, and the designation for the particular features of phenomena is just that. One should compare and apply this in the same way to all phenomena.

On "The collection of letters is the letters, because they make both of those clear": the gathering together of many letters, such as a and ka, the mothers of all letters, is the collection of letters. That letters label the essential nature of phenomena and their particular features as names and phrases and thereby make their meanings understood one by one is what is called making clear.

"An ordinary being is one who has not attained the qualities of a noble one" means that one who has not abandoned the afflictions such as attachment and who has been born in various different abodes through the power of their own karma is called an ordinary being. From the stream enterer and the attainment of the first ground upward one is called a noble one. The qualities of that noble one are the abandonment of the obscurations to be abandoned by seeing and those to be abandoned by meditation; because such a one has not attained these, that one is called an ordinary being.

"Those of a like kind are engagement and the rest" refers to what is of a like kind with the formations not associated with mind. The word "rest" gathers in the nine, from engagement to assemblage.

Among these, engagement refers to the engaging, once the aggregates of any sentient being at all, such as a human, have been taken up, in the uninterrupted manner of a string of instants and of results; it does not refer to a single instant or to the cutting off of the continuum of the aggregates.

Distinct determination is the distinct determination of cause and result in outer and inner phenomena. As for outer phenomena, if a barley seed is sown as the cause, the result too will definitely come forth as barley itself and will not become buckwheat; if a buckwheat seed is sown as the cause, the result too will be the same. As for inner phenomena too, from virtuous karma the result is definitely happiness, and from nonvirtuous karma the result is definitely suffering; it does not turn out otherwise.

Correspondence is the matching of the causes and results of good qualities. Through generosity wealth comes; through moral discipline the higher realms; through patience a fine appearance; through diligence good qualities increase and are not interrupted; through meditative concentration wisdom; and through wisdom expertise in the five fields of knowledge and so on. Thus every excellence comes about.

Speed is the swift arising of cause and result, because the causes and results of generosity and the rest just mentioned, and also the causes and results of earlier and later instants, arise swiftly.

Sequence is the cause arising first and the result arising afterward. In all phenomena, outer and inner, cause and result do not arise at the same time; they arise one after another in order.

Time is the time in which cause and result continually arise. When cause and result have arisen and ceased, this is designated past time. When cause and result have not yet arisen, this is designated future time. When cause and result have arisen and have not ceased, this is designated present time.

Place: every one of the ten directions, south, north, east, west, and so on, all around, wherever there is arising of outer and inner phenomena as things that are causes and results, is called place.

Number: a single thing cannot be counted as a number, but since many different things are counted as two, three, and so forth, the meaning is that formations are not single but of many kinds.

Assemblage is the causes and conditions for the arising of outer and inner phenomena. The seed, and earth, water, fire, and wind, are the outer ones. Ignorance and formations, these two, and the six elements are the inner causes and conditions. Because their coming together produces outer and inner phenomena, it is assemblage.

"What is the aggregate of consciousness? It is the cognizing of an object" means the eight consciousnesses knowing their respective objects. On "It is also mind and mentation, because it is variegated and because it serves as the support of mentation": mind means "variegated," because the six, from the eye and the ear and so on up to mental consciousness, engage the various phenomena that are their own distinct objects. The previous consciousness that has ceased is called mentation; because it acts as the cause producing the subsequent consciousness, it is said to serve as the support of mentation.

"In the true sense, mind is called the all-base consciousness, for it is that which accumulates the seeds of all formations" means that although the six consciousnesses are also called mind, when the matter is settled, mind refers to the all-base. Why is this? The word "mind" carries the sense of accumulating, and the all-base, in each and every instant, accumulates the habitual tendencies of all formations, of virtuous, nonvirtuous, and indeterminate karma.

"Moreover, its object and its aspect are not delimited" means that the all-base consciousness does not observe some of its objects and fail to observe others; rather, its observation of the entire world, the container and its contents, without exception, is not delimited. Because it does not clearly distinguish particulars such as the colors blue and yellow, its aspect is not delimited. "It is also the appropriating consciousness, because it appropriates the body" means that what takes up a body in the next life is not a permanent self; it is the mind that takes up the body. And since, from the time it grasps the blood and the semen in the womb until death, it holds the body, it is stated that the appropriating consciousness is also this very consciousness. The meaning is that the six consciousnesses, such as the eye consciousness, and a permanent self do not take up the body.

"In the true sense, mentation observes the all-base consciousness and is always accompanied by four associates." Just as above, although the consciousnesses of the six doors were designated mind, it was said that in the true sense mind is the all-base, so here too, although above the six consciousnesses that have just ceased were designated mentation, in the true sense that term does not apply to them. Here it is the afflicted mentation that is called mentation. If it is a consciousness, it cannot be without an object, so the object it observes is the all-base. If it is mind, it cannot be without mental factors, so its mental factors are the four, namely delusion about self, the view of self, pride in self, and attachment to self, together with the five omnipresent factors. Thus it is said to be accompanied by an entourage of nine mental factors.

"The six engaging consciousnesses too should be understood as appropriate, as they are taught in other sūtras" refers to the six from eye consciousness to mental consciousness. Thus eye consciousness relies on the eye, observes form, and thoroughly cognizes form; and the same should be applied to all of them, ear consciousness and the rest, until finally mental consciousness relies on the mental faculty, observes phenomena, and thoroughly cognizes phenomena. It should be understood in that way.

On "What are the twelve sense sources?": they are called sense sources because they are like the cause or the door through which the six consciousnesses, eye consciousness and the rest, come about and arise. In actual fact, the text states: the eye sense source and the form sense source, the ear sense source and the sound sense source, the nose sense source and the smell sense source, the tongue sense source and the taste sense source, the body sense source and the tangible sense source, and the mental sense source and the phenomena sense source. Since the six consciousnesses arise by relying on the faculties, the eye and the rest, and observing their objects, the six faculties and the six objects are called the twelve sense sources. Among them, the eight consciousnesses belonging to the aggregate of consciousness are included in the mental sense source. Cessation through analysis, cessation not through analysis, space, suchness, feeling, perception, formations, and imperceptible form: these eight phenomena are included in the phenomena sense source. The rest is easy to understand.

On "What are the eighteen elements?": they are the six supporting elements, the six object elements, and the six supported elements, making eighteen. Here the six faculties are called the supports, the six objects are called the objects, and the six consciousnesses are called the supported. In actual fact, the text states: the eye element, the form element, and the eye consciousness element; the ear element, the sound element, and the ear consciousness element; the nose element, the smell element, and the nose consciousness element; the tongue element, the taste element, and the tongue consciousness element; the body element, the tangible element, and the body consciousness element; and the mental element, the phenomena element, and the mental consciousness element. These are to be applied in order as explained above.

"What are the six elements?" Here other enumerations that are likewise named the six elements are taught. The earth element performs the function of supporting the seed. The water element performs the function of moistening the seed. The fire element performs the function of warming the seed. The wind element performs the function of making the seed sprout. The space element performs the function of not obstructing the seed. Time performs the function of transforming the seed. There is also what is designated the consciousness element: the all-base consciousness performs the function of giving rise to name and form. It is the seed from which inner phenomena arise.

"What are the twelve links of dependent origination?" This shows the causes and results by which inner phenomena arise. Here, "Ignorance, because it is great darkness" means this: just as, when things are obscured by darkness, even coarse material objects are not seen, so too ignorance obscures what is to be seen, namely the existence of karma and its results, the truths, the Jewels with their qualities, and the characteristic of phenomena, emptiness, so that they are not seen. Therefore it is called darkness. "Formations, because they form" means that because one performs various virtuous, nonvirtuous, and indeterminate actions in the three realms, they are formations. "Consciousness, because it cognizes" means that because the consciousnesses, the eye consciousness and the rest, individually cognize and know the distinct objects among outer and inner phenomena, such as forms and sounds, it is called consciousness. "Name and form, because they support one another" means this: just as three sticks whose tips are joined together can stand as a tripod, so too the aggregates of name and the aggregate of form support one another, and therefore they are called name and form. "The six sense sources, because they are the doors of arising" means that because they act as the cause for the arising and coming forth of mind and mental factors, they are called doors; in actual fact, the six faculties, the eye and the rest, are called the sense sources. "Contact, because it contacts" means that the coming together of the three, object, faculty, and consciousness, is called contact. "Feeling, because it experiences" means that when the three, object, faculty, and consciousness, come together, then, caused by contact with whatever object it may be, pleasing, displeasing, or neither, the experiencing of feeling in its three kinds, pleasant, painful, and neutral, is called feeling.

"Craving, because it thirsts" means that, like one tormented by thirst, one wishes to be parted from painful feeling and wishes to meet and remain together with pleasant feeling; therefore it is called craving. "Grasping, because it grasps" refers to the four kinds of grasping; it is called grasping because of grasping at sense pleasures, grasping at views, grasping at moral discipline and ascetic practices as supreme, and grasping at the doctrine of a self. "Becoming, because it gives rise to rebirth" means because it gives rise to birth in the three realms; that is, birth in the next life, once the habitual tendencies of karma and afflictions, the causes of birth, have grown strong, is called becoming. "Birth, because the aggregates come forth" means that the first coming forth, from previous nonexistence, of the bodily aggregates of gods, humans, and the rest in the five paths of existence is called birth. "Aging, because the aggregates ripen" means that, like a fruit that ripens and then grows old, becoming white haired and wrinkled is called aging. "Death, because they disintegrate" means that it is death because the time for the bodily aggregates to remain is exhausted, and, abandoned by warmth and consciousness, they disintegrate.

"Because it is pertinent" means that these sufferings, too, belong to and are connected with the links of birth and of aging and death. Sorrow is the anguish of mind that fears being parted from relatives and friends and from one's possessions and necessities. Lamentation is speaking out in words and wailing over the story of whatever caused the sorrow. Suffering is the body being impaired and ill at ease. Unhappiness is the mind being impaired and ill at ease. Agitation is the mind being terrified on recalling all the various wrongs one has done in the past. "Are spoken of" means that these are appended to the end of dependent origination and explained.

"What are the ten perfections?" They are called the ten perfections because, having crossed beyond the suffering of the three realms, which is like an ocean, they bring about the gradual attainment of the ten grounds. The perfection of generosity is the giving of three things: Dharma, material things, and fearlessness. Among these, giving away and benefiting others by means of the sublime Dharma, worldly poetry, crafts, and skills is the giving of Dharma. Giving away and benefiting others by means of three kinds of things, outer and inner, namely food, drink, wealth, goods, and so forth, including citadels, lands, and kingdoms; servants, fields, livestock, riches, children, and wives; and one's own flesh and blood, head, body, and the like, is the giving of material things. Protecting and benefiting others from punishment by kings, ministers, and the like, and from the dangers of enemies, wild beasts, and the like, is the giving of fearlessness. Practicing these with the three spheres completely pure is called a perfection.

The perfection of moral discipline is the threefold discipline of gathering virtue, restraining nonvirtue, and working for the benefit of sentient beings. Among these, gathering virtue is accomplishing the qualities of awakening, from listening to and reflecting on the sublime Dharma up to the grounds, the perfections, and so on. Restraining nonvirtue is the seven classes of the śrāvaka discipline, kept by monks and nuns, male and female novices, laymen and laywomen, and probationary nuns. Working for the benefit of sentient beings is working for the benefit of all sentient beings without partiality, as one cherishes an only child. Practicing with the three spheres pure is to be applied to all those that follow as well.

The perfection of patience is threefold: not minding suffering, not retaliating against harm, and the patience of realization regarding the Dharma. Among these, not minding suffering means that when doing virtue, whatever harmful circumstances arise, such as hunger and thirst, cold and heat, one does not give up and does not break the continuity of virtue. Not retaliating against harm means that even when others, such as sentient beings one has helped, do one various harms such as beating and binding, one does not become angry at them and no malice arises. The patience of realization regarding the Dharma: when the profound meanings of emptiness and selflessness, or of no arising and no ceasing, are taught, the mind is not terrified, and faith in them and joy arise; therefore it is called the perfection of patience.

The perfection of diligence is threefold: armor, gathering virtue, and working for the benefit of sentient beings. Among these, first vowing, "I shall accomplish the benefit of all sentient beings and unsurpassed awakening until the end of the eon," is armor-like diligence. Not breaking the continuity of listening to the Dharma, reflecting, and meditating is the diligence of gathering virtue. Skillfully gathering sentient beings by means of the four kinds of attraction and establishing them in virtue is called the diligence of working for the benefit of sentient beings.

The perfection of meditative concentration is threefold: abiding in bliss in this very life, bringing forth good qualities, and working for the benefit of sentient beings. Among these, the nonconceptual meditative absorption and the absorption of cessation are the meditative concentration of abiding in bliss in this very life. Attaining all the great qualities of the Buddha by means of meditative absorptions such as the Śūraṃgama is the meditative concentration of bringing forth good qualities. Having attained the six superknowledges through accomplishing meditative concentration, making minds joyful by means of bodily miracles, and, through knowing the minds of others, teaching the Dharma in accordance with their minds, turning them back from nonvirtue and establishing them in virtue, is called the meditative concentration of working for the benefit of sentient beings.

The perfection of wisdom is threefold: the wisdom that realizes one's own aim, the wisdom that realizes the aim of others, and the wisdom that defeats opponents. This, moreover, is expertise in the five fields of knowledge. Among these, the inner science is the wisdom that realizes one's own aim through expertise in the meaning of the twelve branches of the scriptures. As for the science of healing, expertise in the treatment and medicines for the four hundred and four outer diseases, and teaching loving-kindness, compassion, and the like as the remedies for the inner diseases such as attachment and aversion, is the science of healing.

Expertise in every kind of work to be done is called the science of crafts. By means of it one develops and strengthens the capabilities of foolish beings and works for the benefit of sentient beings. Thus the knowledge of the two, healing and crafts, is the wisdom that realizes the aim of others. The two sciences of reasoning and of language are expertise in tarka and in vyākaraṇa, that is, in logic and grammar; because by these two one refutes the non-Buddhists (tīrthikas) and others, it is called the wisdom that defeats opponents.

The perfection of skillful means is so called because whatever roots of virtue one has accumulated, many or few, down to the very least, are shared in common with all sentient beings and dedicated to great unsurpassed awakening, so that their result becomes immeasurably great and inexhaustible.

The perfection of aspiration is making the aspiration, "Wherever I am born, in all my lives, may I never be parted from the awakening mind, and may I never break the continuity of the conduct of the perfections for the sake of all sentient beings."

The perfection of power is of two kinds: the power of reflection and the power of cultivation. Among these, when, through wisdom accompanied by meditative absorption, one sees outer and inner phenomena as empty and selfless, so that attachment to them does not arise, this is called the power of reflection. When, through the predominance of diligence, even though wisdom is slight, one accomplishes the side of virtue without needing to strive, by the force of familiarity, this is called the power of cultivation. They are powers because they cannot be overcome by their opposing factors, such as stinginess.

The perfection of wisdom is so called because, through fully comprehending that the characteristic of phenomena is emptiness, one enjoys the great enjoyment of the Dharma among the great bodhisattvas.

"Being the occasion of cause and result: what are the ten grounds of a bodhisattva?" These are words meaning that the perfections are the cause and the ten grounds are the result, so it is fitting to explain them in succession, and they are connected. "What are the ten grounds?" is the heading, to be connected with what actually follows below.

Here, "The first ground is the Very Joyful" means this: just as when a poor person finds a precious jewel they are overjoyed, thinking, "I have found such a supremely precious jewel as I have never seen before," so too one directly sees the expanse of phenomena, never seen before at any time, and is able to bring about excellence in one's own aim and that of others. Because, with generosity perfected, immeasurable special joy arises, the first ground is called the Very Joyful.

The second ground is the Stainless. The stains are delight in the phenomena of saṃsāra and delight in the lesser vehicle, that is, doing what is nonvirtuous and lacking compassion. Because these two stains are absent at the time of the second ground, it is the Stainless. Alternatively, it is called the Stainless because, with moral discipline perfected, one does not commit acts of corrupt discipline even in dreams.

The third ground, the Luminous, is so called because, with the perfection of patience perfected, one seeks learning without regard even for body and life, dispels the ignorance of oneself and others, and wisdom arises.

The fourth ground is the Radiant. It is called the Radiant because, with the perfection of diligence perfected, a wisdom like fire arises from cultivating the factors of awakening and burns up the afflictions as if they were plants and trees.

The fifth ground is the Very Difficult to Master. It is called Difficult to Master because, with the perfection of meditative concentration perfected, one does not decline from the meditative absorption of nonconceptual wisdom, nor does one break the continuity of working for the benefit of sentient beings, and to practice both at once is extremely difficult.

The sixth ground is the Manifest. It is called the Manifest because, with the perfection of wisdom perfected, one directly realizes the meaning of dependent origination as without arising and without ceasing.

The seventh ground is the Far Gone. It is called the Far Gone because, with the perfection of skillful means perfected, one reaches the end of the signs that are engaged with by effort and exertion, and is free of signs.

The eighth ground is the Immovable. It is called the Immovable because, with the perfection of power perfected, signlessness is spontaneously present, and by the power of wisdom signs are overpowered, so that not even the slightest one arises.

The ninth ground is Good Intelligence. It is called Good Intelligence because, with the perfection of aspiration perfected, one possesses the four discriminating knowledges, namely discriminating knowledge of phenomena, of meanings, of etymologies, and of eloquence, and so becomes a great master of the Dharma.

The tenth ground is called the Cloud of Dharma. With the perfection of wisdom perfected, just as when rain falls from a great cloud crops grow even in a wilderness, so too wisdom and compassion, like a cloud, send down the rain of the Dharma upon the minds of sentient beings, which are like a desolate wilderness, and the crops of virtue grow and increase. Therefore it is called the Cloud of Dharma.

"What are the eighteen emptinesses?" Emptiness itself cannot be divided into distinct kinds and cannot be counted as many. Yet, by way of teaching that the distinct objects that sentient beings imagine to be real things are not real things, the enumeration of emptinesses is divided into eighteen.

Here, the emptiness of the inner refers to everything from what is called the eye up to what is called the mind. The six faculties, the eye and the rest, and the five aggregates of the body that are their support are called the inner. It is called the emptiness of the inner because in them there is not even an atom of any real thing called a phenomenon or a person that abides permanently and eternally. In this way, that the absence of any real thing that is a phenomenon or a person, called emptiness, is the ultimate, should be compared and understood in all the other cases as well.

The emptiness of the outer refers to the six objects, from what is called form up to what is called phenomena. The four great elements, such as plants, trees, and forests, which are not held by consciousness and are of a dull, inanimate nature, and the objects of the faculties and consciousnesses, are called the outer. To these too one applies the statement that they are not real things.

The emptiness of the outer and inner refers to the twelve sense sources. Some may think, "Even if it is taught that there is no real thing in either the inner or the outer, still there is a real thing in the general whole in which outer and inner are combined." Yet there are only the six consciousnesses, which take the six faculties and the six objects as their focus and depend on them, and in these too there is no real thing: that is the meaning.

The emptiness of emptiness is the emptiness of all phenomena. Some may think, "Even if all phenomena included in the three, the outer, the inner, and the outer and inner, are taught to be without any real thing and empty, still, because the name has been applied in words, 'All those phenomena are empty,' that very thing called 'empty' exists as some real thing." But how could there be a real thing that is emptiness? There is none. Therefore it is called the emptiness of emptiness.

The great emptiness refers to the ten directions. Even if one thinks that there are real things in the three container worlds of all the directions, as many as there are, south, north, east and west, and in the inhabitant worlds born into the five classes of beings, which are many and vast, in them too there is no real thing: so it is said.

The emptiness of the ultimate has the characteristic of being neither permanent nor perishing. The dharmadhātu, which is nirvāṇa, is the ultimate. Since it has no permanent real thing, it is not permanent; since it is not utterly nothing whatsoever, it is called not perishing. The meaning is that even if one conceives that there is a real thing in it, there is not.

The emptiness of the conditioned refers to the three realms: the three, the desire realm, the form realm and the formless realm, are conditioned. Or else it refers to the four characteristics of the conditioned, namely arising, abiding, impermanence and destruction. Even if one conceives that there are real things in these, they do not exist; there are none: so it is said.

The emptiness of the unconditioned is the absence of arising, the absence of cessation, and the absence of changing into something else while abiding. These are cessation through analysis, cessation not through analysis, suchness and space. These four phenomena are called unconditioned because they are not produced by any cause or condition whatsoever. Unlike conditioned phenomena, they do not arise at the beginning, cease at the end, and in between, while abiding, change into good or bad, large or small. Even if one thinks that there is a real thing in them, there is no real thing; they are empty: so it is said.

The emptiness of what transcends extremes is freedom from all extremes. If one says that there is a real thing in what is called transcending the extremes of existence and nonexistence, or else if one conceives that there are real things in the extremes of the former, the present and the later, in them there is no real thing: so it is said.

The emptiness of what has neither beginning nor end refers to the three times. Even if one conceives that there is some real thing in what is called the beginning of saṃsāra and what is called its end, or else in the three times of past, future and present, they are empty, because there is no real thing.

The emptiness of what is not discarded means that among all phenomena there is nothing to be taken up and nothing to be abandoned. Take the example of a vase into which water was first poured: when that water is spilled out, the vase becomes empty. This emptiness is not to be understood in that way, as though in the ultimate too some substantial real thing existed before, and then, that substance being discarded and becoming nonexistent, it became empty. Since the ultimate has from the very beginning the characteristic of emptiness, in emptiness there is no discarding, no taking up and no abandoning. Therefore if some conceive that there is a real thing in it, there is no real thing: so it is said.

The emptiness of intrinsic nature refers to the conditioned and unconditioned phenomena, which were not made empty by the śrāvakas, the pratyekabuddhas, the bodhisattvas or the Tathāgatas. That is, all conditioned and unconditioned phenomena, beginning with form, are pervaded by emptiness from the very beginning. Their abiding as empty by intrinsic nature did not come about because noble ones such as the śrāvakas searched and deliberately made them empty; rather, since they abide as emptiness from the very beginning, it is called the emptiness of intrinsic nature.

The emptiness of all phenomena refers to the aggregates, the elements, the sense sources, the three realms, and the conditioned and the unconditioned. That is, of the two kinds of characteristic of outer and inner phenomena such as the aggregates, the specific and the general, here, as general characteristics, all phenomena are said to be impermanent, suffering, empty and selfless; and in that there is no real thing. Or else, when some conceive that there is a real thing in the ultimate absence of essential nature in all phenomena, in that there is no real thing: so it is said.

The emptiness of specific characteristics covers everything from "form has the characteristic of breaking down" up to "consciousness has the characteristic of cognizing." It also covers everything from "earth is hard, water is wet, fire is hot and wind is light" up to "omniscience has the characteristic of direct perception." Of the two kinds of characteristic, even if one conceives that there are real things in these specific characteristics, they have no real thing: so it is said.

The emptiness of the unobservable refers to the phenomena of the three times. That is, outer and inner phenomena have never once become possessed of a real thing at any time, whether past, present or future. Since they are never observed as real things, it is called the emptiness of the unobservable.

The emptiness of non-things, the emptiness of essential nature, and the emptiness of the essential nature of non-things are stated with the words "because all phenomena arise, abide and cease in dependent origination." The meaning of the first term is this: phenomena such as form and sound, just as they appear at present, have no real thing in truth; therefore it is called the emptiness of non-things. The second term refers to what is called the nature of reality of phenomena such as form and sound; since that nature of reality has no real thing, it is called the emptiness of essential nature. The third term is this: if some conceive that there is a real thing in the combination into one of both the emptiness of non-things and the emptiness of the absence of essential nature mentioned above, then, since there is no real thing in the combination of those two either, it is called the emptiness of the essential nature of non-things.

Why is this? All phenomena arise in mutual dependence upon many causes and conditions, each other and distinct, and they arise and cease in every single instant. Therefore there is no permanent real thing; yet neither are they utterly nothing whatsoever in every respect. For this reason it is stated, "because all phenomena arise, abide and cease in dependent origination."

On the words "The factors of awakening are set out in the manner of grounds and antidotes": awakening is of three kinds, the awakening of the śrāvakas, of the pratyekabuddhas, and unsurpassed awakening, and it refers to attaining the knowledge of exhaustion and the knowledge of non-arising. The factors on its side are what serve as aids, such as causes and means, for attaining it; they are the actual items that come below. The grounds are the ten grounds, the first ground and the rest, beginning from stream entry. The antidotes are what reverse and abandon miserliness and the other factors adverse to those grounds. The meaning is that these are set out in the manner of an enemy and its opponent.

The four close placements of mindfulness are the close placement of mindfulness on the body, the close placement of mindfulness on feeling, the close placement of mindfulness on mind, and the close placement of mindfulness on phenomena. Here, mindfulness is wisdom: because it knows the characteristics of phenomena unerringly, just as they are, it is linked with insight. Close placement is placing one-pointedly just what has been examined by wisdom, and so it is linked with calm abiding.

The body is of three kinds: inner, outer, and outer and inner. The inner body is one's own body. The outer is the container world, such as plants and trees, which are not held by consciousness. The outer and inner body is the body of other sentient beings: it is outer with respect to oneself, and inner with respect to plants and trees, which have no mind. Of these, the inner body is examined as having the specific characteristic of being the elements and what derives from the elements, capable of being broken and destroyed, and the general characteristics of being impermanent and suffering, and so on, up to having the characteristic of various impurities, such as pus, lymph and phlegm, from the crown of the head downward and from the feet upward. The outer and inner body is likewise in accord with that. The specific characteristic of the outer body is that it is an aggregation of many minute particles, and its general characteristic is like those above. When this has been settled by wisdom, placing the mind one-pointedly is called the close placement of mindfulness on the body.

As for feeling, the three kinds of feeling that arise focused on the inner body are called inner feeling. Likewise, what arises focused on the outer body is outer feeling, and what arises focused on the outer and inner body is outer and inner feeling; thus it is applied in three ways. Further, what refreshes body and mind is pleasant feeling; what torments body and mind is painful feeling; and what does neither, neither tormenting nor refreshing, is neutral feeling. Their general characteristics too are like those above. As for their specific characteristic, all three feelings have the characteristic of suffering: painful feeling is pervaded by the suffering of suffering, pleasant feeling is pervaded by the suffering of change, and neutral feeling is pervaded by the suffering of conditioning. Examining in this way and placing the mind one-pointedly is the close placement of mindfulness on feeling.

Mind is of twenty kinds, beginning with the mind with attachment: the mind free of attachment, the mind with aversion, the mind free of aversion, the mind with delusion, the mind free of delusion, the collected mind, the distracted mind, the slack mind, the elated mind, the excited mind, the unexcited mind, the calm mind, the uncalm mind, the mind well settled in equipoise, the mind not well settled in equipoise, the mind that has well cultivated the path, the mind that has not well cultivated the path, the mind freed through having well cultivated the path, and the mind not freed through having well cultivated the path: these are the twenty. Condensed, these are of two kinds: mind at the time of investigating and mind at the time of abiding. Of these, the six minds from the mind with attachment up to the mind free of delusion are called investigating mind; from the collected mind onward they are called abiding mind. Investigating is setting the mind to work; abiding is resting it in meditative absorption. These minds too are applied in three ways, as outer and inner: the twenty minds that arise by way of focusing on the inner body are called inner mind, and likewise there are the minds focused on the outer body and on the outer and inner body. Placing the mind one-pointedly on the thought that all three kinds are impermanent, suffering, empty and selfless is called the close placement of mindfulness on mind.

Phenomena are the phenomena of thorough affliction and of purification. That is, when the twenty minds above are associated with the afflictions and the secondary afflictions, they are called thoroughly afflicted. When they are associated with the antidotes that abandon those afflictions, such as meditation on the unattractive, loving-kindness and dependent origination, they are called purified. Realizing those phenomena as empty and selfless and meditating on them is called the close placement of mindfulness on phenomena.

By meditating on these, the four distortions are abandoned. That is, by meditating on the close placement of mindfulness on the body as impure, the view of it as pure is abandoned. By meditating on the close placement of mindfulness on feeling as suffering, the view of it as pleasure is abandoned. By meditating on the close placement of mindfulness on mind as impermanent, the view of it as permanent is abandoned. By meditating on the close placement of mindfulness on phenomena as selfless, the view that a self exists is abandoned. Meditating on and realizing the four truths is also just these: the truth of suffering, the truth of the origin, the truth of cessation and the truth of the path are applied one by one to these four in their order.

The four correct abandonments are these. One generates aspiration so as not to give rise to evil, unwholesome phenomena that have not yet arisen. One generates aspiration to abandon evil, unwholesome phenomena that have arisen. One generates aspiration to give rise to wholesome phenomena that have not yet arisen. One generates aspiration so that wholesome phenomena that have arisen may abide, increase further, not decline, and be brought to completion. Above, in the section on the close placements of mindfulness, when one meditates on the close placement of mindfulness on phenomena, one realizes that the phenomena of thorough affliction are the adverse side and the phenomena of purification are the antidote. The fourfold diligence that then arises, abandoning what is adverse and giving rise to the antidote, is called the correct abandonments.

The three actions of body, the four of speech and the three of mind in the desire realm, which are the ten nonvirtues, together with attachment, aversion and the rest, the causes that produce them, are called evil, because they harm oneself and others and their maturation too produces great suffering. They are called unwholesome because they are contrary to virtue and are censured by the noble ones. When, through focusing on and engaging with those objects, habitual tendencies have been accumulated in the mental continuum, they are called arisen; when habitual tendencies have not been accumulated, they are called unarisen. The thought, "I shall not give rise to these phenomena in the mind now, and I shall see that they do not occur later either," and the thought, "As for those of these phenomena that have previously arisen in the mind, I shall by every means cut off their continuity and abandon them so that they do not become manifest," are called aspiration.

Wholesome phenomena are the reverse of the above: the ten virtues, non-attachment, non-aversion and so on. If one asks why these are called abandonments, it is because these wholesome phenomena too abandon the phenomena adverse to them; therefore they are called correct abandonments. There is the thought, "These phenomena were not previously present in my mental continuum; I shall give rise to them and bring them about directly," and the thought, "What little was previously present in my mental continuum I shall make increase and expand." That they do not diminish from what was previously present is abiding. That, by bringing them about directly again and again, they increase in ever greater measure is increasing further. That one does not fall under the power of evil, the adverse side, is not declining. Swiftly bringing to its consummation this abandoning of the unwholesome and giving rise to the wholesome is bringing to completion. In this way, the preparatory thought, "For their sake I shall do this and this," is aspiration; it is the characteristic of generating diligence.

The four bases of miraculous power are the base of miraculous power endowed with the samādhi of aspiration and the formations of abandonment, the base of miraculous power endowed with the samādhi of mind and the formations of abandonment, the base of miraculous power endowed with the samādhi of diligence and the formations of abandonment, and the base of miraculous power endowed with the samādhi of investigation and the formations of abandonment. Here, miraculous power refers to the five superknowledges: miraculous power, the divine ear, knowledge of others' minds, recollection of former abodes, and knowledge of death and birth. The four bases refer to the causes by which these are attained, and they are linked with the four samādhis of the main list.

When, through particular delight and interest in placing the mind in meditative absorption, one constantly enters meditative absorption and thereby attains one-pointedness of mind, this is called the samādhi of interest. Its nature is longing for the fruits of meditative concentration, the higher realms, and for the qualities of liberation. To that samādhi there are five obstructions on the adverse side: laziness, forgetting the object, slackness and excitement, non-application, and application. Of these, laziness is not engaging in virtue; it is a fault because it obstructs at the time when one first applies oneself to hearing and reflecting. Forgetting the object means that, because one does not remember the instructions on meditative concentration, one has no grasp of what the object of meditation is; it is a fault because one is bewildered at the time of applying oneself to meditative concentration. Slackness and excitement mean being dull toward the object of meditation and distracted from it; it is a fault because, at the time of entering meditative concentration, the mind is unworkable. Non-application is a fault because, when slackness or excitement occurs, one does not abandon it and does not bring the mind back to calm abiding. Excessive application is a fault because, at the time when the mind abides in equipoise, it moves the mind from that and distracts it. These five are to be abandoned.

The formations that abandon them are eight: interest, diligence, faith, pliancy, mindfulness, vigilance, intention and equanimity. How do these abandon those faults? Four of the formations abandon laziness. How so? They abandon it by way of showing the basis, the support, the sign and the result of diligence. That is, since diligence is undertaken when there is interest, interest is the basis of diligence. Diligence itself is the support. The cause from which interest arises is faith: since interest comes about when there is faith, faith is the sign, and sign and cause have the same meaning. When diligence is undertaken, body and mind become workable; this is pliancy, and it is the result of diligence. When body and mind are thus workable and one has gained mastery over diligence, laziness has no foothold; therefore the first four formations are the antidote to laziness.

The antidotes to the remaining four faults are linked one by one, in order, with the remaining four formations. The antidote to forgetting the object is mindfulness. Since, when slackness or excitement occurs, it recognizes whichever has occurred, the antidote to slackness and excitement is vigilance. The antidote to non-application is intention, because when slackness or excitement occurs, intention brings the mind back to calm abiding. The antidote to excessive application is equanimity, because whenever the mind is being moved, one cultivates it as unmoving. Therefore these are called the formations of abandonment.

The samādhi of mind is so called because one attains one-pointedness of mind by the force of long cultivation, having in former lives cultivated meditative concentration again and again, or else having accumulated the habitual tendencies of cultivating meditative absorption again and again earlier in this life. The samādhi of diligence is so called because one attains one-pointedness of mind by striving and striving without break to abandon evil, unwholesome phenomena and to make the wholesome phenomena that are their antidote arise and increase. The samādhi of investigation refers to attaining one-pointedness of mind by investigating, with the wisdom that comes from hearing the sublime Dharma from a spiritual friend and reflecting on it, analyzing and settling the characteristics of phenomena as impermanent, empty and so on, free of doubt. The words "the base of miraculous power endowed with the formations of abandonment" are to be applied to each of the following in the same way as they were applied to the samādhi of interest, and so to all of them.

The five faculties are the faculty of faith, the faculty of diligence, the faculty of mindfulness, the faculty of meditative absorption and the faculty of wisdom. Why are they called faculties? Because they exercise control in producing the roots of virtue that accord with liberation.

Alternatively, conviction, clarity and aspiration are faith. When it is present, one thinks, "I shall emerge from saṃsāra," and so one undertakes diligence. Diligence exercises control in accomplishing the three trainings: the faculty of mindfulness exercises control over the training in higher moral discipline, the faculty of meditative absorption exercises control over the training in higher mind, and the faculty of wisdom exercises control over the training in higher wisdom. Or else, because by the power of each preceding one they exercise control over the arising of each following one, they are called faculties.

Among these, conviction in karma and its results, in the truths and in the Three Jewels, together with clarity of mind toward them, is the faculty of faith. Not interrupting the continuity of virtue, such as listening, reflecting and meditating, is the faculty of diligence. Not forgetting, and keeping clear, the meaning of what one has heard, reflected upon and meditated on is the faculty of mindfulness. The mind's not being distracted toward or scattered among objects such as forms and sounds is the faculty of meditative absorption. Clearly settling, without error, the specific and general characteristics of phenomena is the faculty of wisdom. Although they have thus become controlling, they are still slightly mixed with the opposing factors, which now and then create interruptions.

At the time of the powers, the opposing factors, namely lack of faith, laziness, forgetfulness, distraction and lack of vigilance, no longer appear to stir in the least; therefore they are called powers. In actual terms these are the power of faith, the power of diligence, the power of mindfulness, the power of meditative absorption and the power of wisdom. Their nature is the same as that of the five faculties; the difference between them is drawn according to whether they are mixed or unmixed with the opposing factors.

The four aids to penetration are also designated on these very same factors: the attainment of illumination, the increase of illumination, partial entry into suchness, and the immediately preceding. At the time when one knows the names designated for phenomena to be mere illusions, and knows the objects expressed by names to be mere illusions, there are the five faculties, and these are to be correlated with the two, the attainment of illumination and the increase of illumination. At the time when one knows the names designated as names to be empty, and knows the objects expressed by names to be empty, there are the five powers, and these are to be correlated with the two, partial entry into suchness and the immediately preceding. These are the characteristics of the faculties and powers that serve as the preparation for the path of seeing.

The seven limbs of awakening are mindfulness as a limb of perfect awakening, discrimination of phenomena as a limb of perfect awakening, diligence as a limb of perfect awakening, joy as a limb of perfect awakening, pliancy as a limb of perfect awakening, meditative absorption as a limb of perfect awakening, and equanimity as a limb of perfect awakening. Concerning them it is said:

The limb of nature, the limb of basis,
the third, the limb of definite emergence,
the fourth is the limb of benefit,
and threefold is the limb of non-affliction.

The word "awakening" means realizing that all phenomena are empty; it is nonconceptual wisdom. It is to be identified with discrimination of phenomena as a limb of awakening, which is both the principal factor and a limb. Because the other six act as its aids, they are called the seven limbs.

Perfect mindfulness is the limb that is the basis of the wisdom that discriminates phenomena: when there is mindfulness, wisdom comes forth and arises from it, and so it is called the basis. Discrimination of phenomena is the limb of nature, since it realizes the characteristics, because it is the very nature of wisdom. Diligence is the limb of definite emergence, because it makes one emerge from the state of an ordinary being and pass beyond it. Joy is the limb of benefit, because the joy of seeing the first ground benefits and satisfies body and mind. The three, pliancy, meditative absorption and equanimity, are the limbs of non-affliction: pliancy is the ground of non-affliction, meditative absorption is the basis of non-affliction, and equanimity is the essential nature of non-affliction. Why is that? Because when body and mind are serviceable, attachment, distraction and the like do not arise; because by settling the mind one-pointedly one abandons the afflictions to be abandoned by seeing; and because, once the afflictions to be abandoned by seeing have been abandoned, from then on afflictions such as attachment do not arise, and one rests in equanimity in the very continuum of a purified mind. So the three are applied one by one, in order, to the ground, the basis and the essential nature of non-affliction. This summarizes the meaning of that verse.

The noble eightfold path consists of right view, right thought, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness and right meditative absorption. Here "noble" is said because the afflictions such as attachment that are to be abandoned by seeing and by meditation have been abandoned far away. Its path is like the cause or the door for attaining that noble state. In actual terms it is eight limbs that are particular kinds of wisdom. Concerning them it is said:

Delimiting, and making understood,
three kinds that make others trust,
and as remedies for the opposing factors:
these are the eight limbs of the path.

Right view is the pure mundane wisdom attained subsequent to the nonconceptual wisdom of the second ground and above. Because it takes as its object and determines, thinking, "This is how I saw the dharmadhātu with nonconceptual wisdom," it is the limb of delimiting. Right thought examines without error the general and specific characteristics of phenomena, and just as one has realized them, so one teaches and explains the Dharma to others with right speech; therefore thought and speech, these two, are the limbs that make others understand. Right speech, because it gives coherent answers without error to challenges and to questions raised by doubt, makes others trust, thinking, "This is completely pure wisdom." Right action, because one has abandoned nonvirtuous actions such as killing so as not to commit them even slightly, makes others trust, thinking, "This is completely pure moral discipline."

Right livelihood: having reduced one's requisites, one does not give rise to a longing to see many material goods and does not hoard them, but seeks only as much in the way of requisites as lets one's own body practice virtue. Therefore it makes others trust, thinking, "This person lives with moderation and has few requisites." Right speech, right action and right livelihood, these three, are the limbs that make others trust, without fault.

Right effort abandons the latent tendencies of the afflictions, such as attachment, that are to be abandoned by meditation. With right mindfulness, when the secondary afflictions of dullness and excitement arise, one counters them one-pointedly and uplifts the mind, and so abandons them. Right meditative absorption abandons the obscurations that prevent one from obtaining the six superknowledges, and so brings about the attainment of the superknowledges. Therefore right effort, mindfulness and meditative absorption, these three, are called the remedies for the opposing factors. They are to be correlated with the verse in order.

The Four Noble Truths are stated as follows: suffering is to be fully understood, the origin is to be abandoned, cessation is to be made manifest, and the path that leads to the cessation of suffering is to be cultivated. Here "noble" is as explained above. "Four truths" means that these four phenomena, suffering and the rest, are certain to be exactly so, without error.

As for suffering being something to be fully understood: the container world and the world of its inhabitants, which are the already accomplished results of former karma, cannot be abandoned, so one should know them to be impermanent, suffering, empty and without self. As for the origin being something to be abandoned: former karma and afflictions arise in the three realms and the five kinds of rebirth and produce that mass of suffering. Recognizing how they come about, as cause, as origin, as production and as condition, one knows that they are to be abandoned by the path. As for cessation being something to be made manifest: the abandonment of both suffering and the origin, nirvāṇa with a remainder of the aggregates and nirvāṇa without any remainder of the aggregates, is to be known as cessation, peace, excellence and definite emergence. As for the path that leads to the cessation of suffering being something to be cultivated: the path is the factors of awakening, or else the noble eightfold path. By these, suffering and its origin cease and are abandoned. It is to be known as path, as reasoning, as accomplishment and as definite emergence. In actual terms it is called the path because it seeks and finds the selflessness of persons and of phenomena, and because it takes one from the level of an ordinary being to the state of a noble one. Becoming familiar with what one has realized in it is called cultivation.

What are the four meditative concentrations? They are conception and analysis, joy, bliss and equanimity. Here the four meditative concentrations means that, in the four abodes of the form realm, the mind is not moved by the opposing factors but remains steady and in command of itself. Of these, conception and analysis is the first meditative concentration. One conceives and analyzes that indulging in the five sense pleasures of the lower level, the desire realm, is faulty, like a disease or a boil, while the first meditative concentration of form and the rest are peaceful and blissful. In this way one obtains the higher qualities, and it possesses the joy and bliss that come from the waning of the opposing factors. Joy is the second meditative concentration: through cultivating the remedy, it possesses joy and bliss born of a special meditative absorption. Bliss is the third meditative concentration: when there is joy, its counterpart, displeasure, also arises; so, thinking, "I shall abandon joy," one abandons joy, and it possesses an equanimity in which its counterpart, displeasure, has also been abandoned. Because bliss has not been abandoned in it, it is called "possessing bliss." Equanimity is the fourth meditative concentration: when there is the bliss of the lower level, its counterpart, suffering, also arises; so, thinking, "I shall abandon that bliss too," one abandons bliss, and it possesses an equanimity in which its counterpart, the suffering of breathing in and out, has also been abandoned. Its characteristic is that both bliss and suffering have been abandoned.

What are the four immeasurables? They are immeasurable loving-kindness, immeasurable compassion, immeasurable joy and immeasurable equanimity. These are the four immeasurables because they are not like the love of worldly people for a limited circle such as their children, grandchildren and relatives, nor like the love of the śrāvakas, who act for the benefit of only a few beings; rather, one arouses loving-kindness and compassion toward all sentient beings as toward an only child. Of these, loving-kindness is the thought, "I shall benefit all sentient beings by establishing them in happiness." Compassion is the thought, "I shall benefit all sentient beings by freeing them from afflictions and removing their suffering." Joy is the thought, "How excellent it is that benefit and happiness have been brought about for sentient beings, as above." Equanimity is to rest in equanimity toward those sentient beings who have already been brought to happiness and liberation, since there is no further need to liberate them.

What are the four formless absorptions? They are the sphere of infinite space, the sphere of infinite consciousness, the sphere of nothingness, and the sphere of neither existence nor nonexistence. The four formless absorptions means that, in the formless realm, the four name aggregates, namely the aggregates of feeling, perception, formations and consciousness, enter one-pointedly and in command of themselves into the four abodes.

Of these, the sphere of infinite space: to think, "When all phenomena are taken apart one by one, there is no entity; they amount to no more than space, and that space is infinite," is called the sphere of infinite space. The sphere of infinite consciousness: the meditative absorption that resolves, "The consciousness accompanying the perception that takes as its object the thought that all phenomena amount to no more than space and that this space is infinite, is itself infinite, just like space," is called the sphere of infinite consciousness. The sphere of nothingness: rising from the sphere of infinite consciousness, one examines what there is to take as an object beyond consciousness, and seeing not even the slightest phenomenon, whether with form or without form, to exist, one resolves, "There is nothing at all," and enters absorption; this is called the sphere of nothingness. The sphere of neither existence nor nonexistence: rising from the sphere of nothingness, one examines, "This perception is like a disease, a boil, a thorn," and suppressing perception, it becomes subtler and subtler. Then, thinking, "If this perception were wholly absent, one would become stupefied, and that will not do," one remains in an extremely subtle perception and enters absorption. Because gross perception is neither present nor wholly absent, it is called the sphere of neither existence nor nonexistence.

The eight liberations are called liberations because they free one from the bonds of the afflictions. The eight are to be correlated with the eight states that come below.

"One with form sees forms" is the first liberation. The meditative absorption that, without first dismantling one's own form, destroys external forms and refutes them as empty, is called "one with form sees forms." The word "liberation" is to be applied to all of them, as shown above.

As for the second liberation, "perceiving no form internally, one sees external forms": dismantling one's own form into its parts as empty is called "perceiving no form internally." "Sees external forms" means refuting external forms, such as cloth and vases, as empty; this is as above.

"Having made the liberation of the beautiful manifest with the body, one dwells having completed it" is the third liberation. Above, having refuted outer and inner phenomena as empty, afflictions such as attachment no longer arise. Then, in order to test whether attachment arises or not even when one sees form, one meditates directly on beautiful form with the mental body; this is "making the liberation of the beautiful manifest with the body." When, even meditating in this way, afflictions such as attachment do not arise, that is called "dwelling having completed it."

"Passing entirely beyond the perceptions of form in every way, with the perceptions of resistance having set, and not attending to the perceptions of diversity, thinking, 'Space is infinite,' one dwells having completed the sphere of infinite space": this is the fourth liberation. One dismantles as empty all the colors and shapes, blue, yellow and so on, that are the form objects of the eye consciousness. Since there is no object, consciousness too does not arise; and when consciousness does not arise, the perception accompanying it does not arise either. This is called "passing entirely beyond the perceptions of form in every way." One dismantles as empty the objects of the ear consciousness and the rest, namely sounds, smells, tastes and tangibles, which are the resistant forms. Since the objects do not arise, the consciousnesses that observe them do not arise either, and since those do not arise, the perceptions accompanying them do not arise either. This is called "the perceptions of resistance having set." Since the five consciousnesses have thus set and do not arise, the mental consciousness that follows upon them and the perception accompanying it do not arise either; this is called "not attending to the perceptions of diversity." Since in this way neither external objects nor the consciousnesses that range over objects arise, one enters absorption thinking, "All phenomena amount to no more than space, and space is infinite." This is called "thinking, 'Space is infinite,' one dwells having completed the sphere of infinite space."

"Passing entirely beyond the sphere of infinite space in every way, thinking, 'Consciousness is infinite,' one dwells having completed the sphere of infinite consciousness": this is the fifth liberation. Stirring the mind from that absorption in which one thinks, "All phenomena amount to no more than space, and space is infinite," one enters absorption thinking that not only is space infinite, but the consciousness accompanying the perception that takes as its object the thought "Space is infinite" is itself infinite, just like space. This is called "passing entirely beyond the sphere of infinite space in every way, one dwells having completed the sphere of infinite consciousness."

"Passing entirely beyond the sphere of infinite consciousness in every way, thinking, 'There is nothing at all,' one dwells having completed the sphere of nothingness": this is the sixth liberation. Moving from the sphere of infinite consciousness, one examines what there is to take as an object beyond consciousness, and seeing not even the slightest phenomenon, whether with form or without form, to exist, one enters absorption thinking, "There is nothing at all." This is called "passing entirely beyond the sphere of infinite consciousness in every way, one dwells having completed the sphere of nothingness."

"Passing entirely beyond the sphere of nothingness in every way, one dwells having completed the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception": this is the seventh liberation. Moving from the sphere of nothingness, one suppresses perception, thinking, "This perception is like a disease, a boil, a thorn; it is faulty," and it becomes subtler and subtler. Then, thinking, "If perception were wholly absent, one would become stupefied, and that will not do," one remains in an extremely subtle perception and enters absorption. This is called "passing beyond the sphere of nothingness in every way, one dwells having completed the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception."

As for "Passing entirely beyond the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception in every way, one dwells having made manifest with the body the cessation of perception and feeling: this is the eighth liberation": moving from the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception is called the absorption of cessation, and this is what is called "passing entirely beyond the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception in every way." In that state perception is stopped, and along with it the six consciousnesses and the afflicted mind are stopped as well. To dwell having stopped them, thinking, "All three kinds of feeling are suffering, and perception is like a disease, a boil, a thorn," is called "dwelling having made manifest with the body the cessation of perception and feeling." Because in this state the afflictions to be abandoned by seeing have been abandoned entirely, and the afflictions to be abandoned by meditation have also been abandoned in part, these are called the eight liberations.

The nine successive absorptions are: conception and analysis; joy; bliss; equanimity; passing entirely beyond the perceptions of form in every way, with the perceptions of resistance having set, and not attending to the perceptions of diversity, thinking, "Space is infinite," one dwells having completed the sphere of infinite space; passing entirely beyond the sphere of infinite space in every way, thinking, "Consciousness is infinite," one dwells having completed the sphere of infinite consciousness; passing entirely beyond the sphere of infinite consciousness in every way, thinking, "There is nothing at all," one dwells having completed the sphere of nothingness;

passing entirely beyond the sphere of nothingness in every way, one dwells having completed the sphere of neither perception nor non-perception; and the absorption of cessation. The explanation of the words of these agrees with the earlier explanation of the eight liberations and the absorption of cessation, so look above. They are called the nine successive absorptions because they are the four meditative concentrations, the four formless absorptions and the absorption of cessation, and one enters them in sequence, one after another in order, from the first meditative concentration until one reaches cessation.

The three doors of liberation are the liberation of emptiness, the liberation of signlessness and the liberation of wishlessness. "Liberation" refers to nirvāṇa. The "three doors" are the causes or means by which that liberation is attained, and refers to the three meditative absorptions. Here phenomena are of two kinds: nonexistent phenomena and existent phenomena. Of these, the imputed nature is a nonexistent phenomenon, and meditating on it is called the door of liberation of emptiness. Existent phenomena are also of two kinds: the conditioned and the unconditioned. Of these, the dependent nature is conditioned. Thinking, "As long as one is involved with that dependent, conditioned nature, there is suffering," one abandons it; this is called the door of liberation of wishlessness. The consummate nature is unconditioned. In it there is nothing established as the signs of color and shape, such as blue and yellow. Attaining it and becoming familiar with it is called the door of liberation of signlessness.

The six superknowledges are the divine eye, the divine ear, the knowledge of the minds of others, the knowledge of bodily miraculous powers, the recollection of former abodes, and the knowledge of the exhaustion of the defilements. To realize and know phenomena that are hidden, because they exist and occur at a great distance or a long time away, directly in the present, as if they were right nearby, is called superknowledge. Seeing all forms, every kind of body near and far, and where beings are born after death, is the divine eye. Hearing and understanding all sorts of sounds, languages and signals is the divine ear. Knowing all the thoughts of sentient beings, and how strong or weak their latent tendencies are, is the knowledge of the minds of others. Knowing how to blaze, to fly, to become many or few, fine or coarse, to walk on water and to sink into the earth, is the knowledge of bodily miraculous powers. Remembering, starting from the present time, the history of former lives, in what place one lived and what one did there, is the recollection of former abodes. Knowing clearly the order in which the afflictions to be abandoned by seeing and by meditation have been abandoned or not abandoned, and knowing that one has attained the knowledge of exhaustion and the knowledge of non-arising, is the knowledge of the exhaustion of the defilements.

The four doors of dhāraṇī are the dhāraṇī that is the cause of attaining patience, the mantra dhāraṇī, the Dharma dhāraṇī and the meaning dhāraṇī. "Dhāraṇī" refers to the certainty that, however many words and letters are spoken, their meaning does not change into something else, or to holding what has been said so that one is never parted from it. "Door" refers to the cause from which such meaning arises; in actual terms it refers to names, words and letters. The dhāraṇī that is the cause of attaining patience: one meditates vividly on the letter A, dismantles it into its parts and realizes it as emptiness; since the other letters are likewise, one extends this to all phenomena too, and the joy that comes from realizing them as emptiness is called the dhāraṇī that is the cause of attaining patience. When one speaks words to sentient beings such as, "May you live long! May you be free of illness! May the harvest be good!" and it comes to be just so, that is called the mantra dhāraṇī. Having heard the holy Dharma from preceptors, masters and others, to retain all the words that were taught and heard without ever forgetting them is called the Dharma dhāraṇī. To grasp quickly the referent of whatever meaning is expressed by words and names, and to retain it without forgetting, is called the meaning dhāraṇī.

The ten powers of the Tathāgata are powers of wisdom: ten kinds of particular knowledge possessed by the Buddha alone. The power of knowing what is possible and what is impossible concerns cause and what is not a cause: he knows that karma and afflictions are the causes that produce sentient beings, and he knows that a self, an agent, Īśvara and the like are not causes that produce sentient beings. Further, it refers to his knowing such things as this: that through practicing virtuous karma it is possible to be born in the higher realms, while it is impossible to be born in the lower realms through it; and that through practicing non-virtue it is possible to be born in the lower realms, while it is impossible to be born in the higher realms through it.

The power of knowing karma and its maturation is the power of knowing that beings are the owners of their karma. It refers to his knowing that when one practices generosity, moral discipline and the like, the happiness of the higher realms, of wealth and so on, comes about, and that by committing evil deeds such as killing, the lower realms and sufferings come about.

The power of knowing all the affliction, purification and emergence of the meditative concentrations, liberations, meditative absorptions and attainments concerns the four meditative concentrations, the eight liberations, meditative absorptions such as the Śūraṃgama, and the nine successive absorptions. Whatever opposing factors interrupt and obscure one's entering them are called affliction. The growth of their remedies is called purification. Emergence means leaving them. The meaning is that he knows all the faults and good qualities of these and all the methods for entering and emerging.

The power of knowing which faculties are superior and which are not superior is his skill in the gradations of faculties: "This being has middling faculties; this one has dull faculties."

The power of knowing the various inclinations is his knowing all the distinctions in how beings incline differently toward the teachings: some beings incline to the Great Vehicle, some incline to the Lesser Vehicle, some incline to the Middle Vehicle; some incline to generosity, some to moral discipline, some to meditative concentration.

The power of knowing the various elements: "element" and "lineage" mean the same thing. It refers to his skill in dividing beings into five lineages according to differences in their fortune and in the strength of their habitual tendencies: the lineages of the three vehicles, greater and lesser, the uncertain lineage, and those without lineage.

The power of knowing the paths that go everywhere is his knowing how beings circle through the paths of the five classes of beings and range throughout the three realms; or else it is his skill in the objects of focus, knowing by abiding in which path one comes to attain the higher realms and liberation.

The power of knowing the recollection of former abodes is his skill in the particulars: "In a former life I lived in this land; my name was this; my father and mother were like this; I did this work; I lived this long; I enjoyed wealth of this kind."

The power of knowing death and birth is his skill in the times: "These beings died and passed from here and were born there; from there too they will die and pass and be born here; and from there too they will be born here."

The power of knowing the exhaustion of the defilements is his skill in the distinctions: "These beings have exhausted the defilements; the defilements, such as the afflictions, of these beings are exhausted." These are called the ten powers.

The four fearlessnesses of the Tathāgata are of two kinds: fearlessness with regard to having attained the perfection of his own aim, and fearlessness with regard to accomplishing the perfection of the aim of others. These are to be correlated in order with what follows. The fearlessness regarding complete and perfect awakening to all phenomena and the fearlessness regarding the exhaustion of all defilements, these two, are the perfection of his own aim; that is, the perfection of wisdom and the perfection of abandonment. Here, because he has fully comprehended all phenomena, conditioned and unconditioned, without exception, he is completely and perfectly awakened. Even if someone were to challenge him, saying, "He has not comprehended all phenomena," he would not be afraid of it; this is the perfection of wisdom. The Buddha himself has exhausted all defilements, such as attachment, and he also knows the exhaustion of defilements in all others. Therefore, if some other person challenges him, saying, "He has not exhausted the defilements," or "He does not know the exhaustion of the defilements," he is not afraid of it; this shows the perfection of abandonment.

The fearlessness regarding the definitive declaration that the obstructing phenomena do not become otherwise, and the fearlessness regarding the fact that the path of definite emergence, by which all perfections are attained, is just so, show the perfection of the aim of others. That is, they are the fearlessness regarding the teaching of the abandonment of the opposing factors and of their remedy. Here, when he declares definitively, "The five sense pleasures are obstacles to liberation, to nirvāṇa," even if someone challenges him, saying, "They are not obstacles," he is not afraid of it. This is called "certain, not becoming otherwise," and it shows his fearlessness regarding the abandonment of the opposing factors. "All perfections" are the perfection of wisdom and the perfection of abandonment. When he teaches, "The path of definite emergence to their attainment is attained through the noble path," it is certainly just so, and even if some other person challenges him, saying, "That path is not attained," he is not afraid of it. The path being just so is called fearlessness regarding the teaching of the remedy.

The four discriminating knowledges are the discriminating knowledge of Dharma, the discriminating knowledge of meaning, the discriminating knowledge of definitive words, and the discriminating knowledge of eloquence. They are called discriminating knowledges because they are four aspects of knowing without error the characteristics of the various distinct phenomena.

The discriminating knowledge of Dharma is to teach the eighty-four thousand forms of conduct of beings with different dispositions, and their remedies, through various means; when explaining the sūtras, to explain them section by section, saying, "Up to here is this general topic; this teaches this and this," and also to explain the meanings of the words in detail; and to know all the many names applied to a single phenomenon, without contradicting the meaning.

The discriminating knowledge of meaning is knowing the meaning expressed and taught by names and words: the general characteristics, impermanent, suffering, empty and selfless; the ultimate characteristics, unborn and unceasing; and the meaning of the true dharmadhātu, which is nondual.

The discriminating knowledge of definitive words is to know the various languages, signals and sounds of sentient beings; to convey many meanings, ten and more, with even a single word; and to speak words that are not confused, halting, repetitive or meaningless, but are praised by the noble ones and make people rejoice.

The discriminating knowledge of eloquence is the ability, whether hearing the holy Dharma from others and asking about doubts, or oneself explaining the Dharma and resolving the doubts of others, to speak pleasingly and meaningfully, like the voice of Brahmā, swiftly, without interruption and without faltering.

The eighteen unshared qualities of the Buddha: because the eighteen qualities that follow are not present even in the slightest in śrāvakas, pratyekabuddhas or ordinary beings, but are possessed by the Buddha alone, they are called the unshared qualities of the Buddha.

As for the Tathāgata having no error: śrāvakas and the like make errors in what they do by way of body, speech and mind, but in the completely pure activity of the Buddha's body, speech and mind there is no error. This is what is meant.

As for having no noisy outcry: when śrāvakas lose their way in a dense forest they shout, and when they go to an empty village they call out, "Who is there?" From former habitual tendencies of delighting in noise, something like a craving for noise arises in them. Since the Buddha has nothing of the kind, this is called having no noisy outcry.

As for having no forgetfulness: śrāvakas engage in knowing only after first reflecting on the matter at hand, but because the Buddha's knowledge is without obstruction and without attachment, this is called having no forgetfulness.

As for having no mind that is not in equipoise: śrāvakas settle into equipoise for a while and then rise from it, but the Buddha, without any entering or emerging, keeps his mind in equipoise at all times, in all four modes of conduct; therefore this is called having no mind that is not in equipoise.

As for having no perception of difference: because śrāvakas regard saṃsāra as a fault and nirvāṇa as a good quality, there arise in them differing perceptions of what to take up and what to reject with respect to phenomena such as affliction and purification. But the Buddha, through great compassion and nonconceptual primordial wisdom, has realized them as emptiness: saṃsāra, being emptiness, is nirvāṇa. Therefore he has no differing perceptions such as those of taking up and rejecting, and this is called having no perception of difference.

As for having no equanimity that has not discerned: śrāvakas do not discern what fortune sentient beings have for being trained, and they abandon them without working for their benefit either; therefore they have an equanimity that has not discerned. The Buddha, having discerned what fortune sentient beings have for being trained, leaves in equanimity only those who lack the fortune, for whom even working on their behalf would bring no benefit, while those who have the fortune he does not leave neglected in equanimity. Therefore this is called having no equanimity that has not discerned.

As for having no decline in aspiration: śrāvakas have no inclination to engage in unsurpassed awakening and in the benefit of all sentient beings. Being inclined only toward their own benefit and the benefit of a mere few sentient beings, their aspiration declines. The Buddha is inclined toward the unsurpassed and toward the benefit of sentient beings without exception, and so he has no decline in aspiration.

As for having no decline in diligence: no flagging of body or mind ever arises in him, for even for the sake of a single sentient being he teaches the Dharma for an eon without partaking of food.

As for having no decline in mindfulness: he knows in advance the conduct, the dispositions, the latent tendencies, the faculties and so forth of all sentient beings, and the particulars of the antidotes and of what is discordant with them, which being is to be trained by which means, and so on, and his mindfulness is held by primordial wisdom. Because he therefore does not need, as śrāvakas do, to reflect on the spot at the present moment, this is called having no decline in mindfulness.

As for having no decline in wisdom: he knows the conduct of sentient beings and the eighty-four thousand doors of the Dharma, and he works inexhaustibly for the benefit of sentient beings through inexhaustible discriminating knowledge. Therefore this is called having no decline in wisdom.

As for having no decline in meditative absorption: since śrāvakas enter meditative absorption only for a while, it declines when they are not in it. Since the Buddha rests in it at all times without decline, this is called having no decline.

As for having no decline in liberation: śrāvakas dissolve one-sidedly into peace and are liberated from the obscuration of the afflictions alone, and this is called declined. The Buddha is liberated from both obscurations, that of the afflictions and that of the knowable; he attains non-abiding nirvāṇa and works for the benefit of sentient beings for as long as saṃsāra lasts. Therefore this is called undeclined liberation.

As for all bodily actions being preceded by primordial wisdom and following primordial wisdom: formerly, when the Buddha was acting as a bodhisattva, he set his intention in advance on all the activities he would perform, and that performance of activity became spontaneously accomplished. Therefore, at the time of his buddhahood, he does not need, as śrāvakas do, to reflect on the spot about all the activities he performs; whatever he does, he performs the activity while knowing it in advance through primordial wisdom. Therefore this is called being preceded by primordial wisdom and following primordial wisdom.

As for all verbal actions being preceded by primordial wisdom and following primordial wisdom: it is because, having first determined through primordial wisdom what will be of benefit to sentient beings, his speech endowed with sixty aspects comes forth in accord with the dispositions of sentient beings.

As for all mental actions being preceded by primordial wisdom and following primordial wisdom: this is the activity of his mind. Having first abandoned through primordial wisdom all conceptual thought and conceptual elaboration, he follows the ultimate and rests his mind in the dharmadhātu. Therefore this is called following primordial wisdom.

As for engaging in the seeing of primordial wisdom that is unattached and unobstructed with regard to past time: this refers to his knowing without error the whole history of whatever phenomena arose, and whatever virtuous and nonvirtuous karma was performed, in former times, back beyond countless eons.

The two, engaging in the seeing of primordial wisdom that is unattached and unobstructed with regard to future time, and engaging in the seeing of primordial wisdom that is unattached and unobstructed with regard to present time, are to be applied in the same way as above: because he clearly knows and perceives every phenomenon that will arise in future time, and every phenomenon that arises right now in all the ten directions, this is called engaging in the seeing of primordial wisdom.

What are the four fruits of the ascetic? They are the fruit of stream-entry, the fruit of once-returning, the fruit of non-returning, and the fruit of the arhat. Here, to abandon, by means of the sixteen moments of forbearance and knowledge, the eighty-eight afflictions to be abandoned by seeing is called attaining the fruit of stream-entry.

The afflictions of the desire realm to be abandoned by meditation, which are four, are gathered into one and divided into nine, and their antidote, primordial wisdom, is likewise divided into nine; abandoning them gradually, to have abandoned up to the sixth is called the fruit of once-returning. To have gradually abandoned all nine is called the fruit of non-returning.

In the form and formless realms, the afflictions to be abandoned by meditation are three in each, making six, and these too are gathered into one. The four meditative concentrations and the four formless absorptions make eight levels, and each is again divided into nine. Abandoning them gradually, at the time when, at the Peak of Existence, the smallest of the small afflictions is abandoned by the vajra-like, the greatest of the great primordial wisdom, this is called the fruit of the arhat.

What are the three paths? They are solitary awakening (pratyekabodhi), the knowledge of the aspects of the path, and omniscience. Here, by the cause of having heard the sublime Dharma in the past, in a time like the present one realizes on one's own the dharma of dependent origination, and without relying on a spiritual friend one realizes the selflessness of the person and attains arhatship; this is called solitary awakening. Having generated the mind set on unsurpassed awakening, one enters into the selflessness of both phenomena and persons and gradually abandons the two kinds of obscuration through the ten grounds; this is called the knowledge of the aspects of the path. Having gradually passed beyond the ten grounds, on the ground of a buddha the four primordial wisdoms and the three bodies are spontaneously present; this is called omniscience.

What are the Three Jewels? They are the Buddha Jewel, the Dharma Jewel, and the Saṅgha Jewel. Here, the one who has woken from the sleep of ignorance and gained the primordial wisdom of omniscience, and in whom the accumulations of merit and primordial wisdom have unfolded like a lotus in bloom, is the Buddha Jewel. It is to be explained as a jewel because it is supreme in good qualities and rare in the world.

The Dharma is the Dharma of explanation, the Dharma of practice, and the Dharma of the result. Of these, the twelve branches of the scriptures are the explanation. The factors conducive to awakening, such as the perfections, are the practice. Nirvāṇa with remainder of the aggregates and nirvāṇa without remainder of the aggregates are called the Dharma of the result.

The Saṅgha consists of ordinary beings, those still in training, and those beyond training. Those fully ordained through the formal act consisting of a motion and three proclamations are ordinary beings. Those from the stream-enterer up to the one who has entered upon arhatship are those still in training. The arhat is one beyond training. A gathering of many such persons, four or more, is called the Saṅgha.

What are the three bodies? They are the dharma body, the enjoyment body, and the emanation body. Here, the dharmadhātu and the mirror-like primordial wisdom are the dharma body. The body that teaches the Dharma to the bodhisattvas of the tenth ground in the palace of Akaniṣṭha, the highest heaven, and is adorned with the major marks and the excellent minor marks, is the enjoyment body. That which displays the body of the Buddha Śākyamuni in Jambudvīpa is the emanation body.

The three natures are the imputed nature, the dependent nature, and the perfected nature. Here, the six objects such as form and the six sense faculties, as wrongly imputed by a mistaken consciousness that thinks they exist as entities, as phenomena and as persons, are the imputed nature. The all-base consciousness together with its retinue, arising from a continuum of moments in the manner of cause and effect, is the dependent nature. Emptiness, the dharmadhātu, and nonconceptual primordial wisdom are called the perfected nature.

What are the three vehicles? They are the Śrāvaka Vehicle, the Pratyekabuddha Vehicle, and going forth by the Great Vehicle. Here, to rely on a spiritual friend and, by knowing the four, the truth of suffering, origin, cessation, and the truth of the path, each in four aspects, to realize the selflessness of the person and abandon the obscuration of the afflictions, is the Śrāvaka Vehicle. Without relying on a spiritual friend, to realize the characteristics of dependent origination as it arises in forward order and as it is reversed, and so to realize the selflessness of the person and abandon the obscuration of the afflictions, is the Pratyekabuddha Vehicle. To accomplish gradually the ten perfections and the ten grounds, to realize the selflessness of both phenomena and persons, and so to abandon both obscurations, that of the afflictions and that of the knowable, is called going forth by the Great Vehicle.

What are their five distinctions? They are the distinction of lineage, the distinction of engagement, the distinction of time, the distinction of awakening, and the distinction of liberation. Here, the differences of greater and lesser scope among the three vehicles are called their five distinctions. Thus, having differing imprints from having previously heard the four truths, the twelve links of dependent origination, or the grounds and perfections, is called the distinction of lineage. The lesser and middling vehicles engage in their own aim, while the Great Vehicle accomplishes the aims of both self and others; this is called the distinction of engagement. Śrāvakas attain arhatship in three lifetimes or seven lifetimes, and pratyekabuddhas in a hundred eons, while those of the Great Vehicle awaken fully in three countless great eons; these are the distinction of time. The two vehicles realize the selflessness of the person, while the Great Vehicle realizes the selflessness of both phenomena and persons; this is called the distinction of awakening. Through the two vehicles one attains the arhatship of one-sided peace, while through the Great Vehicle one attains non-abiding nirvāṇa; these are called the distinction of liberation.

The three baskets are what is included in the Sūtra collection, what is included in the Vinaya, and what is included in the Mātṛkā. Here, of the twelve branches of the scriptures, seven, namely the class of discourses, the class of melodic recitations, the class of verses, the class of inspired utterances, the class of explanations, the class of extensive teachings, and the class of marvels, are included in the Sūtra collection. Four, namely the class of introductory accounts, the class of narratives of deeds, the class of accounts of what happened thus, and the class of birth stories, are included in the Vinaya. The class of teachings that establish definitively is the Abhidharma, and this is included without error.

And what is the purpose of this? It is in order to complete the three trainings. Within the Sūtra collection meditative absorption is taught extensively; within the Vinaya turning away from evil actions is taught; within the Mātṛkā the specific and general characteristics of the various phenomena are taught. Therefore they correspond, in that order, to the three, higher mind, higher moral discipline, and higher wisdom, and bring them to completion. That is the meaning.

What are the three kinds of wisdom? They are wisdom arising from hearing, wisdom arising from reflection, and wisdom arising from meditation. Here, the understanding that comprehends the Dharma, coarse and subtle, is the three kinds of wisdom. Thus, the understanding that focuses on names, phrases, and letters is the wisdom arising from hearing. The understanding that focuses on names, phrases, and letters together with all the meanings they express is the wisdom arising from reflection. The understanding that focuses one-pointedly on a single meaning is the wisdom arising from meditation.

What are the three trainings? They are higher moral discipline, higher mind, and higher wisdom. Here, the object of activity for the holy ones with the eye of intelligence, who put into practice what is supreme among things to be done, is the three trainings. Non-Buddhists are higher than ordinary people; śrāvakas are higher than non-Buddhists; pratyekabuddhas are higher than they; and the Great Vehicle is higher than the pratyekabuddhas.

The six, male and female, among the śrāvakas' monks, novices, and lay practitioners, together with the intermediate precepts, are the seven classes of discipline. These, together with the three vows of the bodhisattva, namely restraint from nonvirtue, gathering virtue, and acting for the benefit of sentient beings, are higher moral discipline.

The meditative absorptions of the Great Vehicle, such as Śūraṃgama, and the śrāvakas' meditation on the unattractive, the four meditative concentrations, and the formless absorptions, are higher mind.

The Great Vehicle knows the selflessness of both phenomena and persons, while the śrāvakas understand the selflessness of the person; this is higher wisdom.

What are the three faculties? They are the faculty of coming fully to know what was not fully known, the faculty of full knowledge, and the faculty of having realized full knowledge. Here, at the time of the path at which what is to be abandoned by seeing is abandoned, there are the five faculties of faith, diligence, mindfulness, meditative absorption, and wisdom, and the four faculties of pleasure, mental pleasure, equanimity, and the mental faculty. When these nine have abandoned what is to be abandoned by seeing, then, because everything not known before is known, they are called the faculty of coming fully to know what was not fully known. At the time of abandoning what is to be abandoned by meditation, the nine faculties clearly distinguish the discordant side and the side of the antidote; this is called the faculty of full knowledge. At the time of arhatship, the nine faculties that have abandoned without remainder the afflictions of seeing and of meditation are called the faculty of having realized full knowledge.

What are the three afflictions? They are the thorough affliction of the afflictions, the thorough affliction of karma, and the thorough affliction of life. Here, they are called the three afflictions because each is itself affliction, and what is born from it as its cause also becomes affliction. Thus, of the twelve links of dependent origination, the three, ignorance, craving, and grasping, are the thorough affliction of the afflictions. The two, formations and becoming, are the thorough affliction of karma. The seven, consciousness, name and form, the six sense sources, contact, feeling, birth, and aging and death, are the thorough affliction of life; this is saṃsāra, the cause and result of every kind of suffering.

What are the three conventionals? They are the conventional of the conventional, the incorrect conventional, and the correct conventional. Of these, things such as illusions, mirages, and optical illusions, whose characteristic is that they cannot perform their respective functions, are the conventional of the conventional. Whatever is produced by misconceiving and constructing the outer and inner phenomena of dependent origination is the incorrect conventional. The causes and results produced by the ten virtues and so forth through correctly examining outer and inner dependent origination are the correct conventional.

What are the three meanings of the terms for the conventional qualities of a bodhisattva? They are aspiration, application, and realization. These are matched, in order, with the three terms "bodhi," "sattva," and "great sattva." To dwell in the four aspects conducive to penetration is called "bodhi," and this is matched with aspiration. To dwell on the grounds from the first up to the tenth is called "bodhisattva," and this is matched with application. At the time of the eleventh ground, the ground of a buddha, one is called "great sattva," and this is matched with realization.

Alternatively, there are teaching sentient beings, establishing them in awakening, and being a bodhisattva. Because one teaches sentient beings the unsurpassed path, one is "bodhi"; this is matched with teaching sentient beings. Because one brings those one has set on the path to attain the result, one is "bodhisattva"; this is matched with establishing them in awakening. Because one also brings oneself to attain great awakening, one is "great sattva"; this is matched with being heroic-minded toward awakening, and it shows that one possesses the qualities of the definition.

What are the three realms? They are the desire realm, the form realm, and the formless realm. Here, the eight hells together with the two, animals and hungry ghosts, make ten; the four continents and the six classes of desire gods make ten; and in all these twenty abodes are the desire realm. The seventeen abodes from the first meditative concentration up to the fourth are the form realm. The four abodes from the sphere of infinite space up to neither perception nor non-perception are the formless realm.

What is the reason with three modes? It is being present in what is to be inferred, being present in the similar side, and being absent from the dissimilar side. Here, although the knowable is not manifest, to bring it to a conclusion by way of interconnection is called the reason with three modes. Thus, although fire and water are not seen, to infer that fire and water are present merely from seeing smoke and water birds is being present in what is to be inferred. To infer that fire is present in a great kitchen is being present in the similar side, because a kitchen and the presence of fire agree. To infer that there is no fire within water is being absent from the dissimilar side, because the two are opposed to each other like enemies.

What are the three sufferings? They are the suffering of suffering, the suffering of change, and the suffering of conditioning. Here, they are called the three sufferings because the three kinds of feeling in the three realms are pervaded by the three sufferings. Thus, although the sentient beings of the desire realm possess all three feelings, painful feeling predominates, and so they are pervaded by the suffering of suffering. The gods of the form realm, dwelling in meditative concentration, mostly possess pleasant feeling; but although they are happy for a while, they do not remain there permanently, and when their life is exhausted they change into something else. Therefore they are pervaded by the suffering of change.

In the formless realm, since the four aggregates designated by name abide as a continuum of moments, beings there possess the feeling of equanimity. Although ordinary people do not feel this as suffering, the noble ones see it as suffering. As a sūtra also says:

A single strand of wool laid on the palm of the hand
Is not noticed by people,
But if it gets into the eye, that same strand
Produces discomfort and harm.
The childish are like the palm of the hand:
They do not notice the hair of the suffering of conditioning.
The wise are like the eye:
Because of it they are deeply disquieted.

For the childish, the thought of suffering does not arise even with regard to the aggregates in Avīci hell, while for the noble ones the thought of suffering enters even with regard to the Peak of Existence. This shows that it is pervaded by the suffering of conditioning.

What are the three countless eons? From the ground of conduct through aspiration up to the first ground is one; from the first ground up to the eighth ground is one; and from the eighth ground up to the tenth ground is one. Here, the first eon begins when the lifespan, having decreased from immeasurable, has become eighty thousand years, and from then the human lifespan gradually decreases until it is ten years; this is one intermediate eon. Then the lifespan again becomes eighty thousand years, and once more by gradual decrease becomes ten years; that period of increasing and decreasing is the second intermediate eon. There are eighteen such, and at the end of them there is again one eon of increase from ten years to eighty thousand, together with the first eon of decrease. In twenty such intermediate eons the world forms; for twenty it abides; for twenty it is destroyed; and for twenty it remains empty. Eighty intermediate eons are called one great eon, and passing beyond the limit of counting such eons is one countless eon. These are matched in order with taking one such eon each for the ground of conduct through aspiration, for the grounds up to the eighth, and for the grounds up to the ground of a buddha, Universal Light.

What are the three persons? They are the person with doubt, the person with wrong knowledge, and the person without knowledge. Here, one whose mind is not certain, thinking, "Are the characteristics of phenomena impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness, or are they not?" is the person with doubt. One who firmly holds that phenomena are permanent, pleasant, pure, and possessed of a self is the person with wrong knowledge. One who has no knowledge at all of the characteristics of phenomena, whether impermanence or permanence and so forth, is called the person without knowledge.

What are the three unshared close applications? They are an even mind toward those who listen respectfully, an even mind toward those who do not listen respectfully, and an even mind toward those who listen respectfully and those who do not. These are absent in ordinary people and in śrāvakas and are present only in the Buddha, and so they are called the unshared close applications. Thus, even when his students and retinue show respect and service and listen to the Dharma diligently, he feels no special delight; this is an even mind toward those who listen respectfully. When they are without respect and obstinate, their service is slight, and they will not listen to the Dharma, still no ill will or anger arises in him; this is an even mind toward those who do not listen respectfully. When at times they are respectful and diligent in service and in listening to the Dharma, and at times not diligent and unwilling either to serve or to listen to the Dharma, then too no delight arises in him when they are respectful, and no anger when they are disrespectful; this is called an even mind toward those who listen respectfully and those who do not.

What are the three distinctions of giving? Giving is the giving of material things, the giving of children and wife, and the giving of life and body. Here, ordinary people, even when they are rich and have possessions, give only what they do not cherish, and only to their children and relatives; when they have nothing, or have something but cherish it, they do not give it to others. The noble ones give even what they cherish to all sentient beings without stinting, and when they have nothing they give even their body and life. Therefore these are the three distinctions of giving. Giving food and drink for the moment, and lasting goods, gold, silver, clothing, and food, is the giving of material things. Giving away children, wife, servants, retinue, and livelihood, up to a kingdom, is the giving of children and wife. Giving away head, hands and feet, flesh and blood, up to the life of the body, is the giving of life and body.

What are the four summaries of the Dharma? They are impermanence, suffering, emptiness, and selflessness. Since the meaning of all outer and inner phenomena is gathered into these four words, they are the four summaries of the Dharma. They are also matched with the three natures. The imputed nature is impermanent because it never exists. The dependent nature is suffering because it is the basis of all suffering. In the perfected nature there is no entity to be designated as a phenomenon, and so it is empty; and there is no entity to be designated as a person, and so it is selfless.

What are the four parts of the bodhisattva's conduct through aspiration? They are the increase of illumination, partial entry into the meaning of suchness, and the uninterrupted meditative absorption. Here, at the time of cultivating the five faculties and the five powers, to be wholly and especially inclined and devoted to the side of virtue, with effort, is the four parts of conduct through aspiration. Of these, the arising of the wisdom that knows all the names designated for outer and inner phenomena to be mere illusion is the attainment of illumination. The arising of the wisdom that the entities of the meanings expressed by those names are nonexistent, mere illusion, is the increase of illumination. The arising of the wisdom that knows those names of phenomena as emptiness is partial entry into the meaning of suchness. The arising of the wisdom that knows all the phenomena which are the meanings expressed by those names as emptiness is called the uninterrupted meditative absorption, because immediately after it one reaches the first ground.

What are the four parts of the śrāvaka? They are heat, summit, forbearance, and the supreme worldly dharma. Here, the four parts are like the above: at the time of the faculties and powers there is inclination toward and aspiration for virtue.

For example, when fire is produced with a fire drill, warmth comes first as a sign of the fire; in the same way there arises a primordial wisdom like fire that burns the afflictions, and so this is called heat. Then, once the roots of virtue, the side of the antidote, have grown strong, one reaches the stage at which one no longer falls under the power of what is discordant, and so this is called summit. To have confidence in emptiness, the meaning of the selflessness of the person, and for joy in it to arise, is called forbearance. Up to this point it is worldly virtue. Although the afflictions of seeing and of meditation have not yet been abandoned, this borders on the noble grounds, such as stream-entry, and since it is foremost among all other worldly states, it is called the supreme worldly dharma.

What are the four primordial wisdoms? They are the mirror-like primordial wisdom, the primordial wisdom of equality, the discriminating primordial wisdom, and the primordial wisdom that accomplishes activity; and the pure dharmadhātu as well, in that order, it is said. Here, when one has passed from being an ordinary being to accomplishing buddhahood, the transformation of the eight consciousnesses is the four primordial wisdoms. Of these, the all-base consciousness, purified of its stains and transformed, is the mirror-like primordial wisdom. The afflicted mind, purified of the stain of viewing a self and transformed, does not divide self and other but is even, and so it is the primordial wisdom of equality. The mental consciousness, purified of stains such as attachment and transformed, is the discriminating primordial wisdom. The consciousnesses of the five doors, such as the eye, purified of the stain of conceiving the various phenomena as distinct and transformed, are the primordial wisdom that accomplishes activity. The emptiness that is the intrinsic nature of the five aggregates, such as the aggregate of form, is the pure dharmadhātu, and it is set forth as the object of supreme primordial wisdom.

What are the activities of the four primordial wisdoms? They are the activity of the mirror-like primordial wisdom, the activity of the primordial wisdom of equality, the activity of the discriminating primordial wisdom, and the activity of the primordial wisdom that accomplishes activity. Here, engaging and focusing on distinct objects is the activity of the primordial wisdoms. Thus, to focus on the dharmadhātu, emptiness, the nondual meaning of all phenomena, is the activity of the mirror-like primordial wisdom. To focus on self and other as indivisible, so that accomplishing one's own aim is also the aim of sentient beings, and accomplishing the aim of sentient beings is also one's own aim, is the activity of the primordial wisdom of equality. To determine without error the general and specific characteristics of all phenomena is the activity of the discriminating primordial wisdom. To accomplish every aim of sentient beings by various means and to perform the activity of emanations is the activity of the primordial wisdom that accomplishes activity.

What are the four intentions? They are the intention regarding sameness, the intention regarding another time, the intention regarding another meaning, and the intention regarding another person. Here, although what is understood at first and the meaning to which it is applied differ, a single statement teaches without contradiction, and by such a means sentient beings come to be trained; these are called the four intentions.

Thus, the past buddhas and Śākyamuni do not differ, as greater or lesser, in the dharma body, in the accumulations of merit and primordial wisdom, or in the activity they perform for the benefit of sentient beings; and on that basis the Buddha Śākyamuni said, "I myself was the earlier buddha of the past." This is the intention regarding sameness.

It is said, "Merely by reciting the name of a single buddha, such as the Buddha Amitābha or Avalokiteśvara, one will attain buddhahood." This does not mean that one awakens immediately through it, but that if one practices relying on that very cause, one will at some time awaken. Likewise, the saying, "If you make an aspiration, it will come about just so," does not mean that it comes about immediately, but that at some time it becomes a cause. This is the intention regarding another time.

The saying, "Unless one has honored as many buddhas as there are sands in the Ganges, one will not hear this sūtra," does not refer here to merely encountering it in the present; it refers to a bodhisattva who has attained the first ground. This is the intention regarding another meaning.

To praise whatever aspect of virtue, such as moral discipline, a person has faith in and is capable of, and so have them practice it; or else, for one who thinks that keeping moral discipline alone is enough, to disparage moral discipline and praise generosity and so forth, in order to have them practice other virtues as well, and to apply this to all the perfections: this is the intention regarding another person.

What are the indirect intentions? They are the indirect intention regarding introduction, the indirect intention regarding characteristics, the indirect intention regarding antidotes, and the indirect intention regarding transformation. Here, what is spoken as a means of guiding others, with only what can lead them in mind, even though the words are not definitive, is indirect intention.

When non-Buddhists ask whether a self exists or not, the reply "A self exists" is given on the basis merely of there being a momentary continuum of sentient beings, or beings born miraculously. When śrāvakas ask whether the entities of phenomena exist or not, the reply "The aggregates, the elements, and the sense sources exist" is given. To explain things so, in order that people may hold to the Great Vehicle and to this teaching and not be frightened, is the indirect intention regarding introduction.

When it is asked whether all phenomena exist as entities or not, then, with the three natures in view, to those who hold that they do not exist it is taught that they do not exist, with the imputed nature in mind; and to those who hold that they exist it is taught that they exist, with the two, the dependent and the perfected natures, in mind. This is the indirect intention regarding characteristics.

As the antidote to the eighty-four thousand kinds of conduct of sentient beings, eighty-four thousand doors of the Dharma are taught; and the teaching of the unattractive and so forth as the antidote to attachment is the indirect intention regarding antidotes.

It is said, "Having killed one's father, killed one's mother, caused a schism in the Saṅgha, killed an arhat, and drawn the blood of a Tathāgata with evil intent, one will awaken fully into unsurpassed awakening." This is not to be done literally, as the words say. Rather, to know ignorance as without entity is killing the mother. To know hatred as without entity is killing the father. To break the five aggregates down into emptiness, so that they are without entity, is the schism of the Saṅgha. To know the all-base consciousness together with its habitual tendencies as without entity is killing the arhat.

To realize the dharmadhātu, through wisdom, as emptiness is given the changed name "drawing the blood of a Tathāgata."

Or again, it is said:

Taking what lacks essence to be the essence,
Abiding firmly in the inverted,
And thoroughly afflicted by the afflictions,
One will attain supreme awakening.

These lines are to be connected, in order, with meditating without distraction, abiding turned back from the inverted, and practicing austerities through various hardships. To change the wording to "if one practices in that way, one will awaken" is the covert intention regarding transformation.

What are the four kinds of reasoning? They are the reasoning of dependence, the reasoning of efficacy, the reasoning of valid cognition, and the reasoning of the nature of reality. Here, that things come about in the world in accordance with the way phenomena can possibly be is what is called reasoning. Thus eye consciousness cannot arise on its own; it is reasonable that it arises in dependence on two things, the eye faculty and its object, form. Apply this likewise to other things as well: a sprout arises in dependence on a seed, formations arise in dependence on ignorance, and so on. This is the reasoning of dependence.

Eye consciousness, once it has arisen, performs the function of seeing forms, but it does not listen to sounds. And the eye faculty produces eye consciousness, but it does not produce other consciousnesses such as ear consciousness. Apply this likewise to everything: a barley seed produces barley itself, but it does not produce something else, such as buckwheat or peas. These are the reasoning of efficacy.

It is also called the establishing of what is tenable. It is the definitive establishment of the valid cognitions of inference, direct perception and scripture. Of these, inference is knowing fire from smoke, and knowing water from waterbirds. Direct perception is seeing by the six consciousnesses, and seeing by the mind of a yogin. Scripture is undeceiving speech, the Buddha's word spoken from his own mouth. These are the reasoning of valid cognition.

Water flowing downward, the sun rising in the east, earth being solid, water being wet, fire being hot, wind being light, and so on, together with such things as emptiness and selflessness: these general and specific characteristics, which have been so from the very beginning, are called the reasoning of the nature of reality.

What are the four knowledges? They are knowing the element, knowing the nature, knowing the disposition, and knowing the latent tendencies. Here, to comprehend which of the different families, of the greater or the lesser vehicle, each being belongs to is knowing the element. Element, family and seed all have the same meaning. To comprehend the distinctions among sentient beings of sharp faculties, dull faculties and middling faculties is knowing the nature. To comprehend which of the six or the ten perfections, such as generosity, each one delights in is knowing the disposition. To comprehend how great or small a share each has of the seeds of virtue and non-virtue, of faith and diligence, or of attachment, aversion and the rest, is knowing the latent tendencies.

What are the four modes of birth? They are birth from a womb, birth from an egg, birth from warmth and moisture, and miraculous birth. Here, humans, cattle and sheep, beasts of prey and the like are for the most part born from a womb. Birds, fish, frogs, lizards and the like are born from an egg. Insects and worms are born from warmth and moisture. The gods of the higher realms, together with demons and ogres, hungry ghosts and hell beings, are born miraculously.

What are the four extremes? They are the extreme of existence, the extreme of non-existence, the extreme that what exists exists and what does not exist does not exist, and the extreme of neither existence nor non-existence. Here, to say: "All outer and inner phenomena exist, since they are transformations of the three, rajas, tamas and sattva. Applying this to outer phenomena, all that is lovely and beautiful, such as flowers, has a predominance of sattva; all that is ugly, such as thorns and ravines, has a predominance of tamas; and grains, fruits, food and the like are predominantly transformations of rajas. So they exist" — this is the extreme of existence. The worldly Lokāyatas' saying "There is nothing at all: no karma, no result, no former or later lives, and so on" is the extreme of non-existence. To say "The permanent self exists, but the city of the elements, the objects, the faculties and so on, does not exist" is the extreme that what exists exists and what does not exist does not exist. The Sāṃkhyas say: "For example, the color of gold does not change at all, so it is not non-existent; yet when a smith hammers it, it turns into one shape after another, so it is not existent. In the same way, the permanent self is just like that." This is the extreme of neither existence nor non-existence.

What are the four reliances? One should rely on the meaning, not on the letter. One should rely on the Dharma, not on the person. One should rely on wisdom, not on consciousness. One should rely on sūtras of definitive meaning, not on sūtras of provisional meaning. To put these into practice, and never parting from them, to rely on them and grow familiar with them, is what is called reliance.

Further, the way of the supramundane Dharma, the result of earnestly cultivating the factors conducive to awakening, in which, in a single instant of the mind's application, the three spheres are completely purified and the wisdom of omniscience is made manifest: this is the meaning, and it is on this that one should rely. As for the letter: engaging in many worldly activities, or else following the words and letters of studying and expounding as far as the eighty-four thousand gateways of the Dharma, are of little meaning, so one should not rely on them.

A person who teaches, without inversion and without confusion, the way of cause and result of the phenomena of purification and of thorough affliction, or who definitively teaches that the characteristic of the meaning has not arisen and is unborn, may be anyone at all, of whatever sort: if he is not at odds with the Dharma, one should rely on him. But a person who has not been examined, a braggart whose conduct is out of keeping with the Dharma, even if he is a king's son, one should not draw near to and rely on. This is relying on the Dharma and not relying on the person.

To know that the characteristic of all phenomena is, in the ultimate sense, free of arising and ceasing, and that phenomena and persons are selfless, is wisdom; one relies on it and grows familiar with it. To follow after forms, sounds, smells and the rest, the five qualities of sensual desire and so on, is called consciousness. Not to be attached to it, not to dwell in it, and not to rely on it, is relying on wisdom and not relying on consciousness.

A teaching of the Dharma through which one comes to understand the profound and vast meaning is called a sūtra of definitive meaning, and one relies on it and grows familiar with it. Those that teach weariness with saṃsāra, engagement with the conventional, and so on, are sūtras of provisional meaning. Not to rely on them and grow familiar with them is relying on sūtras of definitive meaning and not relying on sūtras of provisional meaning.

What are the four means of gathering? They are generosity, pleasant speech, purposeful conduct, and consistency of aim. Here, to satisfy others with whatever bodily necessities they need, such as food and clothing, is generosity. After their minds have been satisfied, to teach and explain the sublime Dharma, turning them back from non-virtue and setting them in virtue, is pleasant speech. Not merely to say it in words, but oneself to put it into practice in accordance with what one says, is purposeful conduct. Just as one has taught the Dharma to others, so to act in accordance with it oneself and to have others act on it as well, is consistency of aim.

What are the four unconditioned things? They are cessation through analysis, cessation not through analysis, space, and suchness. Because they are not produced by any characteristic of causes and conditions, they are called unconditioned. Thus, knowing that these phenomena of the three realms are impermanent and suffering, one is not attached to the five qualities of sensual desire and does not engage in them; and when one does not engage in them, habitual tendencies do not accumulate and no result arises. This is called cessation through analysis. When one is occupied with and distracted by, say, a form, then even if sounds, smells and the like arise, one does not attend to them; since they pass by, habitual tendencies do not accumulate and no result arises. Likewise, being distracted by a sound and not attending to a form, and so on, since there is no investigation by wisdom, is called cessation not through analysis. That which has no arising and coming forth, as cause and result do, and which gives room to other things, is space. That in the nature of reality of phenomena there is nothing like cause and result is called suchness.

What are the four kinds of answer? They are the categorical answer, the answer by distinguishing, the answer after a counter-question, and the answer by setting aside. Here, to give a definite reply to what another has asked is an answer. Thus, if one is asked, "Do these sentient beings die or not?", to teach, "Of all who have become sentient beings, there is none who does not die; they die," is the categorical answer. If one is asked, "Are these sentient beings born or not?", to teach, "When the afflictions are exhausted, there are also those who are not born in the three realms through the power of karma," is the answer by distinguishing. If one is asked, "Are these humans supreme or not?", one asks back, "Do you ask in relation to the gods, or in relation to demons?" Then to teach, "If you say in relation to the gods, they are not supreme; if you say in relation to demons, they are supreme," is the answer after a counter-question. If one is asked, "Do sentient beings have a self or not?", one cannot teach that it exists, since it is not established by intrinsic nature; and one cannot teach that it does not exist, since it now appears and is present to direct perception. So to set the question aside, teaching nothing at all, is the answer by setting aside.

What are the five fields of knowledge? They are the inner science, the science of reasoning, the science of language, the science of healing, and the science of crafts. Since the objects to be known by wisdom, when summarized, are five, they are the five fields of knowledge. Thus, to know the twelve branches of the scriptures is the inner science. To be skilled in logic (tarka), or else in the reasons why this or that result arises from this or that cause and condition in outer and inner dependent origination, is the science of reasoning. To be skilled in the language of grammar (vyākaraṇa), such as drawing ten different meanings from a single word, is the science of language. To be skilled in medicine and treatment, knowing which remedy helps which illness, as with the four hundred and four diseases of the body, is the science of healing. To be skilled in the many methods of everything that is to be done, such as crafts and archery, is the science of crafts.

A treatise too says:

Without striving in the five fields of knowledge,
Not even the supreme noble one will become omniscient.
Therefore, to refute others and to care for them,
And to know for oneself, one strives in them.

Reasoning and language are logic and grammar: by these one refutes opponents. Through the crafts and the science of medicine one benefits others and cares for them. And to clear away the afflictions in one's own mind there is the inner science, for it is said that when one is skilled in the twelve branches of the scriptures, wisdom arises, and through it one becomes noble.

What are the five uncontaminated aggregates? They are the aggregate of moral discipline, the aggregate of meditative absorption, the aggregate of wisdom, the aggregate of liberation, and the aggregate of the seeing of the wisdom of liberation. Here, the seven classes of the śrāvakas' discipline and the three kinds of bodhisattva vows are the aggregate of moral discipline. Meditative absorptions such as the Heroic Progress, the four meditative concentrations, the four attainments, and so on, are the aggregate of meditative absorption. To realize general and specific characteristics without error, and to understand that phenomena and persons are selfless, is the aggregate of wisdom. Nirvāṇa with a remainder of the aggregates, and nirvāṇa with no remainder of the aggregates at all, is the aggregate of liberation. The four wisdoms of the Great Vehicle, and, for śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas, the thought that comes immediately on attaining nirvāṇa, "What was to be done has been done, the holy life (brahmacarya) has been lived, I know no existence other than this one," are the aggregate of the seeing of the wisdom of liberation.

What are the five dharmas? They are name, sign, conceptuality, suchness, and nonconceptual wisdom. Since all phenomena are gathered into this summary, they are the five dharmas.

Moreover, although it does not appear as the domain of consciousness and is inexpressible, it is designated and indicated by a name in order to teach it: this is name. The seven consciousnesses and the all-base consciousness abiding in relation to one another as cause and result is sign. Because the eight consciousnesses realize the characteristics of phenomena, both their intrinsic natures, such as the solidity of earth and the wetness of water, and their particular features, such as impermanence and suffering, this is conceptuality. All phenomena are not established as entities; since they are emptiness, the dharmadhātu is suchness. The correct wisdom, unerring and perfectly established, is nonconceptual wisdom.

What are the five acts of immediate retribution? They are killing one's mother, killing one's father, killing an arhat, causing a schism in the Saṅgha, and drawing the blood of a Tathāgata with evil intent. If one commits any one of these five acts, one is born for a long time in the realm of hell, a place where the stream of great suffering is not interrupted even for a moment, and there is no opportunity for liberation; this is why they are called "without interval." Killing one's mother, killing one's father and killing an arhat are easy to understand. A schism in the Saṅgha is dividing, in its views, a Saṅgha large enough to form a community. Drawing the blood of a Tathāgata with evil intent is drawing his blood with the intent to kill. Although the Buddha does not dwell here nowadays, this is applied to such things as destroying and rejecting the sublime Dharma.

What are the five acts close to those of immediate retribution? They are defiling a female arhat, killing a bodhisattva who abides on the ground of certainty, killing one in training, seizing the Saṅgha's source of income, and destroying a stūpa. Although the suffering they bring is greater than that of other hells, it is not as severe and as long as the suffering of immediate retribution itself; therefore they are called "close to those of immediate retribution." As for these acts: if one engages in impure conduct, the act of sexual intercourse, with a woman who is one's own mother and who has also attained arhatship, that is defiling a female arhat. To cut off the life of a bodhisattva abiding anywhere from the first ground up to the tenth ground is killing a bodhisattva who abides on the ground of certainty. To cut off the life of any of the seven persons, from the stream-enterer up to those who have not yet attained arhatship itself, is killing one in training. To use for one's own personal ends, or to steal and seize, the food that belongs to the Saṅgha in common, or its common store of livestock and wealth, is seizing the Saṅgha's source of income. To tear down and defile, with evil intent, things such as temples, sacred images and caityas, is destroying a stūpa.

What are the five degenerations? They are the degeneration of lifespan, the degeneration of views, the degeneration of afflictions, the degeneration of the age, and the degeneration of beings. Whatever remains as the worst and last, after the good has been picked out, is called the dregs. Thus, after lifespans of eighty-four thousand years and so on, lifespan grows shorter and shorter until the last, a lifespan of ten years: this is the degeneration of lifespan. Having fallen away from the view that phenomena and persons are selfless and that karma and its results and so on are never lost, views that grasp at the extremes of permanence and annihilation are the degeneration of views. Having fallen away from loving-kindness and compassion free of attachment and aversion, attachment, aversion, jealousy, avarice and the like grow exceedingly strong: this is the degeneration of afflictions. Having fallen away from the time when barley, rice and the like grew back by themselves overnight as soon as they were reaped, with no need of plowing or digging, and when there was no beating, abuse, fighting or quarreling, being tormented by the eons of famine, the eons of disease and the eons of weapons is the degeneration of the age. Having fallen away from the excellent age when human bodies were as large as a krośa, or at the least a fathom, human bodies become a cubit or less, small enough to make their dwelling on a horse's dropping and the like, and beings have ugly faces and evil intentions: this is the degeneration of beings.

What are the five paths? They are the path of accumulation, the path of joining, the path of seeing, the path of meditation, and the path of culmination. The five that bring one from the state of an ordinary being to the state of a noble one are the five paths. Connecting them with the thirty-seven factors conducive to awakening: first, having generated the mind of awakening, to meditate on the four applications of mindfulness, the four correct abandonments and the four bases of miraculous power, and to accumulate the collection of merit, is the path of accumulation. To meditate on the five faculties and the five powers and to abide on the ground of practice through aspiration is the path of joining. To meditate on the seven limbs of awakening and attain the first ground is the path of seeing. To meditate on the noble eightfold path and abide from the second ground up to the tenth ground is the path of meditation. The Buddha's abiding on Universal Light, and the śrāvakas' attainment of arhatship, are the path of culmination.

What are the results of the ten non-virtues? There are the maturation, the result concordant with the cause, and the dominant result of killing. There are the maturation, the result concordant with the cause, and the dominant result of taking what is not given. There are the maturation, the result concordant with the cause, and the dominant result of sexual misconduct. There are the maturation, the result concordant with the cause, and the dominant result of lying. There are the maturation, the result concordant with the cause, and the dominant result of slander. There are the maturation, the result concordant with the cause, and the dominant result of harsh speech. There are the maturation, the result concordant with the cause, and the dominant result of idle talk. There are the maturation, the result concordant with the cause, and the dominant result of covetousness. There are the maturation, the result concordant with the cause, and the dominant result of malice. There are the maturation, the result concordant with the cause, and the dominant result of wrong view.

As for what is called the result of maturation, it applies equally to all ten, from killing up to wrong view: all lead to birth in the three lower realms. Then, as a result of killing, to have a short life and many illnesses, and to want to kill, is the result concordant with the cause. To meet with a land where the food is unpalatable and of little strength is the dominant result. As a result of taking what is not given, to become poor and to want to steal is the result concordant with the cause. To meet with a land where food and crops are carried off by frost, hail and the like, and grow poor in color, is the dominant result.

As a result of sexual misconduct, to be prone to wanton adultery, and to have one's wife shared with a paramour, is the result concordant with the cause. To be born in a land full of dust and sand is the dominant result. As a result of lying, to be slandered often, and oneself to delight in lies and slander, is the result concordant with the cause. To meet with a land where the food that grows is foul-smelling is the dominant result. As a result of slander, to find no friends, and to be always at odds with and divided from those one loves, is the result concordant with the cause. To be born uncomfortably in a land of ravines and precipices is the dominant result. As a result of harsh speech, always to hear unpleasant words and talk that makes the mind unhappy is the result concordant with the cause. To be born in a land where the ground is hot, where grain and crops cannot grow, and where food is scarce, is the dominant result. As a result of idle talk, that whatever words one speaks carry no weight, and that others do not listen whatever one says, is the result concordant with the cause. To be born in a land where the seasons of summer and winter are reversed, where rain falls in winter, and in summer the heat of the sun is great and no rain falls, is the dominant result. As a result of great covetousness, to have great desire for and attachment to whatever one sees is the result concordant with the cause. To be born in a land where all grain and crops are thin and small is the dominant result. As a result of malice, to be by nature fierce and wrathful, with great aversion, is the result concordant with the cause. To be born in a land where grain and crops taste pungent and purge the bowels is the dominant result. As a result of wrong view, to have great delusion, lacking understanding and clear mindfulness, is the result concordant with the cause. To be born in a land where, though seeds are sown, crops cannot sprout, and where, even if a few sprout, they bear no fruit and do not ripen, is the dominant result.

If one asks why there come to be three results, it is said: because one caused suffering, because one killed, and because one robbed of luster. In killing, first of all to make the victim sick and in pain is called causing suffering; by that cause, to be born in places of various harms, such as the hells, is the result of maturation. Then comes the killing itself: because one shortened a life, even if one is finally born as a human, to have a short life and many illnesses is the result concordant with the cause. At the moment of killing, because one deprived body and mind of their splendor and vigor, this is called robbing of luster; through it, that the grain and fruit of one's food are poor and unpalatable, and that one is born in a poor land, is the dominant result. Apply this in the same way to all ten non-virtues.

As for the names of the twenty peaks of the view of the transitory collection: "The self is form, like an owner; the self is feeling; the self is perception; the self is formations; the self is consciousness," and so on. The tīrthikas, with their differing views, hold that any one of the five aggregates is the permanent self, and that the remaining four are the retinue of that self; thus they hold to a self and to what is mine. Viewing each of the five aggregates in four ways is what is called the names of the twenty peaks of the view of the transitory collection.

Here, "Form is the self, like an owner" means this: just as an owner of wealth has the power to use his own wealth as he pleases, so the aggregate of form is the permanent self, while the remaining four aggregates are that self's retinue, abiding like a lord of wealth and the deities of his wealth; so they say. Likewise, shift the names and apply this in the same way to the ones that follow.

"The self possesses form, the self possesses feeling, the self possesses perception, the self possesses formations, the self possesses consciousness" means this: just as one says, "Devadatta possesses ornaments," the permanent self exists separately, the aggregate of form abides like its ornament, and the remaining four aggregates abide like that self's retinue. For the remaining four aggregates, too, shift the names and apply it as above.

"Form is mine, feeling is mine, perception is mine, formations are mine, consciousness is mine" means this: just as one says, "The blanket belongs to Devadatta," the self exists separately and holds sway over the aggregate of form as its belonging. Apply it in the same way to the others.

"The self abides in form, the self abides in feeling, the self abides in perception, the self abides in formations, the self abides in consciousness" means this: just as fruit sits on top of a bowl, the permanent self abides in the aggregate of form; so they say. Apply this in the same way to the others that follow.

What are the eighteen dreams that portend what will come in later times? An elephant's tail catching in a window; a well following a thirsty man; flour and pearls being exchanged as equals; an elephant calf being driven from its golden abode; the flowers and fruit of a park being enjoyed by someone other than its owner; those unfit to fight splitting into factions and feuding and quarreling; a single bolt of cloth being divided up and sorted into good and bad; a flower urging, "Scratch me!"; seeing the middle of three jars empty; sandalwood being sold as charcoal; an old man dwelling naked in public; a horse eating with both its mouth and its anus; a small dog with a fiercer bark than a big dog; a cow, as soon as it has calved, wanting to suck from its calf; one who comes of age in the morning growing old and decrepit by evening; a lion's corpse being eaten by the worms of that very corpse; many monkeys gathering and appointing one monkey as king; and seeing one monkey smear filth on another.

These appeared as portents, in the dreams of the king called Kṛkī, of what would later come about in the conduct of humans and in the ways of the Dharma. The elephant's tail catching in the window that he dreamt is an omen that those who go forth, having come out of the household, a state hard to come out of, and having been able to give up and abandon many possessions, will, once in the state of going forth, remain attached to mere begging bowls and robes, or to their lodgings. It is like an elephant entering through a window: the huge body, which one would not expect to fit, fits through, and then the slender tail does not fit and gets stuck.

The well following the thirsty man is a sign that, although it is fitting for those who have gone forth to come to the master in order to hear the Dharma, they will not be willing to come; and when the master comes to the listeners to teach the Dharma, the listeners will be lazy and unwilling to listen. It is like this: although it is fitting for whoever is thirsty to go to a well and drink its water, the thirsty one runs away, and the water of the well follows along behind him.

Flour and pearls being exchanged as equals is a sign of teaching the Dharma in order to obtain flour. The elephant calf being driven from its golden abode is a sign that those who keep moral discipline will be driven from their places by those of corrupt discipline. The flowers and fruit of the park being enjoyed by someone other than its owner is a sign that householders will enjoy the wealth of the Saṅgha. Those unfit to fight splitting into factions and feuding and quarreling is a sign that the teaching of the Tathāgata Śākyamuni will decline through strife.

A single bolt of cloth being divided up and sorted into good and bad: the Dharma and Vinaya of the Buddha are of one, white side, but later, because of differing views, people will take up differing scriptural systems and split into many views. It is like one bolt of cloth that has no good or bad in it, yet is seen as good and bad.

The flower urging, "Pick me!": although it is fitting for men to court women, they will not court them; instead women will court men and press them on. It is like this: although flowers are there to be picked, the flower presses, saying, "Pick me!"

Seeing the middle of three jars empty: there is no one who does not grow haughty, and when someone becomes poor, even relatives grow weary of him, abandon him, and make friends with others. It is like setting up three pots, boiling meat and fat in the two at either end and boiling water in the middle one: the ladle stirs among the meat and fat, but the ladle does not stir in the pot where water is boiling.

Sandalwood being sold as charcoal: teachers of the Dharma will teach the Dharma only for the profit of gain and honor from some audiences, and will not, by the force of compassion, teach it to all who want the Dharma, nor seek the precious merit. It is like this: although sandalwood ought to be sold at a great price, one does not sell it dear, but trades it as charcoal.

The old man who is bald on the crown is an omen that, at the time when the sublime Dharma declines, it will first decline in the central land, then spread to the borderlands and decline there. It is like an old man's hair thinning first at the crown of the head.

The horse eating with both its mouth and its anus: chieftains, ministers and the like will prey upon both those above and those below them, superiors and inferiors alike. It is like a horse eating grass with both its mouth and its anus.

The small dog with the fiercer bark: ministers will be more aggressive in speech than the king, and women more aggressive in speech than men. It is a sign that power will pass into the hands of ministers and women. It is like a big dog that obeys while the small one barks fiercely.

The cow, as soon as it has calved, wanting to suck from its calf: this is a sign that parents, as soon as they have set up a household for their children and grandchildren and given them a share of the wealth, will expect to be repaid, saying, "We gave our possessions to you; now you must support us." It is like a cow wanting to suck from its calf.

One who comes of age in the morning growing decrepit by evening: whatever work people undertake, at first they set about it on a grand scale, as if to do something marvelous, and then at once they grow weary of it and give it up. This is the sign. It is like someone who reaches his prime in the morning and, without even a few days passing, has grown old by that very evening.

The lion's corpse being eaten by the worms of that very corpse: this is a sign that when this sublime Dharma is destroyed, other outsiders will not be able to refute and demolish it, but the monks themselves will destroy it. It is like a lion's corpse, which no birds, dogs or other creatures dare to eat, yet worms arising from the lion's own body eat it.

Many monkeys gathering and appointing one monkey as king is a sign that many monks of corrupt discipline will gather and appoint a wicked one as their leader, saying, "Let us appoint one of our own faction, who agrees with us, as chief." One monkey smearing filth on another is a sign that monks will bring accusations and slander against one another and deceitfully impute faults to each other.

As for the names of the three miracles, they are the miracle of magical power, the miracle of revealing, and the miracle of instruction. Displaying what is impossible for ordinary people, as actually set out below, is called magical power. Thus, by way of the body, flying in the sky, blazing like fire and so on, transforming into whatever one wishes and displaying it, is called the miracle of magical power. By way of knowing the minds of others, teaching whatever faults and good qualities there are in keeping with their minds is the miracle of revealing; it is like personal advice. Having examined which share of the afflictions is greatest in a person's mind-stream, then teaching the appropriate antidote, meditation on the unattractive if attachment is great, loving-kindness if aversion is great, dependent origination if delusion is great, and so on, is the miracle of instruction.

What are the twelve qualities of purification? They are wearing robes from the rubbish heap, keeping only three robes, wearing felt, keeping a single seat, living on alms, not accepting food afterwards, dwelling in the forest, dwelling at the foot of a tree, dwelling without a roof, dwelling in a charnel ground, sitting upright, and accepting whatever place is given. Attachment to livelihood, to the requisites of livelihood, and to dwelling places is a fault, and non-attachment to them is called the qualities of purification. Of these, the first four show non-attachment to robes, the middle two show non-attachment to livelihood, and the last six show non-attachment to dwelling places.

Here, not being attached to new and fine robes, one gathers from the rubbish heap scraps and patches of old cloth that have been thrown away, sews them together, and wears them; therefore one is a wearer of robes from the rubbish heap. And even when seeking them from the rubbish heap, not seeking more than three robes is keeping only three robes. If one cannot find patches and scraps, one gathers wool and keeps no more than three robes of felt; this is wearing felt. Having sought out one single seat of one's own, one seeks no other seat until dawn, and sits on that one without sitting on anyone else's seat; this is keeping a single seat.

Not being attached to the fine food that faithful patrons such as kings and ministers prepare, but living content with whatever alms one obtains, is being an alms-gatherer. Taking at a single sitting just enough food to be full, and eating it without discriminating between flavors and without wanting or accepting more afterwards, is not taking food afterwards. Dwelling at least one krośa away from places of bustle such as enclosures and towns is being a wilderness dweller. If that wilderness dwelling is in a place such as a forest, one is a dweller at the foot of a tree. Dwelling in that wilderness without pitching anything like a tent or a house is being without a roof. If that wilderness dwelling is in a place where human corpses are cast away, one is a charnel-ground dweller. Not laying out a comfortable bed in that wilderness and lying down on the ground on one's side is being one who remains seated. Staying wherever one happens to settle in that wilderness, without choosing comfort, is being content with whatever place one has.

"What are the five eyes of the Tathāgata? The flesh eye, the divine eye, the eye of wisdom, the Dharma eye, and the buddha eye." These arise from the ripening of virtuous karma, and since the Buddha alone possesses them, they are called the five eyes of the Tathāgata. Seeing all forms up to the extent of one three-thousandfold world system is the flesh eye. Seeing all the forms of the worlds that reach to the very limits of the element of space is the divine eye. Fully realizing the selflessness of phenomena is the eye of wisdom. Fully realizing the selflessness of persons is the Dharma eye. Fully realizing, in the domain of what is to be known, every knowable thing without exception is the buddha eye.

"In the twenty-one intentions of a bodhisattva" means being free of deceit and of harm to others. Of these, as for "the intention not to deceive any teachers or masters": one who confers the precepts and teaches the Dharma is a teacher; one who is senior in discipline and knowledge is a master. Not employing bad means such as lies and deceit toward any such person, even for the sake of one's life, is not deceiving anyone; and since there is no one at all toward whom one would act thus, it is the superior intention.

As for "the intention to be gentle toward bodhisattvas who share the Dharma and pleasant to associate with": all who have generated the mind set on awakening and have taken up moral discipline are bodhisattvas who share the Dharma. Having no aversion within and a serene mind is being gentle. Not beating or striking with the body and not speaking harsh words with the voice is the intention to be pleasant to associate with.

As for "the intention to master one's own mind in overcoming the afflictions, the secondary afflictions, and the deeds of Māra": attachment, anger, pride, ignorance, view, and doubt, these six, are the afflictions. Wrath, resentment, concealment, spite, jealousy, miserliness, deceit, dissimulation, self-infatuation, harmfulness, shamelessness, lack of embarrassment, lethargy and excitement, lack of faith, laziness, heedlessness, forgetfulness, distraction, non-vigilance, and sleep and investigation and analysis, these twenty, are the secondary afflictions. Attachment to the five qualities of sense pleasure, and delight in the one-sided peace of the śrāvakas, are called deeds of Māra, because, being directed exclusively toward saṃsāra or toward nirvāṇa, they obstruct the non-abiding nirvāṇa, the Great Vehicle. Not falling under their power, but bringing them under the power of one's own mind, is called the intention to master one's own mind in overcoming them.

As for "the intention not to view any formations as faults": the phenomena of the three realms are called formations. Fully realizing all of them in their intrinsic nature is itself nirvāṇa; and since one does not, like the śrāvakas, abandon the faults of saṃsāra and seek a nirvāṇa somewhere else, it is called the intention not to view formations as faults.

"The intention not to view nirvāṇa as a good quality" means not thinking, as the śrāvakas do, "That nirvāṇa of one-sided peace is bliss; I shall enter it."

"The intention always to cultivate the virtuous factors aligned with awakening": whatever serves as an aid and cause for attaining unsurpassed awakening is called the virtuous factors of awakening. In actual terms these are the applications of mindfulness and the rest, the ten grounds, and the ten perfections. Thinking, "I shall make myself familiar with these continuously, without interruption, at every single instant," is called the intention always to cultivate.

"The intention of solitude conducive to cultivating these very things" means this: having put that virtue into practice, to think of going far away from bustle to a place such as a wilderness is the intention of solitude conducive to meditation.

As for "the intention not to look to worldly material gain, profit, or honor": food, clothing, and the like, the alms bowl and the Dharma robes, are worldly material gain and profit. Prostrations and acts of service are honor. When one teaches the Dharma to sentient beings, one teaches it with the intention of benefiting them, and with no intention of acquiring material things; therefore it is called the intention not to look to honor.

As for "the intention to abandon the Lesser Vehicle and realize the Great Vehicle": not engaging in the conduct of the śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas, who have set out for their own sake alone, is abandoning the Lesser Vehicle. Accomplishing the grounds and the perfections so that both oneself and others may attain unsurpassed awakening is the intention to realize the Great Vehicle.

As for "the intention to accomplish every aim of all sentient beings": those who are born through the four kinds of birth and circle among the six classes of beings are all sentient beings. Thinking, "I shall establish them in virtue in this life, and in later lives bring them to attain the result of gods and humans, and liberation," is called the intention to accomplish every aim.

"The supreme intention" means thinking, "The Three Jewels are the very finest in good qualities, supreme among all gods and humans."

"The intention of moral discipline" means thinking, "Having taken the bodhisattva vow, I shall practice without contradicting it."

"The intention of the perfections" means thinking, "I shall accomplish the ten perfections, beginning with generosity."

"The intention of the meaning of reality" means thinking, "I shall realize the characteristic of emptiness, which is the meaning of the selflessness of phenomena and of persons."

"The intention of power" means thinking, "I shall attain the six superknowledges, beginning with the divine eye."

"The beneficial intention" means thinking, "I shall make sentient beings happy in this life." "The intention of well-being" means thinking, "I shall make sentient beings happy in their later lives."

"The intention of definitive liberation" means that, even when one benefits sentient beings, one does not hope for a return in this life, and has no hope for a ripening in later lives.

"The steadfast intention" means that, from the time one first generates the mind set on awakening onward, one's mind does not change or turn back from it.

"The undeceiving intention" means that, in the ways of benefiting oneself and others, one's knowledge is decisive, so that one is not mistaken about the means and does not go astray on the path.

"The impure intention" means thinking, "I shall accomplish the ground of conduct through aspiration." "The pure intention" means thinking, "I shall accomplish the grounds from the first ground up to the seventh." "The thoroughly pure intention" means thinking, "I shall accomplish the grounds from the eighth ground up to the tenth."

"The restrained intention": one who abides on the eighth ground is spontaneously accomplished, without exertion or effort arising, and is therefore called restrained; it means thinking, "I shall attain that."

"The innate intention": at the time of the eighth ground, wisdom too has become nonconceptual in nature, and it is therefore called innate; it means thinking, "I shall attain that."

"What are the four māras? The māra of the aggregates, the māra of the afflictions, the māra who is a son of the gods, and the māra of the Lord of Death." They are the four māras because they obstruct the roots of virtue, which are the cause of unsurpassed awakening and are like life itself. Once these aggregates produced by karma and afflictions are established, one has no independence, and through conditions such as sickness, aging, and decline, they obstruct virtue; therefore this is the māra of the aggregates. Through the force of the predominance of attachment, aversion, and the like, virtue is obstructed; therefore this is the māra of the afflictions. The sons of the gods of the desire realm, through the force of jealousy, obstruct virtue; this is the māra who is a son of the gods. When the formations of life disintegrate and one is parted from life, the continuity of doing virtue is cut off; this is called the māra of the Lord of Death.

Among the names of the successive levels of the abodes of the gods of the desire realm, the lofty heaven is that of those who dwell on the surface of the earth, at the foot of trees, at the foot of rocks, and so on. The heaven of the intermediate space is that of the sun, moon, stars, and the like, which move through the sky. The heaven of the class of the Four Great Kings is this: on the four faces of Mount Meru, in the north is Kubera, in the east Dhṛtarāṣṭra, in the south Virūḍhaka, and in the west Virūpākṣa; those who belong to their retinues are the class. The heaven of the Thirty-Three is on the summit of Mount Meru: there are god-kings, eight in each direction, making thirty-two, and over them all there is one great king, so it refers to there being thirty-three. As for the heaven Free from Strife (Yāma): the gods of the Thirty-Three fight with the asuras, but these gods are free from fighting with the asuras and do not fight, and so it is called Free from Strife. The heaven of Tuṣita, "Joyful," is so called because, beyond surpassing those below in great enjoyments and pleasant experience, it is endowed with the enjoyment of Maitreya teaching the Dharma. The heaven Delighting in Emanation (Nirmāṇarati) is so called because its gods themselves emanate and enjoy whatever sense enjoyments they please. The heaven Controlling Others' Emanations (Paranirmitavaśavartin) is that in which they enjoy whatever sense enjoyments they please, emanated by others.

Of the four meditative concentrations, the names of the first concentration are these; they dwell from the form realm upward: the heaven of Brahmā's Class (Brahmakāyika), the heaven of Those Who Recite Before Brahmā (Brahmapurohita), and the heaven of Brahmā's Retinue. Among these, because they are born into the class of, and as the servants and retinue of, the higher Mahābrahmā, it is called the heaven of Brahmā's Class. Because they serve Mahābrahmā morning and evening, are born close to him like a retinue, and speak and recite before him, they are also called Brahmā's Close Helpers, and also Those Who Recite Before Brahmā. Because they surpass the two lower Brahmā heavens in fine bodies, long life, roots of virtue, and power, they are called Brahmā's Retinue, and they are also called Mahābrahmā.

The names of the second concentration are the heaven of Limited Light (Parīttābha), Immeasurable Light (Apramāṇābha), and Clear Light (Ābhāsvara). The gods born in this abode have lesser bodily splendor and radiance than the two above, and so it is the heaven of Limited Light. The gods born in this abode cannot be measured as having just so much bodily light, and so it is called Immeasurable Light. The gods born in this abode make even the abodes of other gods shine with clear light, and so it is called Clear Light.

The names of the third concentration are Limited Virtue (Parīttaśubha), Immeasurable Virtue (Apramāṇaśubha), and Śubhakṛtsna, Vast Virtue. The bliss of concentration of the gods born in this abode is called virtue; because their bliss is less than that of the two above, it is called Limited Virtue. Because the bliss of concentration of those born in this abode cannot be measured as just so much, it is called Immeasurable Virtue. Because the gods born in this abode have perfected without remainder the bliss of worldly concentration, it is called Vast Virtue; for from there upward, those of the fourth concentration have abandoned bliss and abide in equanimity.

Of the eight abodes of the fourth concentration, the names of the three of ordinary beings are Cloudless (Anabhraka), Increasing Merit (Puṇyaprasava), and Great Fruit (Bṛhatphala). The gods born in this abode, just as the sky is clear when it is not blocked by clouds, are not blocked or obscured by the celestial palaces of other gods, for those gods arise and perish together with their own celestial palaces; therefore it is called Cloudless. Since this abode is the place where beings attain the supreme of merit, it is called Increasing Merit. The gods born in this abode are the result of having accomplished roots of virtue greater than the others below, and since their result too is greater than that of the others below, it is Great Fruit. The names of the class of the five Pure Abodes are Not Great (Avṛha), Without Torment (Atapa), Beautiful Appearance (Sudṛśa), Clear Sight (Sudarśana), and Akaniṣṭha.

Because noble ones such as Vairocana dwell there as the principal inhabitants, these are called the Pure Abodes. Among those born in the class of the Pure Abodes, because they have less merit than the others above, it is called Not Great. Those born in this abode have attained a special concentration, so that afflictions are unable to torment them; therefore it is Without Torment. Those born in this abode, however they meditate in concentration, are not obscured or blocked by afflictions; therefore it is Beautiful Appearance. The gods born in this abode, when other gods look at them, are seen as distinguished by great bliss and peace; therefore it is called Clear Sight. The gods born in this abode have reached the very limit of the form realm, and above it there are no other embodied beings; because it is above all, it is called Akaniṣṭha.

"What are the eight worldly concerns? Gain and loss, praise and lack of praise, fame and lack of fame, pleasure and displeasure." Here, the minds of the noble ones who have transcended the world are like Mount Meru, unmoved and undisturbed by any condition whatever. But the minds of childish beings, who are like small children, are tossed about by the wind of desire like a wisp of grass, and even through conditions of trifling benefit or harm they become pleased or displeased; these variations are called the worldly concerns. Of these, being pleased at obtaining some small material thing, such as an alms bowl or Dharma robes, is gain. Not obtaining such material things and being displeased is loss. Being pleased when one's good qualities are spoken of to one's face is praise. Being disturbed in mind when one's faults are spoken of to one's face is lack of praise. Being pleased on hearing that one's good qualities are spoken of behind one's back is fame. Being displeased on hearing that one's faults are spoken of behind one's back is lack of fame. Experiencing ease of body and mind and being pleased is pleasure. Experiencing harm to body and mind and being displeased is displeasure. People too should train as far as they can to abide in equanimity, unstained by these eight concerns.

The names of the four kinds of establishing are establishing in the vehicles, establishing in the truths, establishing in reasoning, and establishing in the Dharma. Of these, the ten virtues, the concentrations, and the meditative absorptions are the vehicle of gods and humans; the four truths, the twelve links of dependent origination, and the grounds and perfections are the noble vehicle; teaching these vehicles is establishing in the vehicles. That all phenomena are without arising and ceasing is the one truth. The conventional and the ultimate are the two truths. Suffering, origin, cessation, and the path are the four truths. Teaching these is establishing in the truths. Teaching the reasoning of dependence, the reasoning of efficacy, the reasoning that establishes tenability, and the reasoning of the nature of reality is establishing in reasoning. Setting forth the twelve branches of scripture in the world, gathering them into the three baskets, and teaching them is called establishing in the Dharma.

The names of the seven riches are the riches of faith, the riches of moral discipline, the riches of diligence, the riches of conscience, the riches of learning, the riches of giving, and the riches of understanding. Of these, being convinced that karma and its results, the truths, and the Jewels exist and possess good qualities is the cause of moral discipline; this is the riches of faith. Abandoning wrongdoing and non-virtue, such as taking life, is the cause of liberation and of birth in the higher realms; this is the riches of moral discipline. Listening to, reflecting on, and meditating on the sublime Dharma without interruption is the cause of the increase of the accumulations of merit and wisdom; this is the riches of diligence. Being careful and wary about wrongdoing and non-virtuous things is the cause of faults not arising; this is the riches of conscience. Not being content with the sublime Dharma one has, but listening much, is the cause of the increase of the wisdom of reflection and of meditation; this is the riches of learning. Giving to others whatever outer and inner things one possesses is the cause of acquiring great wealth; this is the riches of giving. Realizing the meaning of the Dharma through the wisdom of reflection and of meditation is the cause of attaining nirvāṇa; this is the riches of understanding. If one asks why they are called riches, it is because there is no need whatever that they do not fulfill.

Among the nine abodes of sentient beings, those diverse in body and diverse in perception are those of the first concentration, setting aside those who have just attained the first concentration, together with all the sentient beings of the desire realm from the gods downward: their bodies are unlike, and their perceptions are also different from one another, and so they are called diverse in body and diverse in perception. Those diverse in body but one in perception are these: the Brahmās born earlier in the first concentration have large bodies of fine color and think, "I created these later Brahmās," while the Brahmās who have just attained the first concentration have bodies and color inferior to theirs and think, "We were created by Mahābrahmā"; therefore they are called diverse in body but one in perception. Those one in body but diverse in perception are the gods dwelling from the second concentration up to the third: their bodies are alike, of a single size, but as for perception, some abide in bliss and some abide in equanimity, and so they are called one in body but diverse in perception. Those one in body and also one in perception are those dwelling in the fourth concentration, because their bodies are of a single size and their perceptions are alike in being accompanied by the feeling of equanimity.

The sphere of infinite space is thinking, "All phenomena amount to no more than space, and space is infinite." The sphere of infinite consciousness is thinking, "All phenomena are infinite like space, and the consciousness that takes them as its object is also infinite." The sphere of nothingness is this: all phenomena are infinite like space, and the consciousness that takes them as its object is also infinite; examining, "Apart from these, what other phenomenon is there?" one sees nothing at all to exist, and thinks, "There is nothing whatever." The sentient beings of neither perception nor non-perception are those who think, "Even this perception, the thought that all phenomena amount to no more than space and that there is nothing whatever, is like a disease or a thorn, and so it is to be abandoned"; they restrain it so that it grows ever subtler, until it has become extremely subtle; and then, thinking, "Yet it will not do for that perception to be entirely absent," they abide in just that extremely subtle portion.

Among the five lineages, the lineage of the Śrāvaka Vehicle is having in one's mind-stream the habitual tendencies of having listened to and meditated on the four truths. The lineage of the Pratyekabuddha Vehicle is having the habitual tendencies of having listened to and meditated on dependent origination.

The lineage of the Tathāgata Vehicle is having the habitual tendencies of having listened to and meditated on the grounds and the perfections. The indeterminate lineage is not fixed in any one direction, because the habitual tendencies of having listened to and meditated on the teachings of all three vehicles, great and small, are present as what leads it on; it enters the side of whichever spiritual friend comes first. Those without lineage have no habitual tendencies of having listened to or meditated on the teachings of any vehicle, great or small; therefore for a long time they have no fortune to meet with virtue, and they wander in the lower realms.

Among the four paths are the difficult path with slow realization, the difficult path with quick realization, the easy path with slow realization, and the easy path with quick realization. Here, the preparatory stage that is not incapable, which leads into the first concentration, and the special concentration are predominant in insight and are therefore difficult; and the four formless absorptions are predominant in calm abiding and are therefore difficult. When a person of dull faculties enters upon these, it is called the difficult path with slow realization. When one of sharp faculties enters upon the preparatory stage that is not incapable, the special concentration, and the four formless absorptions, it is called the difficult path with quick realization. When one of dull faculties enters upon the four concentrations, it is called the easy path with slow realization. The four concentrations of the form realm have equal portions of calm abiding and insight; therefore, when one of sharp faculties enters upon them, it is called the easy path with quick realization.

Among the four things that need no guarding: the conduct of the Tathāgata's bodily activity is completely pure, so there is no impure bodily activity. Whatever bodily activity he performs, going, staying, sitting, or lying down, not even the slightest fault such as taking life arises, and therefore he has no fear of being criticized or reproached by others. The conduct of the Tathāgata's verbal activity is completely pure, so there is no impure verbal activity. Because he has abandoned the four faults of speech, such as lying, when he teaches the sublime Dharma and so forth he has not the slightest fear that others will criticize any fault in his words. The conduct of the Tathāgata's mental activity is completely pure, so there is no impure mental activity. Because he has abandoned the three faults of mind and is intent on the sublime Dharma and the welfare of sentient beings, he has no error that he would fear others might detect. The Tathāgata's livelihood is completely pure, so there is no impure livelihood. Because he has abandoned living by hypocrisy and deceit and lives in accordance with the Dharma through wisdom, he has no fault that he would fear others might detect and that would need to be concealed.

Among the eighteen unshared qualities of a bodhisattva are these: "those whose generosity is untaught, those whose moral discipline is untaught, those whose patience is untaught, those whose diligence is untaught, those whose concentration is untaught, and those whose wisdom is untaught." Ordinary beings enter upon the perfections, such as generosity, when a spiritual friend has taught them and urged them on concerning the nature, the qualities, and the results of the six perfections. But bodhisattvas, because they have the habitual tendencies and the momentum of familiarity from having practiced the perfections again and again in the past, enter upon generosity and the rest naturally, even without being taught or urged on with reasons. This is to be applied to all of them.

Here, the nature of generosity is giving outer and inner things to others. Its qualities are merit and the like. Its results are attaining the happy destinies and the higher realms, and buddhahood. The nature of moral discipline is not engaging in wrongdoing and non-virtue. Its quality is the increase of merit. Its results are passing into nirvāṇa and birth in the happy destinies. The nature of patience is that the mind does not become angry even when others do one harm. Its qualities and results are like those above; in particular, one has a pleasing and beautiful appearance, and a large retinue gathers. The nature of diligence is never interrupting the continuity of abandoning non-virtue and accomplishing virtue. Its qualities are the two accumulations of merit and wisdom, and the like. Its results are bringing all one's aspirations to completion and attaining buddhahood. The nature of concentration is the mind's one-pointedness on its object. Its qualities are the accumulations of wisdom and primordial wisdom, and the like. Its results are a happy mind and a body free of illness in all one's lives, and finally full awakening. The nature of wisdom is expertise in the specific and general characteristics of phenomena. Its qualities are the accumulation of primordial wisdom and the like. Its results are expertise in all the fields of knowledge in all one's lives, and full awakening.

As for "gathering sentient beings through the means of attraction": a miserly person is gathered through giving and established in virtue. An angry person is gathered through loving-kindness and established in virtue. A proud person is gathered through consistency of aims, without going against his mind. The lazy are gathered by one's acting as their companion in work and through conduct that serves the aim, and are then brought into virtue.

"Knowing the rite of complete dedication" means that, by making even the smallest and most trifling virtue something held in common with all sentient beings and dedicating it to great, unsurpassed awakening, the accumulation of merit increases immeasurably.

As for "through skill in means, in accordance with the conduct of all sentient beings, showing deliverance by the supreme vehicle": being endowed with compassion and wisdom is called skill in means. The differences of lesser vehicles, of times, of habitual tendencies, and of fortune are called the conduct of sentient beings. Examining with wisdom whether their fortune is great or small, first establishing them in the three vehicles in a way that suits their minds, and in the end establishing them in unsurpassed awakening, is called showing deliverance by the supreme vehicle.

"Not falling away from the Great Vehicle": because one is endowed with compassion, one does not practice the Śrāvaka Vehicle and does not abandon saṃsāra; and because one is endowed with wisdom, even while dwelling in saṃsāra one is not stained by the faults of saṃsāra. This non-abiding nirvāṇa is called not falling away from the Great Vehicle.

"Showing the doors of saṃsāra and nirvāṇa": showing the passing from the palace of Tuṣita, the entry into the womb, the birth, the youthful sport, and the dwelling in the household is showing the door of saṃsāra. Showing the going forth from the household, the practice of austerities, the subduing of Māra, the full awakening, and the passing away is showing the door of nirvāṇa.

As for being skilled in the method of pairs and of alternations: explaining only the conventional truth, or else explaining only the ultimate truth, is being skilled in the method of alternations. Explaining both the ultimate truth and the conventional truth intertwined is being skilled in the method of pairs. Alternatively, the phrase also applies to the capacity for such miraculous powers as emitting both fire and water from the body, or emitting either one of them.

As for "being preceded by wisdom, without deliberate striving and without blame, intentionally entering upon all lives": all the conduct of body, speech, and mind of bodhisattvas is preceded by wisdom. Because it is held by wisdom, they do not commit wrongdoing, and their engagement in virtue arises without needing to be sought; this is called being without deliberate striving. Being thus free of blameworthy faults, and yet taking birth in saṃsāra for the sake of sentient beings, is called intentionally entering upon all lives.

"Being endowed with the ten virtuous courses of action of body, speech, and mind" does not mean, as with other people, doing virtue on some occasions, holding that to be enough, and then giving it up. Never interrupting the continuity of virtue of body, speech, and mind until one becomes a buddha is called being endowed with the ten virtuous courses of action.

As for "taking on a body that endures the entire mass of suffering, and so not abandoning the entire realm of sentient beings": of the two patiences, the patience of accepting suffering and the patience of disregarding harm, not giving up working for the welfare of sentient beings even when the harms of cold and heat, hunger and thirst, arise is the patience of accepting suffering. Not being disturbed, and not giving up working for the welfare of sentient beings, even when sentient beings do one harm such as beating one, is the patience of disregarding harm. Because, living in a body endowed with these two patiences, one never casts aside the welfare of sentient beings, even when harm occurs at any time, it is called taking on a body that endures the entire mass of suffering, and so not abandoning the entire realm of sentient beings.

As for "showing delight to all beings": because all the bodily and verbal conduct of bodhisattvas arises from the accumulations of merit and wisdom, whether sentient beings see their bodies or hear the words they speak, faith and joy arise in them through whatever door, and nothing displeasing to their minds arises; this is called showing delight to beings.

As for "not losing the mind of omniscience, which is firm like a precious wish-fulfilling tree of many virtues, even amid all the harshness of childish beings and of śrāvakas": that people, lacking wisdom, are bound by afflictions such as attachment and constantly tormented by the sufferings of birth, aging, sickness, death, and the rest, is the harshness of childish beings. That one who, although possessing the wisdom that has abandoned the afflictions, lacks great compassion, and so casts aside the welfare of all sentient beings and passes into nirvāṇa for oneself alone, is the harshness of the śrāvakas. Although dwelling amid these two, being endowed with skillful means and wisdom one never interrupts the welfare of both oneself and others, which is the cause of the increase of many virtues, and does not give up the awakening mind in the slightest. This is what is firm like a precious wish-fulfilling tree of many virtues, and it is called not losing the mind of omniscience.

As for "not turning back from seeking and teaching the Buddha's Dharma, in order to attain the empowerment that bestows the means of all phenomena": because all phenomena whatsoever, worldly and transcendent, outer and inner, conditioned and unconditioned, have become directly perceived, at the time of the tenth ground the tathāgatas of the ten directions bestow empowerment as regent of the King of Dharma. Therefore, at the time of the tenth ground, it is called the empowerment that bestows the means of all phenomena. In order to attain such a state, oneself seeking the Buddha's Dharma by way of listening to it and so forth, and never interrupting the continuity of teaching and explaining it to others, is called not turning back from seeking and teaching the Buddha's Dharma.

As for "the nine things preceded by supreme joy": seeing liberation and the wisdom of liberation is called supreme joy. What precedes it is something like the cause of attaining it, and refers to easily guarding the three higher trainings. These are nine.

As for "from gladness, joy arises": easily guarding the three bodily virtues, the mind not being disturbed by attachment and aversion, is called gladness; and being able, with that gladness as the cause, easily to guard the three mental virtues is called the arising of joy.

"When the mind is joyful, the body becomes thoroughly pliant": the mind is joyful, and if one is able easily to guard the three mental virtues, from that cause the body becomes serviceable and is free of heaviness and discomfort; therefore it is said, "When the mind is joyful, the body becomes thoroughly pliant." "When the body is thoroughly pliant, one experiences bliss." These four, up to this point, teach abiding in higher moral discipline.

"The mind that has become blissful is placed in equipoise" means that, when the mind is serviceable and has become blissful, from that cause the meditative absorption of one-pointed mind arises in the meditator's mind-stream. This teaches the training in higher mind.

As for "when the mind is placed in equipoise, one fully knows as it truly is, and sees as it truly is": for example, just as when a river or the like is not muddied and not stirred, the reflection of a face and so on appears clearly in it, so when the mind is placed in equipoise and is not stirred by the winds of conceptual thought, insight and calm abiding are joined as a pair. Knowing through insight the characteristics of phenomena such as the four truths, and seeing them unerringly through calm abiding, is called knowing as it truly is and seeing as it truly is.

"When one sees as it truly is, one becomes weary" means that, by seeing and knowing without error the characteristics of the four truths and the rest, one sees the faults of saṃsāra, which belong to the truth of suffering, and becomes disenchanted with saṃsāra; this is becoming weary when one sees as it truly is. These two preceding statements teach the training in higher wisdom.

"When one is weary, one becomes free of attachment" means that when one has thus become weary of the mass of suffering, one abandons the afflictions of the three realms, which belong to the truth of the origin, the cause that produces that suffering; therefore it is said, "When one is weary, one becomes free of attachment."

"When free of attachment, one becomes liberated" means that when one has thus abandoned the afflictions of the three realms that are to be abandoned by seeing and by meditation, nirvāṇa, the truth of cessation, becomes directly manifest; therefore it is said, "When free of attachment, one becomes liberated." These two preceding statements teach the attainment of the aggregate of liberation.

"In those who are liberated there arises the wisdom and vision that thinks, 'I am liberated'" means this. After nirvāṇa has thus been attained, a wisdom of this kind arises: "Suffering, which was to be understood, has already been understood, so now it need not be understood. The origin, which was to be abandoned, has already been abandoned, so it need not be abandoned. Cessation, which was to be made manifest, has already been made manifest, so now it need not be made manifest. The path, which was to be cultivated, has already been cultivated, so now it need not be cultivated." The arising of these four wisdoms is the arising, in one who is liberated, of the wisdom and vision that thinks, "I am liberated," and it is called the attainment of the aggregate of the wisdom and vision of liberation. In general, these statements teach the attainment of the five undefiled aggregates.

The ten totality sense bases (kṛtsnāyatana) are: the blue totality sense base, the yellow totality sense base, the red totality sense base, the white totality sense base, the earth totality sense base, the water totality sense base, the fire totality sense base, the wind totality sense base, the space totality sense base, and the consciousness totality sense base.

"Some perceive the earth totality above, below and across, as nondual and immeasurable. Some perceive the totality of water, fire, wind, blue, yellow, red and white above, below and across, as nondual and immeasurable." This means the following. A yogin who has gained mastery over the mind of meditative concentration, just as they wish, contemplates any one of these ten: the four great elements and the four colors, making eight; space, making nine; and consciousness, making ten. Just as, when all the directions, the intermediate directions, above and below are transformed into a mass of fire, they become nothing but a mass of fire, so too, gaining the freedom for all ten, the colors and the rest, to become just as one transforms them, is called the ten totality sense bases. This should be applied likewise to each one.

"One perceives the earth totality above, below and across, as nondual and immeasurable": when any one of earth, water and the rest is being transformed, there is no particular distinct from it, so it is called nondual. Since there is no distinction such as "the expanse of earth extends this far and up to here," it is called immeasurable. Focusing on just one of them, thinking, "Everything is filled with earth," and so on, is called perceiving. This should be applied likewise to each of the others in turn.

As for the nine meditations on the unattractive: the perception of the livid is of a corpse or the like whose skin has turned bluish on the outside. The perception of the festering is that, when one examines what is within the skin, it is filled with pus, blood, lymph, phlegm and brain matter. The perception of the worm-infested is that flies have gone inside and worms have entered. The perception of the putrefied is that everything inside and outside has rotted. The perception of the bloody is that birds, dogs, foxes and wolves have opened wounds, and the flesh, blood and so on within appear on the outside. The perception of the devoured is that it has been eaten by birds, dogs, foxes, wolves and the like. The perception of the scattered is that the four limbs and the joints of the bones have come apart from one another. The perception of the scorched is that the bones, with shreds of flesh and sinew still clinging to them, have been dried and seared by the sun and the wind. The perception of the skeleton is that the bones have turned white like the color of a conch. These are taught as the antidote to attachment to the body.

The twenty-two faculties are: the eye faculty, the ear faculty, the nose faculty, the tongue faculty, the body faculty, the mind faculty, the male faculty, the female faculty, the suffering faculty, the pleasure faculty, the mental pleasure faculty, the mental displeasure faculty, the equanimity faculty, the faith faculty, the diligence faculty, the mindfulness faculty, the samādhi faculty, the wisdom faculty, the faculty of coming to know what is not yet known, the faculty of knowing, the faculty of one who has fully known, and the life faculty.

These twenty-two are also condensed into five kinds: the support of the mind, conceptualization in relation to it, thorough affliction, the accumulation for purification, and purification. Of these, the six from the eye up to the mind, together with the life faculty, are the support of the mind. The male and female faculties are conceptualization in relation to it, for others conceive of that person as "a man" or "a woman." Pleasure and mental pleasure give rise to attachment, suffering and mental displeasure give rise to aversion, and equanimity gives rise to delusion; therefore they are thorough affliction. The five faculties of faith and the rest act as the cause of nirvāṇa, and so they are the accumulation for purification. The three, the faculty of coming to know what is not yet known and the rest, are purification.

Of these, the eye faculty is like an open flax flower. The ear is like a twisted roll of birch bark. The nose is like a split needle. The tongue is like a moon cut in half. The body is like fingernails clad as armor. The mind faculty is the six consciousnesses that have just passed. The male faculty is like a thumb. The female faculty is like the hollow of a drum. The suffering faculty is the experience of discomfort in the body, together with consciousness and the sense faculty. The pleasure faculty is the experience of ease in the body, together with consciousness and the sense faculty. The mental pleasure faculty is the experience of joy in mental consciousness. The mental displeasure faculty is the distress that arises in mental consciousness. The equanimity faculty is when, in both body and mind, neither any particular pleasure has occurred nor any suffering has arisen.

The faith faculty is believing that karma and its results, the truths and the Jewels exist and possess good qualities, and having a mind clear with confidence in them. The diligence faculty is not interrupting the continuity of the virtuous side, such as listening, reflecting and meditating. The mindfulness faculty is not forgetting, even for an eon, the words and the meaning that one has listened to and heard concerning the characteristics of phenomena. The samādhi faculty is not falling away from the mind's remaining one-pointedly on the object of meditation. The wisdom faculty is knowing without error the specific and general characteristics of phenomena.

The faculty of coming to know what is not yet known is this: when the afflictions to be abandoned by seeing are being abandoned, there are the five faculties from the faith faculty up to the wisdom faculty, and the four, namely the pleasure faculty, mental pleasure, equanimity and the mind faculty; and these nine faculties, having abandoned the afflictions to be abandoned by seeing, come to know fully what was not known before. The faculty of knowing is this: at the time when the afflictions to be abandoned by meditation are being abandoned, those same nine faculties clearly distinguish the discordant side from the side of the antidote. The faculty of one who has fully known is the name given to those same nine faculties at the time when arhatship is attained, when they have abandoned without remainder the afflictions of seeing and of meditation and are endowed with the wisdom of exhaustion and the wisdom of non-arising. The life faculty is what sustains the lifespan and life for as long as one has not died.

The bases of meritorious action are: the basis of meritorious action arising from giving, the basis of meritorious action arising from moral discipline, the basis of meritorious action arising from meditation, and the basis of meritorious action arising from material things. In substance, this teaches the perfect causes: the cause of abundant wealth, the cause of birth in the happy destinies, and the cause that turns one back from both the lower destinies and the happy destinies. Of these, the two, giving and what arises from material things, are the cause of wealth. Moral discipline is the cause of the happy destinies. Meditation is the cause that turns one back from both the lower and the happy destinies.

Here, if one asks by whom the giving is done, it is by the master of the gift. If one asks to whom, it is to destitute beings. If one asks what is given, the giving of whatever is needed from among the various requisites such as food and clothing is called the basis of meritorious action arising from giving.

If one asks, as for moral discipline, by whom, for what purpose and what is guarded: a person endowed with faith, in order not to harm self or others in this life, guards the five, the ten, or the two hundred and fifty precepts and so on, and this is called the basis of meritorious action arising from moral discipline.

If one asks, as for meditation, by whom, for what purpose and what is meditated on: a person who delights in yoga, in order to purify the stains of the afflictions, meditates on the four immeasurables, the three doors of liberation and so on, and this is called the basis of meritorious action arising from meditation.

If one asks, as for what arises from material things, by whom, to whom and what is offered: faithful householders, brahmins and others offer to the Three Jewels various requisites such as temples, dwellings, pleasure groves, food, robes and medicine, and this is called the basis of meritorious action arising from material things.

The four foundations (adhiṣṭhāna) are the foundation of truth, the foundation of relinquishment, the foundation of pacification, and the foundation of wisdom. Having gained the mastery to abide in whatever is the meaning of the Dharma of the noble path, and having power, is called a foundation. Of these, having understood the truth of suffering, which was to be understood, is the foundation of truth. Having abandoned the truth of the origin, which was to be abandoned, is the foundation of relinquishment. Having made manifest the truth of cessation, which was to be made manifest, is the foundation of pacification. Having cultivated the truth of the path, which was to be cultivated, is the foundation of wisdom.

The six causes are the efficient cause, the co-arising cause, the congruent cause, the homogeneous cause, the pervasive cause, and the cause of maturation. This means the following. Within any one of earth, water, fire and wind, for example, all four are fully present; one acting as the cause of another, they depend on one another mutually and arise simultaneously. It is like this: a single person cannot travel through a place of danger, but when many people travel together, they help one another and escape from the danger. This is called the co-arising cause.

When mind and mental factors engage an object and determine it, one alone cannot engage and determine it; rather, many minds and mental factors, one acting as the cause of another, help one another and arise simultaneously. To give an example, in the former case the companions merely keep one another company in danger; but in this case they not only help, but if one cannot walk, they do not let that one stay behind, and they even give their backs as support and lead the person along. Arising in a way like this is called the congruent cause.

When a virtuous mind arises, the mental factors too arise as virtuous; when a nonvirtuous mind occurs, the mental factors too occur as nonvirtuous. The mental factors occurring in a manner that accords with the way the mind arose is called the homogeneous cause.

There are the desire realm, the four meditative concentrations of the form realm, and the four formless absorptions. Since what has arisen earlier in these nine levels later causes afflicted phenomena to arise, it is called the pervasive cause.

Being born in the higher realms through the cause of virtuous karma, being born in the lower realms through the cause of nonvirtuous karma, and being born with a good or a bad body is called the cause of maturation.

Any phenomenon whatsoever that does not obstruct or hinder an arising is designated the efficient cause. For example, if nothing else hinders a sprout from arising, that very thing, because it does not hinder, is designated the efficient cause.

The four conditions are the causal condition, the dominant condition, the immediately preceding condition, and the object condition. Of these, the all-base consciousness acts both as a cause and as a condition, and so it is the causal condition. The five faculties such as the eye act as the dominant factor in producing their respective consciousnesses, and so they are the dominant condition. The mind faculty that has just ceased sets the mental consciousness in motion, and so it is the immediately preceding condition. The six outer objects, such as form and sound, are the object condition of consciousness.

The five results are the result that accords with its cause, the dominant result, the result of personal effort, the result of maturation, and the result of separation. Of these, when one who habitually did virtue in a former life wants to do virtue in this life as well, and one who habitually did evil in a former life wants to do evil deeds in this life as well, that is the result that accords with its cause. Being born in this life in a bad land and region because one committed evil and nonvirtue in former lives, and being born in a good land because one did virtue, that is, being born into the possession of a good or a bad land, is the dominant result. Things like obtaining much barley by farming the fields, obtaining many goods by trading, and obtaining much livestock by herding are the result of personal effort. Becoming rich through giving, becoming poor through theft and robbery, and having a short life and many illnesses through killing and through beating and abuse, and the like, are the result of maturation. Having abandoned the afflictions by cultivating the noble path is the result of separation.

Of the four ways of taking up phenomena, the one that is pleasant at present but ripens later as suffering refers to this: by the force of habituation in a former life, if one is set to hunting wild game, there is no difficulty, and one is able to hunt with ease and delight; but through that cause one is born in the three lower realms in the next life. The one that is painful at present but ripens later as happiness refers to this: because one has no habituation from a former life, one is not capable of the various austerities on the side of virtue and does them only with difficulty; yet although the suffering is great, in the next life one is born in the higher realms and in the state of liberation. The one that is painful at present and also ripens later as suffering refers to this: like one who has no habituation from a former life being set to hunting wild game, one is not equal to it by temperament; one is unable even in the moment and is ill at ease, and in the next life too one is born in the three lower realms. The one that is pleasant at present and also ripens later as happiness refers to this: those who abide in meditative concentration are at ease in body and mind even now, and its result is the attainment of the higher realms and nirvāṇa.

Of the eight sufferings, the suffering of birth is this: when the aggregates that are born in the three realms take shape, they become, as it were, the basis of the three sufferings and the eight; and because they are pervaded by the suffering of suffering, the suffering of change, and the suffering of conditioning factors, it is called the suffering of birth. When the vigor and strength of youth, the color and shape of the face, all decline from their youthfulness, when one is covered with white hair and wrinkles, and diligence, intelligence and the like are gone, that is called the suffering of aging. When the body is stricken with phlegm, lymph, leprosy, sores or fever, and when, thinking of one's household, relatives, friends, wealth and the like, torment and sorrow arise within the mind, that is the suffering of sickness. When karma and lifespan are exhausted and warmth and consciousness abandon the body, one leaves behind beloved relatives and friends and fine wealth, and is led away powerlessly by the henchmen of Yama, lord of death, who carry ropes, clubs and whips: that is the suffering of death. When something one needs and cherishes, whether a living being or not, or one's wealth, is seized by enemies such as fire and water, or dies and is lost, and through this sorrow arises and the mind is tormented, that is the suffering of separation from what is dear. Meeting with unwelcome things, such as an uncomfortable place, unruly companions and the results of nonvirtuous karma, which constantly harm body and mind and make anger arise, is called the suffering of meeting with what is not dear. Wanting to be with dear ones such as those above and having no way to be with them, and not wanting to meet with what is not dear and yet meeting with it, is called the suffering of not finding what one wants though one seeks it. Alternatively, it also refers to those who are doing virtue wanting virtue to increase and nonvirtue to diminish, and not being able to bring that about.

The meaning of "in brief" is that the eight sufferings are gathered into three kinds. The five, birth, aging, sickness, death and meeting with what is not dear, are the suffering of suffering. Separation from what is dear, and not finding what one wants though one seeks it, are the suffering of change. "The five aggregates of appropriation are suffering" refers to the suffering of conditioning factors. Thus the three sufferings pervade the three feelings, and the three feelings pervade the three realms; and so it is taught that the aggregates of appropriation born in the three realms are the basis of suffering.

If one asks how, there are three kinds of suffering: the initial suffering, the intermediate suffering and the final suffering. Of these, the suffering of birth is the initial one. The six later ones, aging and the rest, are the intermediate suffering. The suffering of death is the final one. Because all suffering is taken up by the five aggregates, this connects with the words "In brief, the five aggregates of appropriation are suffering."

Of the eight unfavorable states, hell beings are tormented by various sufferings and held captive by the hell guardians, so they have no power to do virtue; this is an unfavorable state. Animals are stupid by nature and do not understand signs, so they have no capacity to do virtue; this is an unfavorable state. Hungry ghosts, under the sway of hunger and thirst, are distracted by craving and have no fortune to associate with humans, so they have no occasion to do virtue; this is an unfavorable state. The long-lived gods, by having cultivated the samādhi without perception, remain like logs in one region of the heaven Great Fruit; because their perception has entirely ceased, they have no fortune to do virtue; this is an unfavorable state. People of the borderlands are stupid and do not understand the language, so they have no capacity to understand and practice the meaning of the Dharma; this is an unfavorable state. Those with incomplete faculties, if they lack eyes, ears and the like, have no way to go into the presence of a spiritual friend and listen to the sublime Dharma; this is an unfavorable state. Those with wrong views hold that there is no virtuous or evil karma, no former or later lives and so on, and no spiritual friend whatsoever can turn them back, so they have no fortune to do virtue; this is an unfavorable state. Being born where tathāgatas have not appeared means this: if one is born at a time when the teaching of the previous buddha has declined and the next buddha has not yet appeared, there is no spiritual friend, and so there is nothing to focus on in doing virtue; this is an unfavorable state.

As for what are called the ten masteries: in the state of sentient beings, one is constantly beset, without any control, by ten harms; but a bodhisattva is endowed with the ten masteries that are their antidote, and so the ten harms do not occur.

Mastery over lifespan is this: abiding immutably for inexpressible eons and being forever free of birth and death, one has no harm of death. Mastery over requisites is this: by blessing all the world-systems throughout the expanse of space to be adorned with ornaments, the harm of poverty does not occur. Mastery over mind is this: because one enters with wisdom into countless samādhis, such as the Śūraṃgama, one is not overcome by the harm of the afflictions of dullness and agitation, such as attachment. Mastery over karma is this: one exercises control, so that, for example, even having done nonvirtuous karma one is born in the higher realms, and even having done virtue one is born in the lower realms; therefore the harm of misconduct does not occur. Mastery over birth is this: one displays births everywhere, in various bodies, in various world-systems, and is born at will; therefore, even when born in the lower realms, the harm of the lower destinies does not occur.

Mastery over inclination is this: to those who revile the Three Jewels with contempt and cast them far away, and who commit deeds that leave them bereft of the Dharma, such as destroying the Dharma, one shows all the world-systems filled with buddhas, and shows them meeting many spiritual friends who teach the Dharma; thus one has mastery over inclination, and the harm of being bereft of the Dharma does not occur. Some also explain that it is mastery over inclination because one has attained the totality sense bases.

Mastery over aspiration is this: one makes the aspiration to display manifest awakening at whatever time one likes, in whatever buddha field one pleases; and since one has gained the mastery to display it just as one aspired, the harm of one's wishes not being fulfilled does not occur. Mastery over miraculous power is this: one displays blazing, flying and so on in every buddha field, so one's miraculous power is unobstructed; since one goes anywhere at all in a single instant, the harm of lacking the power to seek the Dharma everywhere does not occur. Mastery over the Dharma is this: one fully comprehends, without exception, mundane and supramundane, conditioned and unconditioned phenomena, and exercises control over them just as one wishes; therefore the harm of fearing reproach from others, such as people of the Dharma, does not occur. Mastery over wisdom is this: since one is endowed with all qualities, such as the powers, the fearlessnesses and the unshared qualities, the harm of others challenging one does not occur.

Of the eight shapes, something like a spear shaft, compared with what is short, is long. Something like an arrow, compared with what is long, is short. Something like a square is square. Something like a round thing is circular. Something like ground without bumps is even. Something like ground with bumps is uneven. Something like a hillock or a knoll is high. Something like the inside of a pond is low.

If one asks what the four equalities are, they are like the equality of the qualities of the buddhas who have come in the three times. That there are neither more nor fewer syllables in the word "buddha" is the equality of letters. Their being alike in possessing the sixty aspects of speech and a voice like the voice of Brahmā is the equality of speech. Their being alike in having realized all phenomena as without arising or ceasing and as the two kinds of selflessness is the equality of Dharma. Their being equal in the dharmakāya of all the buddhas, without any distinction of greater or lesser, is the equality of body.

Each of the Four Truths is further examined as fourfold. For cessation there are cessation, peace, excellence and definite emergence. For the path there are the path, the proper, accomplishment and deliverance. For suffering there are impermanent, suffering, empty and selfless. For the origin there are cause, origin, condition and arising.

Of these, having abandoned the afflictions, such as attachment and aversion, and the evil, nonvirtuous karmas, such as taking life, so that they do not arise, is cessation. That no result of suffering whatsoever produced by karma and affliction arises is peace. Since one will not afterwards be born in the three realms and experience suffering, it is excellence. Since one has escaped the three realms, which are like a prison, and reached the blissful state of nirvāṇa, it is definite emergence.

Since it goes from the level of an ordinary being to the state of a noble one, it is the path. Since it has become the antidote that abandons the improper, the afflictions, it is the proper. Since it abandons the mistaken mind and applies itself to what is unmistaken, it is accomplishment. Since it brings one forever free of the faults of saṃsāra and into the state of nirvāṇa, it is deliverance.

Suffering has the nature of arising and dying in each single instant, so it is impermanent. Since it afflicts one without interruption with the three sufferings and the eight, it is suffering. Since within the five aggregates there is no permanent self such as the non-Buddhists (tīrthikas) imagine, it is empty. Since the five aggregates themselves are also not the permanent self that the non-Buddhists imagine, it is selfless.

As for the origin, the afflictions such as attachment and aversion, and the worldly virtuous and evil karmas that spring from them, are the cause. Those afflictions and karmas deposit imprints in the mental continuum, which remain as the seeds of birth among the five kinds of beings in the three realms; this is the origin. Through later virtuous karma, virtuous imprints are increased, and through nonvirtuous karma, nonvirtuous imprints are increased and made to ripen into results; this is called the condition. When cause and condition thus come together, and the virtuous and nonvirtuous imprints have each been increased, the mass of suffering is brought into being in the higher and lower states of the three realms; this is called arising.

Of the five kinds of monks, someone like a blind beggar is a monk by begging. Someone like a householder to whose name the word "monk" has been attached is a monk in name. One who has not received the vows, or who received them but has broken them and lives without them, and who says, "I too am a monk," is a monk by claim. One who has received pure moral discipline from a preceptor and a master is a monk fully ordained by the formal act of a motion followed by three proclamations. The four pairs and eight individuals, from stream-enterer up to arhat, are monks free of afflictions.

The five obscurations are attachment, malice, torpor, sleep, regret, distraction and doubt.

If one asks what these obscure, they obscure the three higher trainings. Sensual attachment cannot bring itself to give up the qualities of sense pleasure, and so engages in sexual misconduct, theft, robbery and the like; and malice, being angry and unable to give up aversion, takes life and commits many misdeeds such as binding and beating others. Therefore they obscure higher moral discipline. Torpor is the mind's being dull and unclear. Sleep is the mind's being drawn inward, so that it has no freedom to engage objects. Regret is fretting over whatever good or bad deeds one has done, thinking, "That was wrong," so that one cannot settle down. Distraction is running loose after the five qualities of sense pleasure, or after memories of one's past conduct, so that one cannot rest in equanimity or one-pointedness. Therefore they obscure the higher mind. Doubt does not realize, just as they are, the distinct specific and general characteristics of profound and vast phenomena; it is like not knowing which way to go on coming to a fork in the road. Therefore it obscures higher wisdom. In this way they obscure the three trainings.

If one asks what the four foods are, they are said to be the food of morsels, the food of contact, the food of mental volition, and the food of consciousness. Most of the gods and humans of the desire realm, and of animal kind such as livestock, live on the food of morsels and sustain their life by it; so it is the food of morsels. All egg-born creatures, such as birds, live by contact with their mother's warmth and sustain their life by it; so it is called the food of contact. A turtle living in the ocean lays its young on dry land, and for as long as the mother's mind remembers the young, the young's life is sustained; this is called the food of mental volition. Once a famine came and there was nothing to eat. A mother deceived her children, saying, "I am going off to seek provisions; when I come back, I will knead dough from this flour and give it to you." She hung a bundle of ashes from a roof beam and left it there, and the mother went away and died. The children kept looking at the bundle of ashes, and because they never let go of the thought, "When Mother comes, we will knead the dough and eat," they were able to stay alive for a long time without dying. Something like this is called the food of consciousness.

The four meditative concentrations of the Great Vehicle are:

The meditative concentration practiced by the childish,
The meditative concentration that discerns meaning,
The meditative concentration that takes suchness as its object,
And the virtuous meditative concentration of the Tathāgata.

Here, "vehicle" refers to the wisdom of each individual person. That which is narrow in knowledge and teaches merely the selflessness of persons is called the Lesser Vehicle; here, the vast wisdom that realizes both phenomena and persons as emptiness is called the Great Vehicle. As for "meditative concentration," the mind remaining undistracted and one-pointed, unmixed with the stains of affliction, is certainty, or settling. Since from such a cause all the valid wisdom of the greater and lesser vehicles arises as its result, it is called meditative concentration, because it settles thought. The four are those mentioned by name.

Moreover, beings who view things as permanent, pleasurable, possessed of an "I," and clean are called the childish. Meditating on impermanence, suffering, emptiness and impurity for the purpose of abandoning those errors is called the meditative concentration practiced by the childish. On the stage of practice through inclination, apprehending and meditating on the five aggregates in this way, form as like foam, feeling as like a bubble, perception as like a mirage, conditioning factors as like a plantain tree, and consciousness as like an illusion, is called the meditative concentration that discerns meaning. The bodhisattvas from the first ground up to the tenth ground meditating progressively, ground by ground, on the meaning of the emptiness of the dharmadhātu, through the distinctions of their wisdom, is called the meditative concentration that takes suchness as its object. Having fully comprehended all knowables without exception, and having abandoned the afflictive and cognitive obscurations without exception, being spontaneously present without conceptualizing anything at all, and upholding the aims of self and others without exception, is called the virtuous meditative concentration of the Tathāgata.
