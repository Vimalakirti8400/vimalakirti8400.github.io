Verses of Auspiciousness (Maṅgalagāthā)

Verses of Auspiciousness, known in Sanskrit as Maṅgalagāthā and in Tibetan as Tashi kyi tsik su chepa, is a short work of benediction verses that celebrates the auspiciousness of the Three Jewels. After an opening homage, it praises the Buddha in turn as a golden mountain of excellence and protector of the three worlds, the sacred Dharma he taught as the supreme and unshakable virtue of the world, and the Saṅgha as the worthy recipient of offerings from humans, gods, and asuras. Each is declared an auspiciousness that brings peace to beings. Such verses are traditionally recited to invoke blessing and good fortune, often at the close of teachings or ceremonies. The text is Toh 1103 in the Kangyur, where it belongs among the brief prayers and auspicious recitations gathered in the collection's later sections.

In the language of India: maṃgalagāthā. In the language of Tibet: Verses of Auspiciousness.

Homage to the Three Jewels.

Possessed of every excellence, like a mountain of gold,
protector of the three worlds, who has cast off the three stains,
the Buddha, whose eyes are like the petals of a full-blown lotus:
that auspiciousness is the first, which brings peace to beings.

Supreme and unshakable, taught by him,
renowned in the three worlds, honored by gods and humans,
the sacred Dharma that brings peace to beings:
that is the second auspiciousness, the virtue of the world.

The Saṅgha, possessed of the sacred Dharma, rich in the auspiciousness of learning,
the place of offering for humans, gods and asuras,
supreme among assemblies, ground of modesty and of glory:
that is the third auspiciousness, the virtue of the world.

He who holds awakening for the benefit of all beings,
fully endowed with the discipline that accomplishes every aim:
whatever auspiciousness arose of the Sage who delighted in the path of accumulation,
may that auspiciousness bring peace to all beings!

To benefit beings, the Tathāgata came here
from the heart of the celestial palace of the gods of Tuṣita:
whatever auspiciousness arose as gods and their lords surrounded him,
may that auspiciousness bring peace to all beings!

In the delightful Lumbinī grove, its leaves and flowers ablaze with light,
when the Protector was born, as many gods proclaimed:
whatever auspiciousness arose of him who had gone to the end of existence,
may that auspiciousness bring peace to all beings!

In the city of Kapilavastu, in the royal palace,
the Teacher, the Sugata, son of Śuddhodana, was born from the womb:
whatever auspiciousness there was when he was bathed with nectar water swiftly prepared,
may that auspiciousness bring peace to all beings!

In the excellent city called Kapilavastu,
praised by the gods of great and wondrous renown:
whatever auspiciousness of the mass of merit from accomplishing inconceivable virtue,
may that auspiciousness bring peace to all beings!

Joyfully, at midnight, he went forth from the supreme city
and proceeded to the grove of austerities:
whatever auspiciousness there was as hosts of gods, Brahmā and the rest, surrounded him,
may that auspiciousness bring peace to all beings!

Possessed of a body that blazes with light like gold,
seated upon a spread of grass blue as beryl,
cross-legged, supremely unmoving: whatever auspiciousness of that,
may that auspiciousness bring peace to all beings!

On the riverbank, encircled by a garland of blue jays,
he who is most wondrous, who dispels existence:
whatever auspiciousness there was when the king of the nāgas bowed to him,
may that auspiciousness bring peace to all beings!

Going to the foot of the king of trees, by the power of loving-kindness
he subdued the many hosts of Māra; on the earth and in the sky,
whatever manifold auspiciousness of the Blessed One arose,
may that auspiciousness bring peace to all beings!

To destroy all suffering, he sat upon the vajra seat,
and at the dawn of night utterly subdued the four māras:
whatever is the auspiciousness of the Teacher, the Sugata,
may that auspiciousness bring peace to all beings!

The supreme sage, dwelling in the city of Vārāṇasī,
taught the supreme wheel of Dharma: whatever auspiciousness of that,
which was most wondrous both in the realm of the gods and here,
may that auspiciousness bring peace to all beings!

Whatever auspiciousness brings sublime benefit,
praised by noble beings who perform meritorious deeds,
which the Blessed One, the lion Śākyamuni, proclaimed,
may that auspiciousness bring peace to all beings!

To crush the pride of all the tīrthikas,
and to bring happiness to beings, he displayed sublime miracles:
whatever is that auspiciousness of the Victor,
may that auspiciousness bring peace to all beings!

To benefit beings, he came here from the higher realms,
surrounded by hosts of gods, Brahmā and the rest, holding in their hands
yak-tail fans, parasols and many other things: whatever auspiciousness of that,
may that auspiciousness bring peace to all beings!

The Tathāgata who passed into supreme peace,
honored with many māndārava flowers,
praised by the foremost gods: whatever auspiciousness of that,
may that auspiciousness bring peace to all beings!

Bowed to by the lords of gods, of nāgas and of humans,
honored by the lords of garuḍas, yakṣas and gandharvas:
whatever auspiciousness of the Tathāgata endowed with the ten powers,
may that auspiciousness bring peace to all beings!

The Verses of Auspiciousness of the Three Jewels and the Twelve Deeds are complete.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The verses after the first three (on the Three Jewels) follow the twelve deeds of the Buddha, as the colophon states. "Blue jays" renders ཙ་ཤ (Skt. cāṣa). In the Lalitavistara, flocks of these birds circle the Bodhisattva on his way to the seat of awakening. The line on Vārāṇasī could also be read as "in the city of Vārāṇasī, at the place of the supreme sages" (Ṛṣipatana). The reading adopted takes "supreme sage" as the Buddha.
