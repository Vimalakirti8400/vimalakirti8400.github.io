The Noble Sūtra Called 'The Auspicious Night' (Ārya Bhadrarātrī Nāma Sūtra)

Toh 617 in the Kangyur is a Buddhist sūtra known in Sanskrit as the Ārya Bhadrarātrī Nāma Sūtra and in Tibetan as the Phakpa Tsenmо Zangpo Zhéjawé Do, meaning "The Noble Sūtra Called the Auspicious Night." It is a relatively brief canonical discourse belonging to the sūtra class of texts, in which the Buddha's teaching on the Auspicious Night is framed by an encounter between a monk seated by the Tapodā river at Rājagṛha and a radiant deity who appears in the deep of the night to question him about the sūtra. The text thus centers on the importance of knowing, reciting, and understanding this particular teaching, using the dramatic nighttime dialogue as a vehicle for conveying the sūtra's content and merit. It was translated into Tibetan and definitively edited by the great translator Yeshé Dé.

In the language of India: Ārya Bhadrarātrī Nāma Sūtra. In the Tibetan language: the noble sūtra called "The Auspicious Night."

Homage to all the buddhas and bodhisattvas.

Thus did I hear. At one time the Blessed One was dwelling at Rājagṛha, in the Bamboo Grove of Kalandaka, in the grove of Jīvaka. At that time a monk was sitting on the bank of the Tapodā river at Rājagṛha.

Then a god of supreme and radiant form appeared in the deep of the night, went to where the monk was, and illuminated the entire bank of the Tapodā river with a great spreading light. He spoke to that monk, saying: "Monk, do you know the Sūtra of the Auspicious Night?"

The monk replied: "God, I have not sought out the Sūtra of the Auspicious Night. God, do you know or not know the Sūtra of the Auspicious Night?"

The god said: "Monk, I too do not know the Sūtra of the Auspicious Night."

"God, then who is there who does know the Sūtra of the Auspicious Night in the way you are seeking?"

The god said: "Here in Rājagṛha, in the Bamboo Grove of Kalandaka, the Blessed One is dwelling. Go into the presence of the Blessed One and ask him. Whatever the Blessed One teaches you, receive it just as he teaches it and apply yourself diligently." Having said this, the god vanished right there and then.

Then the monk, rising at dawn, went to where the Blessed One was, and having arrived, bowed his head at the Blessed One's feet and sat down to one side. Seated to one side, the monk addressed the Blessed One with these words:

"Venerable One, last night, when the dark of night had grown deep, a god of supreme and radiant form came to where I was. He illuminated the entire bank of the Tapodā river with a vast and spreading light, and spoke to me as follows: 'Monk, do you know the Sūtra of the Auspicious Night?' I replied to him: 'God, I have not sought out the Sūtra of the Auspicious Night. God, do you know or not know the Sūtra of the Auspicious Night?' He said to me: 'Monk, I too do not know the Sūtra of the Auspicious Night.' Then he said: 'Then who is there who does know the Sūtra of the Auspicious Night in the way you are seeking?' And he said to me: 'The Blessed One is dwelling here in Rājagṛha, in the Bamboo Grove of Kalandaka. Go into his presence and ask him. Whatever the Blessed One teaches you, receive it just as he teaches it and apply yourself diligently.' Having said this, the god vanished right there and then. Venerable One, Blessed One, it is for this reason that I make this full request of you."

The Blessed One declared: "Monk, do you know who that god was?" The monk replied: "Venerable One, I did not recognize that god." The Blessed One declared: "Son of the gods, that was Tsandana, the leader of the thirty-three gods."

The monk said: "Venerable One, I too wish to hear the Sūtra of the Auspicious Night."

The Blessed One declared: "Monk, therefore listen well and attentively, and hold it in your mind. I shall explain it to you."

"Monk, if a monk possesses three qualities, he is said to dwell in the Sūtra of the Auspicious Night. What are those three? They are as follows: not running after the past with longing, not placing hope in the future, and remaining ungrasped by the phenomena of the present moment. If one possesses these three qualities, a monk is said to dwell in the Sūtra of the Auspicious Night."

The Blessed One having declared this, the Sugata having spoken thus, the Teacher then spoke further in these words:

One does not chase after what has passed.
One places no hope in what has not yet come.
Whatever was past has already ceased,
and what has not come has not yet arrived.

The phenomena arising in the present moment,
each and every one, one beholds with clarity,
ungrasped by conceptual thought.
A wise one knows this inwardly and deeply.

Who knows whether one will die tomorrow?
Therefore, let one exert oneself this very day.
With the great host of the Lord of Death
there is no bargaining, no making it one's own.

May all sentient beings and all living creatures,
all elemental spirits and all beings whatsoever,
come to accomplish happiness and happiness alone,
may all be without affliction.

May all behold what is good,
and may none come to evil.
Abiding thus, with diligent effort,
with no slackness by day or by night,
this is why the Sage ever taught
this sūtra of the Auspicious Night.

Then, in order to protect all sentient beings, the Blessed One, having taught this sūtra of the Auspicious Night, spoke these root mantras of the spirit-soaring secret formulas:

Tadyathā. Vinī viraṇī. Panī pūraṇī. Buddhāmarte. Manīnī. Ṇi ṇi ṇi ṇi. Ṭi ṭi ṭi ṭi. Paraṭī. Gaurī. Gāndhārī. Caṇḍālī. Mātaṃgī. Pukkasī. Bhamanī. Dramidri dramidri. Śabarī. Sarālambhe. Hīnamadhama dhātaraṇī. Maholaṇa. Dalavānī dalavānī. Dalavate. Mahādalinī. Cālinī. Muṣṭe cakrapātī. Mahācakrapātī. Śabari śabarī. Mahāśabarī. Bhūcidginī. Bhūciranginī. Nimi. Nimonggirī. Nīmindhīnī. Bhūbhānī svāhā.

Tadyathā. Eṭu ṭu ṭu ṭu. Naṭumirṇī. Kirṇī. Eṃku. Siddhatarī. Calalū. Naṃtu. Narlamlū. Gallū. Āvatranone. Śekunīrabaradasale. Tarke. Tare. Tarodtu. Nortu. Tilalalū. Bhūtavatīya. Vatayiye svāhā.

Tadyathā. Aragaṛḍe. Naragaṛḍe. Pāṃsuka. Pāyiye. Kapotakapayiyeye tabodhanī svāhā.

Whatever monk, son of good family, or daughter of good family, for the sake of protecting all sentient beings, bears, retains, reads, brings to mind, perfectly masters, or extensively and correctly teaches to others this sūtra of the Auspicious Night together with its words and verses and these root mantras of the spirit-soaring secret formulas: fire will be unable to harm that person's body, nor poison, nor weapons, nor water, nor royal punishment, nor the punishment of robbers. Untimely death will not befall them, and they will draw assuredly close to nirvāṇa.

In whatever place one is born,
if one reads this sūtra of the Auspicious Night,
one will assuredly attain recollection of past lives,
attain arhatship, and attain sovereignty.

By a single recitation, throughout all places,
untimely death and the intermediate state are avoided.
One does not bring upon oneself what is untimely and contrary to Dharma.
All sentient beings are protected and purified.

Illness, grief, fear, birth,
bad dreams, confusion, and sorrow —
if spoken of, this Auspicious Sūtra transforms them into splendor.

If it is written out and placed upon the body
of a child at birth, or bound upon a woman's body,
good fortune, radiance,
and that person's splendor will arise.

Whatever non-virtue, however slight,
one has committed through body, speech, or mind —
all of it, through this Auspicious Sūtra,
is protected from untimely death.

Kings, water, fire, lightning,
famine, disease, enemies,
combat and conflict —
if one recalls the Auspicious One, one is protected.

If one always recites this noble incantation
and these secret mantras,

one is unafraid and unyielding in every place;
therefore, let this be recited from the heart.

To all the Tathāgatas, arhats, and fully awakened buddhas of the past, those yet to come, and those arisen in the present, I pay homage. Having paid homage, these incantations are to be recited.

May I be made prosperous through these incantations. Thus: nimi nimindhi re, timi timingili, trailokini, alokini, triśūladhāraṇi, akupiṇi krimi kriti, koli koli, kudto kudto, kuto kisi, kuti, kupiti.

Protect, protect me and all sentient beings, together with Vajrapāṇi, from all terrors, all illness, all poison, all epidemic disease, all harmful spirits, all seizures by malevolent forces, all robbers and thieves, all humans and non-humans, from fear and harm and all contagious sickness, from strife, entanglement, evil, quarrel, discord, and all fault-seeking, from all wrongful conduct of body, speech, and mind, and from all terrors everywhere. Thus: hili mili, cili, ili mili, viśīcana, parṇaśabari, cili cili svāhā.

When the Blessed One had so declared, those monks, together with the entire assembly, and the gods, humans, asuras, garuḍas (the great sky-ranging birds), gandharvas, kinnaras, great serpents, yakṣas, rākṣasas, hungry ghosts, and the flesh-eating beings of the world all rejoiced, and they acclaimed what the Blessed One had proclaimed.

The noble sūtra called the Auspicious Night is complete.

The grove name "Jīvaka" is a reasonable reading of "བྱ་ཀ་ལན་ད་ཀ་གནས་པ" as it follows "འོད་མའི་ཚལ" which is the standard rendering of Veṇuvana Kalandakanivāpa; the phrase is the standard locative descriptor of the Bamboo Grove. The full phrase renders the canonical compound Veṇuvana-Kalandakanivāpa, the Bamboo Grove where the Kalandaka squirrels reside, so "Bamboo Grove of Kalandaka" is the accurate rendering rather than invoking Jīvaka. The text is the Tibetan version of the Bhadrarātrī Sūtra, a short Pāli-paralleled text preserved in the Tibetan canon.

The god's name "Tsandana" renders the Tibetan phonetic transliteration of what in Sanskrit and Pāli is Candana; this is the divine leader identified in the parallel Pāli text as a deity of the Tāvatiṃsa heaven (the heaven of the thirty-three gods, Skt. Trāyastriṃśa).

The phrase rendered "not running after the past with longing" literally means "not coming into contact afterward with the past," a technical meditative instruction meaning one does not pursue or revisit past experience with craving or conceptual elaboration. Similarly, "remaining ungrasped by present phenomena" means that arising phenomena do not seize or carry away the mind. These renderings reflect the standard commentarial understanding of this triad.  The phrase rendered "spirit-soaring secret formulas" translates a Tibetan term that literally means "the mantras of those who fly through space" or "the mantras with a basis in sky-travellers," likely referring to a class of protective dhāraṇī associated with ḍākinīs or vidyādharīs. The rendering is a compromise between literalism and readability for a listening audience.

The mantra strings are transliterated as they appear in the Tibetan phonetic rendering, which is itself a transcription of Sanskrit or a related Indic language. The phonetic reconstructions are approximate; the underlying Sanskrit originals cannot be established with certainty from the Tibetan alone, and the forms appear to name a series of female protective figures including Gaurī, Gāndhārī, Caṇḍālī, Mātaṃgī, Pukkasī, and Śabarī, all known classes of ḍākinī-like beings in tantric literature.

The final verse of the passage is syntactically incomplete as given, ending mid-stanza. The translation faithfully renders what is present without supplying a conclusion.  The mantra syllables in both dhāraṇī passages are transliterated directly from the Tibetan phonetic rendering of Sanskrit; no attempt is made to restore a definitive Sanskrit original, as the phonetic forms show some corruption in transmission. The rendering follows the Tibetan as given.

The term rendered "fault-seeking" translates a Tibetan expression meaning dragging out or exposing another's faults or blemishes, a recognized category of harmful social conduct.

The colophon names are kept in their recognized Sanskrit forms: Jinamitra and Dānaśīla are known translators of the imperial-period translation bureau, and Ye shes sde (Yeshé Dé) is the celebrated Tibetan chief editor of that period.
