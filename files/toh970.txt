The Noble Dhāraṇī of Maṇibhadra (Āryamaṇibhadranāmadhāraṇī)

The Noble Dhāraṇī of Maṇibhadra, known in Sanskrit as the Āryamaṇibhadranāmadhāraṇī and in Tibetan as Phakpa Norbu Zangpo'i Zung, is a short dhāraṇī scripture set at Śrāvastī in the Jeta Grove, the park of Anāthapiṇḍada, where the Buddha was staying. In it the son of Maṇibhadra, the great general of the yakṣas, approaches the Blessed One and vows to follow closely and serve any monk, nun, layman, or laywoman who recites his heart mantra three times each day, providing for their needs, including food and clothing. The text thus presents a protective and prosperity-granting incantation associated with a powerful yakṣa figure linked to wealth and guardianship. It is Toh 970 in the Kangyur, where it belongs to the class of dhāraṇī texts, the collection of incantations and mantras valued for their protective and beneficial power.

In the language of Tibet: The Noble Dhāraṇī of Maṇibhadra.

Homage to the Three Jewels.

Thus did I hear at one time. The Blessed One was staying at Śrāvastī, in the Jeta Grove of Prince Jeta, the park of Anāthapiṇḍada. Then the son of Maṇibhadra, the great general of the yakṣas, went to where the Blessed One was. When he arrived, he bowed his head at the feet of the Blessed One and sat down to one side. Seated to one side, the son of Maṇibhadra, the great general of the yakṣas, said this to the Blessed One:

"Venerable One, if any monk, nun, layman, or laywoman recites this heart mantra three times each day, I wish to follow close behind that person always. I will also do everything that person needs. I wish also to grant food, clothing, wealth, gold, riches, grain, and silver. I will also bring all that person's aims to fulfillment. I will also bring all sentient beings under that person's power. I wish also to accomplish at once all of that person's various tasks, except for anything involving sexual union."

If one recites this seven times, it will be accomplished.

This is its rite. On the fifteenth day of the waxing moon, having made oneself clean, one burns agaru incense at the three times of day. If one recites it seven thousand times, one will obtain gold.

The Tibetan reads literally "the son of Maṇibhadra, the great general of the yakṣas," and the translation follows it. The phrase could also be read as calling Maṇibhadra himself the speaker, since the dhāraṇī invokes Maṇibhadra by name. The text as it stands names his son, however, so that reading has been kept. Lui Wangpo (Klu'i dbang po) is a Tibetan translator, so his name is given in Tibetan phonetics rather than Sanskrit.
