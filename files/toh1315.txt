The Means of Accomplishing Kurukullā, Arisen from Hevajra (Hevajrodbhava Kurukullāsādhana)

The Means of Accomplishing Kurukullā, Arisen from Hevajra, known in Sanskrit as the Hevajrodbhava Kurukullā Sādhana and in Tibetan as Dgyes pa'i rdo rje las byung ba'i ku ru kul le'i sgrub thabs, is a concise sādhana, a manual of tantric deity practice, devoted to the red goddess Kurukullā as she appears within the Hevajra cycle. It guides the practitioner through inviting the buddhas and bodhisattvas, the sevenfold practice and the four immeasurables, dissolving all into emptiness, raising the vajra enclosure of protection, and generating oneself as the one-faced, four-armed goddess holding a flowered bow and arrow, an utpala noose and a hook, before reciting her mantra and noting the numbers of recitations said to draw kings, ordinary people and other beings under her sway. Its colophon attributes it to the great master Sahajavilāsa and names Dānaśīla as its translator. It is Toh 1315 in the Tengyur, where it is placed among the tantra commentaries of the Hevajra class.

In the language of India: hevajredhava kurukulle sādhanaṃ. In the language of Tibet: The Means of Accomplishing Kurukullā, Arisen from Hevajra.

Homage to the goddess Kurukullā.

First of all, the mantra practitioner should imagine at his own heart, upon a moon disk, a red syllable byāḥ. By its light he should invite into the sky in front of him the many buddhas and bodhisattvas, headed by the goddess. Having worshipped them with offerings, mentally produced or actually arranged, as is fitting, he should perform the sevenfold practice, beginning with going for refuge. After that he should extend the four immeasurables to all sentient beings.

Then, with the svabhā mantra, he should seal the whole world and its inhabitants as emptiness. After that, out of the state of emptiness, he should imagine a sun disk arising from the syllable re, and upon it meditate on a blue hūṃ. From its complete transformation comes a crossed vajra. From the complete transformation of that, above, below, and in all the cardinal and intermediate directions, everything is enclosed by a vajra ground, a vajra fence and a vajra tent. Then he should recite this mantra: oṃ rakṣa rakṣa hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ phaṭ svāhā.

Then he should imagine at his own heart a moon disk upon a red lotus, and upon it meditate on the seed, a red syllable hrīḥ. From the complete transformation of the hrīḥ he should visualize a red utpala, and upon it meditate on an expansive disk of a moon red in color. From the complete transformation of that, he should generate himself as the goddess Kurukullā, red in body color.

She has one face and four arms, and wears the five bone ornaments. She has a lower garment of tiger skin, and sits in the posture of half-crossed legs. Her yellow hair bristles upward. Her head is adorned with the buddha Amitābha. She holds as hand emblems a bow and arrow, a noose of utpala, and a hook.

The yogin who has meditated in this way should perform the mantra recitation, reciting oṃ kurukulle hrīḥ svāhā at a volume that cannot be heard.

In half a month a king is drawn in;
with ten thousand recitations ordinary people are brought under control;
with ten million, cattle, yakṣas and the like;
with seven hundred thousand, the asuras.

"The seed, a red syllable hrīḥ" passes over the syllable "rmi" after "seed", which makes no sense here and appears to be a scribal intrusion. "Five bone ornaments" renders "five mudrās" (phyag rgya lnga), the standard sense for such deities. The author's name here (lhan skyes sgeg pa) differs slightly in Tibetan from that in Toh 1314 (lhan cig skyes pa'i rol pa); both are rendered Sahajavilāsa, though Sahajalalita is also possible.
