The Six-Branched Yoga (Ṣaḍaṅgayoga)

The Six-Branched Yoga, known in Sanskrit as the Ṣaḍaṅgayoga and in Tibetan as Jorwa Yenlak Drukpa, is a short Indian treatise on the completion-stage practice of the Kālacakra tradition, composed by the accomplished paṇḍita Anupamarakṣita and translated into Tibetan by the Indian scholar Vibhūticandra together with the translator of Nyal, Minyam Zangpo. Opening with homage to the great adept Śabaripa and verses praising Avalokiteśvara as the clear moon rising in a sky free of obscuring clouds, it sets out a path beyond the extremes of existence and nonexistence. Its heart is the six branches of yoga, namely withdrawal, meditative concentration, breath control, retention, recollection and samādhi, explained with reference to the inner signs such as smoke, the movement of the winds into the central channel, and the unwaning bliss that culminates in Mahāmudrā and buddhahood. The work is Toh 1367 in the Tengyur, where it belongs among the commentaries and practice texts on the tantras.

In the language of India: śaḍaṅgayoganāma. In the language of Tibet: The Six-Branched Yoga.

Homage to the glorious Śabaripa.

In the sky free of the massed clouds of obscuration,
where at the end of counting all winds and thoughts have ceased,
Avalokiteśvara rises,
beautiful as the full moon.
He who frees from the objectifications of existence and peace,
the pervader, is that very one, not separate;
though empty, he is endowed with all aspects;
lord of the grounds, he does not abide at all.
Though unmoving and free of all thought,
he shows the wise the undiminishing bliss.
Protector who dawns from what is not mind,
may he bless the minds of beings.

The conception of existence becomes the extreme of permanence;
the unexamined conception of nonexistence is the cause of the Avīci hell.
When the path of liberation has thus been erased,
the one who teaches emptiness beyond mind,
inseparable from compassion,
the image in the sky:
to that guru, with great joy,
I always bow with reverence.

Praising such a guru, the great reality,
is simply voicing my own understanding:
when there is no blazing fire,
where would a stream of smoke arise?
Though it is a fault to speak of one's realization,
since it leads others in, it is a cause of others' benefit.
In a matter the teacher has not made certain,
how would one who wishes to accomplish the purpose engage?

Mantrins swollen with pride at understanding the provisional meaning
have strayed from the path.
So that, through words that bring refutation to bear, those who have not understood might understand,
and for the sake of the whole world of the Buddha's word,
Mañjughoṣa, in words easy to understand,
by way of compiling the teaching, in Śambhala
taught well the Abridged Kālacakra Tantra.
That emanation body of Great Compassion
composed the commentary called Stainless Light.
Having gained the definitive meaning that makes it clear,
all the learned clear away error in the world and take their delight.

Where, toward all that can be known,
the name "omniscient" bears its meaning,
the whole host of conceptions eager to arise,
deluded and become without essence,
the Perfection of Wisdom,
born of space, the one mother of the buddhas,
destroys, so that they never arise again.
Where it is taught that the half-open eyes
are held steady on the stainless sky,
then from great bliss, the realization of emptiness, free of thought,
arise smoke and the other signs, free of thought.

Therefore I shall gather a precious collection of scripture that clarifies the completion stage, that accords with meditation on the path of Mahāmudrā, that is taught in the tantras such as the glorious Ādibuddha, and that bestows the fruit of perfect and complete buddhahood itself. As it is taught in the Ādibuddha, king of tantras, the precious casket of the buddhas:

For the lord, the king of the drop, undiminishing and blissful, master of gnosis, endowed with all aspects, holding the illusory display of all beings,
the vowels and consonants and the sun and moon seats are not those of the Vajra Holder;
a bodily form transformed from the syllable hūṃ and hand emblems are not accepted.

So it is said. Therefore, consider the beginner yogin who earnestly wishes to accomplish the undiminishing supreme attainment; who has received the pith instructions of a holy guru; who has abandoned unruly companions; who has become accustomed to the signs such as smoke and has trained the central channel; who regards all sentient beings as an only child; who relies on worldly and transcendent truth; who has no attachment to one's own body, children, wife and the like; who keeps apart from enjoying the wealth of hermitages, temples and the guru; who follows the path of the buddhas and bodhisattvas; who abides in the four abodes of Brahmā, loving-kindness, compassion, joy and equanimity; who is free of the wish to accomplish worldly attainments such as pacifying; who wishes to attain the state of omniscience; who sees unborn phenomena, like an image called down into a divination mirror, on the mirror of emptiness, the dharmadhātu endowed with all aspects, their nature like an optical illusion or a dream; who is free of analyzing phenomena as aggregates of atoms; who has put nihilistic emptiness far away; who is devoted to the inner bliss, the phenomenon of self-awareness; who has entirely abandoned the bliss of the outer senses; and who strives solely to make stable the awakening mind that is the nature of wisdom and method. Because such a one has received the pith instruction on the path of supreme great bliss, wisdom-gnosis, it is said that such a one does not accept conceptions of support and supported, of hand emblems, of color, of shape, or of a maṇḍala.

Likewise:

The reality taught by the Victor,
fully divided into sixteen and into four,
is not attained by the paths of various conceptions,
not even once in a hundred.
Beyond the generation stage,
conceptual with hūṃ phaṭ and the like,
the yoga of the completion stage
alone is the cause of that reality, and nothing else.
Though fire abides in wood,
it is not seen even if the wood is chopped to pieces;
yet just as, by rubbing the fire drill on its base,
it is seen right there,
so by all the methods of habituating conceptual thought
the way mind appears is not seen;
but by joining rasanā and lalanā into one,
right there it comes to appear.
The signs are smoke and the rest.
Through breath control moving in the central channel,
the observance with the consort ever unimpaired,
and the bodhicitta moving upward,
by this path of all the buddhas
one will attain the bliss of yoga,
and the innate bliss of all the messengers,
the undiminishing supreme bliss, will be attained.
Meditators on maṇḍalas and the like,
who conceive of forms and so forth,
may achieve worldly accomplishments,
but how could the great accomplishment they desire arise?

Compassion for sentient beings, the welfare of others,
and constant striving in virtue:
a thoroughly pure disciple,
through the guru's kindness, obtains the supreme path.
Even if this excellent jewel, the supreme path,
is given to a careless person,
robbers in the domain of one who has entered
the forest of his own household will steal it away.
One who enters the forest of kinsfolk,
with its robbers, carelessness, laziness, doubt,
sleep and regret,
will have the supreme path of liberation stolen.
It is stolen too by the robbers of killing, taking what is not given, lying,
slander, harsh speech, the wives of others,
idle talk, covetousness, malice,
and wrong views.
By the robbers of utterly immoderate food and drink
and of many enjoyments,
carelessness prevails, as with the deluded,
and even from one who has diligence it will be stolen.

One who does not know whether it is a jewel or not
shows it to another who is unskilled;
through the words of the unskilled,
that priceless jewel is lost to him.
One who does not know should show it
to one skilled in the qualities of jewels;
through that one's pure words
one should come to know one's own jewel.

Father and mother, daughter, sister, younger brother,
brothers, and a charming wife,
and even all the buddhas and bodhisattvas:
in these forms the māra of form creates obstacles.

Having abandoned the robbers and the kinsfolk,
the disciple, striving, having heard what comes from the jewel
through the kindness of the holy guru,
should keep it utterly secret.
The stage of self-blessing and the stage of the hero
are not the path to liberation;
the utterly pure stage alone
was taught by the buddhas for the sake of liberation.
By the very meaning of empowerment,
in the tantra endowed with wisdom and method,
even those who bear the five deeds of immediate retribution
are gathered in by the buddhas through bliss.
The stage of the hero is not external:
it is what exhausts the life-wind in the body.
Whatever appearance of the three realms there is in the sky,
that is explained as self-blessing.
In saṃsāra, which has no core,
by its exhaustion, from that very thing,
just as the plantain yields its fruit when it is spent,
the fruit of buddhahood will arise.
The she-mule and the scorpion
are born from a mother and kill the mother;
the yogin's mind, arisen from illusion,
in the same way kills illusion.

Nondual, unmoving, without flow,
the undiminished commitment, the great passion,
neither a thing nor a nonthing,
the gnosis being, Samantabhadra,
the soundless great sound, the innate,
the glory that holds the bodhicitta drop,
the vajra of Kālacakra,
the yoga endowed with wisdom and method:
such is the vajra yoga
relied upon and taught by many sugatas,
and the Vajra Holder, those of the Secret Mantra family,
and all the bodhisattvas compiled it.

Abiding in the vajra drop, keeping the commitments,
free of turbidity, without desire,
patient, abiding in the path, meditating on that reality,
protecting disciples from the terrors of hell,
showing the path, practicing pure conduct in the true sense,
the conqueror of māras, renowned as Vajrasattva:
such a guru one should first rely upon.

The visualization of the deity is of two kinds:
direct perception and inference.
Through the yoga of reality, in the sky,
like stars, directly,
many enjoyment bodies appear.
For the yogin not yet fully matured,
for the sake of familiarization, drawings and the like,
a conception that is not ultimate,
like a dead corpse, are inference.

Here, by force of the dispositions of sentient beings, yogins have two kinds of deity visualization: direct perception and inference. Of these, direct perception is, through the yoga of reality, apprehending many enjoyment bodies, like stars in the sky, like illusions and dreams, the three existences of the three times, with the fleshly eye and the others. Here, at first, since there is no clairvoyance, the beginner yogin sees various images with the fleshly eye. Once clairvoyance has arisen, one sees with the divine eye. Being free of attachment, one sees with the buddha eye. Having become a bodhisattva, one sees with the eye of wisdom. On perfect and complete awakening, one sees with the eye of gnosis, being free of any remaining aggregates. Thus, for the sake of seeing emptiness, the Tathāgata taught the five eyes, the fleshly eye and the rest. Other sentient beings, who do not see emptiness, are simply blind. Such is the definitive account of meditation on reality.

A practice that is not reality, inference without direct perception, which is like a dead body, is to be shown by means of conceptions that are not ultimate, such as drawings. Thus, having made cast and fashioned images of the buddhas and bodhisattvas, or having drawn a maṇḍala, one should make stable that which has a definite form, in order to meditate in the yoga of mental aspiration. Such is the definitive account of meditation by way of conception.

Approach is by the five nectars and the rest,
by the four vajras, by mantra recitation and the rest.
Near accomplishment is by individual discernment and the rest,
and by the nectar arising from the lotus and vajra.
Accomplishment is by the joys and the rest,
and by the three vajras of one taste in the lotus.
Great accomplishment is by the subtle yoga
and by union with the consort without loss.

Here the beginner should first perform the approach through the rite of the sādhana. The five outer nectars are excrement and the rest. The word "and the rest" means the five lamps. The five inner nectars are the five aggregates. Eating them to please the deity is approach. By the word "and the rest," the five lamps are the five senses. To be without regard for them is approach, that is, the complete abandonment of craving for body and wealth. It is through that approach that the deity grants the supreme attainment, not through eating excrement and the like.

The four vajras are being without regard for the enjoyments of body, speech and mind, and being without regard for the bliss of emission; that is the meaning of the observance of pure conduct of body, speech and mind. It is by this that the deity grants the supreme attainment, not through regard for the enjoyments of craving.

As for "mantra recitation and the rest," recitation is breath control, and by the word "and the rest" is meant the practice of expelling, filling and holding. This is approach: it is by this that the deity surely grants the supreme attainment, and not by leaving the life-wind unrestrained. That is the definitive meaning. Reciting with an ordinary rosary in the practice, and the like, is the provisional meaning.

Near accomplishment is individual discernment and the rest. The food of those in saṃsāra is the apprehending of forms and the like by the eyes and the rest. Turning back from that is individual discernment: apprehending objects in the image of emptiness with other eyes and the rest. Likewise there are meditative concentration, breath control and retention. In the definitive meaning, near accomplishment is by the nectar arising from the vajra and the lotus. In the provisional meaning, it is the generation of all the deities of the retinue.

Accomplishment is explained as the joys and the rest. Here the joys and the rest, the three vajras, are the drops of body, speech and mind. Meditating on the drop that has become of one taste in the heart, the navel and the secret lotus is accomplishment. Great accomplishment, in the definitive meaning, the subtle yoga, is by the seed moving upward in the central channel, that is, by the undiminishing bliss of union with the wisdom consort. In the provisional meaning, it is the practice of the drop at the tip of the nose of the wisdom consort's source of phenomena, and the like.

First, emptiness and awakening;
then holding the moon drop;
from that the body is generated;
the blessing of the senses by the six families:
this is the common approach.
Near accomplishment is by the four vajras;
here there are four kinds, beginning with the lesser method;
accomplishment and great accomplishment also in speech.

Here, in the generation stage, first there is awakening from emptiness. For sentient beings, between finally casting off the aggregates and not yet taking up other aggregates, there is the seeing of the three existences as empty, having the character of emptiness: that is called emptiness. After that, holding the moon drop is the taking hold, in the mother's womb, of the drop that is the seed of the foundation consciousness. Generating the body from that is the completion of the form in seven months.

The blessing of the eyes and the rest by the six families is their engaging with objects such as forms. In the same way, the beginner should also meditate on the conceptual nature in the practice of the deity. This is the common approach. Near accomplishment is the complete perfection of the life-wind; the four vajras come about through the perfection of body, speech and mind, the three, and the gnosis vajra.

Here the four kinds of method are approach, near accomplishment, accomplishment and great accomplishment, up to the age of sixteen. Here the lesser is the newborn infant. Once the teeth have come in, one is a middle child. When the teeth are shed, one is a youth. From the coming in of the new teeth up to sixteen, one reaches maturity, for the sake of begetting children. In the same way, the four branches should also be meditated on for the deities. Such is the definitive account of worldly truth.

Now the generation of the buddha body by ultimate truth is described. First, awakening from emptiness is thinking of nothing at all in pitch darkness. Then holding the moon drop is the arising of the signs, smoke and the rest, up to the great drop. Begetting the child is seeing everything from that drop. "Of the six families" means by the six aggregates free of obscuration, and the blessing is the cessation of the aggregates, elements and sense sources of ordinary beings. After that, attainment comes through the divisions beginning with the lesser, up to the lord of the twelve grounds. Then:

The meaning of truth in twelve aspects,
knowing reality in sixteen aspects,
awakening in twenty aspects,
the aspect supreme among all buddhas.

Such is the definitive account. Likewise:

Having meditated on the path of the sky, smoke and the rest,
up to the various forms,
then the two, the life-wind and the downward-clearing wind,
should be made firm in the wheel of the channels.
The yogin's bodhicitta moon
should be placed like Rāhu.
Whatever one desires, in this very life,
will be attained within a few days.

The instruction on meditating step by step on this king of paths, which the Blessed One taught in the supreme Ādibuddha, is as follows:

Having meditated on smoke and the rest,
the mind should be made firm on its own.
Having purified the central channel,
one should accomplish the undiminishing supreme.
Having placed the vajra in the lotus,
the life-wind should be made to abide in the drop.
Over the drops one is a wheel-turning monarch;
the vajra drop should be held back.
The yogin is ever constantly upright,
the bodhicitta ever moving upward.
Through union with the Mahāmudrā,
blessed by the descent of the vajra,
endowed with twenty-one thousand,
together with six hundred, great victors,
the moments of undiminishing bliss:
through pure conduct one becomes Vajrasattva.
As long as the ten have not arisen,
one should not take another wisdom consort.
Because outward distraction arises,
one also meditates on the signs.
Individual discernment is Vajrasattva himself,
ten in kind; meditative concentration is Akṣobhya;
breath control is Amoghasiddhi, ten in kind;
retention is Ratnasambhava;
recollection is in Ḍombī,
and it is likewise Amitābha;
meditative absorption is Vairocana.
Individual discernment is the ten, objects and subjects,
not engaging.
Meditative concentration is wisdom in the body,
conception, analysis, joy,
and the blissful mind one-pointed.
Breath control, having abandoned the two paths,
is the life-wind moving in the center.
Retention is the life-wind entering the drop
without the two movements.
Recollection is the arising of appearances
in the body and in the sky, up to caṇḍālī.
Meditative absorption is undiminishing bliss
through the union of wisdom and method.
Through individual discernment, free of objects,
the blessing of all mantras arises.
Purified through the yoga of meditative concentration,
one attains the five clairvoyances.
Purified by breath control free of sun and moon,
one is praised by all the bodhisattvas.
By the power of retention, which takes the life
of the māras and afflictions, one enters the ten powers.
Being purified by recollection,
a halo of light arises from the gnosis body.
Through meditative absorption, within a few days,
the gnosis body will be accomplished.
If the mantrin has not accomplished the aim
through individual discernment and the rest,
then by the forceful method of familiarization with nāda
one should accomplish it by stopping the drop
at the jewel of the vajra in the lotus.
In approach, the yoga of the drop
is the ten aspects of the sky,
through the wrathful gaze of the Cakrin.
Near accomplishment, the six yogas,
is through the gaze of Vighnāntaka,
abiding in the path of nectar.
Accomplishment is when the drop arisen from union with the consort,
having reached the jewel of the vajra,
becomes the undiminishing three.
The undiminishing single bliss, peace,
the innate gnosis, is great accomplishment.

Now the six yogas are explained. Individual discernment, at the beginning, is Vajrasattva, that is, the aggregate of gnosis. It has ten aspects by the division of the signs: smoke, mirage, fireflies, a lamp, blazing, the moon, the sun, Rāhu, lightning and the drop. By the division of appearances, it is the aggregate of unimagined gnosis.

Meditative concentration, Akṣobhya, has ten aspects; it is the aggregate of consciousness. The ten, objects and subjects, becoming one in the various forms is meditative concentration.

Breath control is also ten: Amoghasiddhi, the aggregate of formations, because the ten maṇḍalas of left and right become one.

Retention, Ratnasambhava, is the aggregate of feeling. The holding of the life-wind has ten aspects, by the division of going to and coming from the lotuses of the navel, the heart, the throat, the forehead and the crown.

Recollection in Ḍombī is Amitābha, the aggregate of perception, in ten aspects. Recollection is in Ḍombī, that is, the central channel, through the division of the ten states of desire.

Meditative absorption, Vairocana, has ten aspects, through the cessation of the ten winds. Thus the Blessed One, because the winds do not move, is the utterly nonabiding nirvāṇa.

Next the characteristics of individual discernment and the rest are described. Individual discernment is consciousness not engaging through the connection of the ten objects and subjects. "Wisdom in the body" is the five other eyes and the rest fully engaging the empty objects, the images in the sky. Likewise, wisdom is the seeing of that very object. Conception is apprehending it as a thing. Analysis is ascertainment. Joy is attachment to the various forms. Unmoving bliss is making the various forms and the mind one. Thus, by the division of apprehended and apprehender, meditative concentration has ten aspects.

"Breath control, having abandoned the two paths," means abandoning the left and right paths: these having ceased, the wind enters the central path. That too is ten, through the cessation of the ten maṇḍalas. The drop is the life-wind entering at the forehead.

"Without the two movements" means free of going and coming. Retention is holding the life-wind at the forehead, that is, the mind one-pointed. "Appearances up to caṇḍālī" is recollection, the appearance of the three existences in the sky; its ten aspects are just as explained before. "Through the union of wisdom and method": because the knowable and consciousness have become one, by the force of undiminishing bliss in the form of gnosis, meditative absorption has ten aspects, since the life-wind and the rest no longer move. Such is the practice of the six-branched yoga.

Now the benefits of individual discernment and the rest are described. One becomes purified by individual discernment when the various images have become stable; at that time one is blessed by all the mantras. By mere speech one grants boons and the like. As for the five clairvoyances, when the eyes no longer close, then the divine eye arises. In the same way, through the purity of meditative concentration, the divine ear arises as well. As for purification by breath control, when the wind, free of the paths of the sun and moon, moves constantly in the center, then, being purified by breath control, one is praised by the bodhisattvas.

As for the ten powers, free of the māras, the afflictions and the rest: the empty image is the apprehended, and the apprehending mind's entering into it is by the power of retention. With the exhaustion of the going and coming of the life-wind, they become of one taste. Purification by recollection is the mind's embracing the image, that is, being free of all conceptions. At that time a stainless halo of light arises, and five rays of light also stream forth from the pores of the body. "From the gnosis body" means from the body of emptiness. As for the purification of meditative absorption: because the two, the apprehended and the apprehending mind, have become one, that undiminishing bliss is meditative absorption, and to be purified by it is to have become free. "Within a few days" means that it is accomplished in three years and three fortnights. As for the gnosis body, one becomes a bodhisattva who has attained the ten masteries and the rest. Such is the definitive account of individual discernment and the rest.

Now the practice of the forceful method is explained. If, though one sees the empty images through individual discernment and the rest, the undiminishing moment does not arise, because the life-wind is not pressed down, then by the forceful method of familiarization with nāda one should make the life-wind move in the central channel, and, by stopping the bodhicitta drop at the jewel of the vajra in the lotus, one should accomplish the undiminishing moment without emission. This is the forceful method.

As for familiarization with nāda, it is as the Blessed One taught in the chapter on sādhana:

The energy that goes from the navel
to the supreme place, at the end of the twelve and the sixteen,
dissolves into the navel; like a staff,
like the fire of lightning, it moves upward.
Set moving in the central channel, it flows gently;
it abides in the wheel, at the aperture of the crown;
like a needle it touches the skin outside.
At that time the downward-clearing wind moves upward.
With the two winds blocked, the crown is pierced,
and one will enter the body of another.
Thus, having made the vajra rise,
when the mind is free of apprehended and apprehender,
one goes to travel in the sky,
and there is Viśvamātā, endowed with the five clairvoyances.

Now, to accomplish the image of emptiness, the gazes are explained. Here approach is meditation on the signs such as smoke. Therein the first practice is the mind's apprehending the signs such as smoke. That too has ten aspects: the ten gnoses that observe smoke and the rest. Moreover, through the wrathful gaze of Uṣṇīṣacakravartin, looking upward without closing the eyes, the signs arise: four kinds in the practice by night and six kinds in the practice by day. Thus, up to the image, individual discernment and meditative concentration are the branches of approach.

As for "the path of nectar" and the rest, Vighnāntaka is Amṛtakuṇḍalin, and his gaze is directed to the forehead, the seat of nectar. By directing it there, near accomplishment there has six branches. Seeing the image, one should practice breath control and the holding of the life-wind: this is near accomplishment. "The drop arisen from union with the consort" is the drop arisen from passion for the wisdom consort, abiding in the vajra jewel. The undiminished three are at the secret place, the navel and the heart. In this way one should perform the branch of accomplishment. "The undiminishing single bliss, peace": the undiminishing moment of the bodhicitta is called peace. "The innate gnosis, great accomplishment" is the fourth branch, the great, in which undiminishing bliss and the bodhicitta have become one together.

The Mahāmudrā, like an illusion,
in the sky, to the mind that is no mind,
appears like a reflection in a mirror,
and makes things appear in the three realms.
Like lightning it sends forth many rays;
it is not separate from the illusory body;
there is no division of object and sense faculty;
it is mere appearance, abiding in the sky.
Though the mind that is no mind is single,
it embraces the minds of the various beings.
Arisen from one's own mind, a mere appearance of mind,
it is like a reflection within a mirror.
All yogins should rely on it;
the buddhas with their heirs rely on it.
When that fire of gnosis blazes,
it burns away the net of conceptions
together with the aggregates and the rest.
For all yogins, within one year,
it brings the attainment of innate bliss.
Wisdom is the apprehending mind itself;
gnosis is what it apprehends:
the ten, smoke and the rest, mere appearances.
That apprehender, free of external objects,
dissolving into the apprehended object, is empowerment.
From that, the undiminishing innate,
the bliss of nirvāṇa,
is the fourth, beyond compare, transcending the world.
Whoever holds this word of the buddhas
in heart and on lips is the supreme guru.

Here wisdom is the apprehending mind. Its appearances, the ten aspects of smoke and the rest, are what it apprehends, like an image called down in divination or a reflection in a mirror; what is apprehended is gnosis. The reflection of one's own eye in a mirror is what one's own eye apprehends. "Dissolving into the apprehended object is empowerment" means that the apprehending wisdom fully enters the gnosis that is the apprehended side and does not enter external objects; individual discernment, meditative concentration, breath control and retention are all called dissolution, and this applies to the six branches. "From that" means from the dissolution: the undiminishing innate and the bliss of nirvāṇa is the bliss of the fourth. The meaning is that it transcends the worldly examples of the bliss of a child, of one come of age, and the supreme bliss of emission, being free of the conduct of the three realms.

It is free of laughing, gazing, touching, embracing, holding hands and sexual union; it does not arise from the cause of the action seal or the mental seal; it has the character of emptiness in which all aspects appear distinctly. This word of the Buddha is the method of gnosis. The master in whose heart it abides, because he has experienced it himself, on whose lips it is, because he teaches it to his disciples, and with whom it is at all times, is the supreme guru; that is the meaning of Vajra Holder. It is not another, one who knows only the bliss of the two organs.

With the mind fixed on the sky, the eyes unblinking,
entered on the vajra path, from the lotus
appear smoke, mirage, fireflies,
lamp, blaze, moon, sun,
Rāhu, lightning and the drop.
Then the image without an object,
many enjoyment bodies, appear.

Now entry into the appearance of one's own mind is described. In the Mantra Vehicle and the Perfection Vehicle there are here two ways of meditating in yoga: meditating in yoga on space, and doing so where there is no roof. Practicing yoga on space means that at night, in a room without openings, in black darkness, by fixing the mind on space, one sees the signs such as smoke. "Entered on the vajra path" means the life-wind entering the central channel. From the space before the unblinking eyes, the four called smoke, mirage, fireflies and lamp are seen through the practice by night. Then one sees the cloudless sky, and from that sky, through the practice by day, one sees the cloudless sky blaze, as in the words, "Arisen from space, self-arisen, the great fire of wisdom and gnosis." Here, withdrawal is this: the eye faculty and the rest do not engage eye consciousness and the rest toward outer forms and so on; instead, toward inner objects such as smoke, the divine eye and the rest fully engage divine-eye consciousness and the rest. By focusing on inner emptiness, one sees, within emptiness, all things that have not been imagined, just as a maiden sees in prasena divination; this is called individual discernment, because one sees the bodies of the three realms.

Meditative concentration is seeing all phenomena as empty. Wisdom is the mind's full entry into them. Conception is the mind's grasping of a thing. Analysis is discerning things one by one. Joy is the mind's abiding in all things. Unmoving bliss is the perfection of all bliss from all things. In this way meditative concentration possesses five limbs.

Then breath control: the wind's paths of lalanā and rasanā, left and right, are blocked, and the life-wind constantly enters the path of avadhūtī, the central channel. Through the practices of filling, of the vase, and of expulsion, it enters the central channel with the syllable oṃ, is held there with the syllable hūṃ, and goes back out with the syllable āḥ. Recollection: seeing the image of one's own chosen deity without conceptualization, and from it the halos of light of rays of many kinds and the manifold emanations of the three realms, is called recollection.
