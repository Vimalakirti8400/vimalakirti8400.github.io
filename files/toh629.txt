The Noble Dhāraṇī Called the Complete Vanquishing of Bandits (Āryacauravid hvaṃsana nāma dhāraṇī)

Toh 629 in the Kangyur is a dhāraṇī text known in Sanskrit as the Āryacauravid hvaṃsana nāma dhāraṇī and in Tibetan as Pakpa Migö Nampar Jompa Zhejawa'i Zung, rendered in English as the Noble Dhāraṇī Called the Complete Vanquishing of Bandits. It belongs to the dhāraṇī section of the Kangyur, a class of texts preserving sacred incantations and ritual formulas endowed with protective and transformative power. The text recounts an episode in which the Buddha's attendant Ānanda, traveling with the Blessed One toward Vulture Peak, is overcome with fear upon sighting a band of bandits, prompting the Buddha to teach a dhāraṇī capable of averting such dangers. In the language of India: Āryacauravid hvaṃsana nāma dhāraṇī. In the language of Tibet: The Noble Dhāraṇī Called the Complete Vanquishing of Bandits.

Homage to all buddhas and bodhisattvas.

Thus did I hear. At one time, the Blessed One (the Buddha) was traveling together with the venerable Ānanda to Gṛddhrakūṭa — the Vulture Peak — when the venerable Ānanda caught sight of a large band of bandits from quite a distance. Upon seeing them he was seized with fright, and his hair stood on end. He addressed the Blessed One in these words: "Blessed One, there are bandits! Sugata, there are bandits!"

The Blessed One declared, "Ānanda, are you afraid of those bandits?"

He replied, "Blessed One, I am afraid. Sugata, I am afraid."

The Blessed One declared, "Ānanda, do not be afraid of those bandits."

Then, at that moment, the Blessed One proclaimed this, which utterly vanquishes the great host of delusion:

Thus: anulome, pratilome, anuloma, pratiloma, atiṃke, pratiṃke, paṃke, atipaṃke, gauri, gandhāri, caṇḍāli, mātaṃgi, pukasi, rimiri miri, ṛddhimaci, biṣṭhati, caurapradandu, biṣṭhahantu, bhamantu, māmiyantu, yā bdagranthi, namucyami, svāhā.

Ānanda, I have bound the feet of all bandits within a distance of ten leagues, and of all those who dwell in mountain passes, all the people of hidden villages, all those who guard concealed paths, and all those who harbor malicious intent. I have stupefied them. I have bewildered them. Just as a potter's wheel is turned — just as a potter's wheel is spun all the way around — so may those bandits tremble! May they be bewildered! May their minds be confounded! And may they not be able to advance so long as this knot remains untied!

When the Blessed One had spoken these words, the venerable Ānanda was overjoyed and praised what the Blessed One had said.

As for the ritual procedure: one should mix together wood chips and donkey hair, twist them into a thread spun by a pure girl, and recite this dhāraṇī twenty-one times over it. Or one may tie a knot in the hem of one's robe, and upon arriving safely at one's destination, untie it. This is the accomplishment.

The Noble Dhāraṇī Called the Complete Vanquishing of Bandits is complete.
