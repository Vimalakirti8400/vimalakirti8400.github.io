The Eight Charnel Grounds (Aṣṭaśmaśāna)

The Eight Charnel Grounds, known in Sanskrit as Aṣṭaśmaśāna and in Tibetan as Dur khrod brgyad, is a short tantric work that opens with homage to Hevajra and sets out the features of the eight charnel grounds that surround a deity's mandala. For each direction, beginning in the east, it lists the field's sacred tree, the name of the charnel ground, the world protector who guards it, the nāga king who dwells there, and the rain cloud with its characteristic color, such as Indra, the nāga king Mahāpadma, and a saffron cloud called Ghora in the east. These correspondences give practitioners a structured way to picture the charnel grounds during visualization. The text is Toh 1212 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the collection of tantric commentarial and ritual literature associated with the Hevajra cycle.

In the language of India: aṣṭaṣmaśāna nāma. In the language of Tibet: The Eight Charnel Grounds.

Homage to Hevajra.

The eight fields and the rest, that is, the eight world protectors, the eight families of nāga kings, the deities who are lords of the fields, the trees, and the rain clouds, should be viewed in their proper order.

First, in the east, there are the nāgapuṣpa tree, Aṭṭahāsa, Indra, the nāga king Mahāpadma, and the rain cloud called Ghora, which is saffron in color.

In the south there are the mango tree, Caritra, Yama, the nāga king Kulika, and the rain cloud called Caṇḍa, which is blue.

In the west there are the aśvattha tree, Kolagiri, Varuṇa, the nāga king Karkoṭaka, and the rain cloud called Ghana, which is white in color.

In the north there are the punnāgara tree, Jayantī, Kubera, the nāga king Śaṅkhapāla, and the rain cloud called Āvarta, which is yellow in color.

In the northeast, the quarter of Īśāna, there are the nyagrodha tree, Ujjayinī, Rudra, the nāga king Takṣaka, and the rain cloud called Garjita, which is black in color.

In the southeast, the quarter of Agni, there are the karañja tree, Prayāga, Agni, the nāga king called Padma, and the rain cloud called Gharmita, which is of many colors.

In the southwest, the quarter of Nairṛti, there are the latājāta tree, the place called Twisted Ear, a rākṣasa, the nāga king Ananta, and the rain cloud called Varṣaṇa, which is black.

In the northwest, the quarter of Vāyu, there are the udumbara tree, Devīkoṭa, Vāyu, the nāga king who is the son of Vāsuki, and the rain cloud called Pūraṇa, which is white and black in color.

The fields, beginning with these, should be understood in their proper order.

Translated by the Indian preceptor Ratnaśrīmitra and the monk Shākya Yeshé.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The southwestern site, Tibetan "rna 'chus sa ma," could not be confidently identified with a Sanskrit place name, so it has been translated literally as "Twisted Ear." The tree in the southwest is given as the Tibetan transliteration suggests, latājāta. Its identity is uncertain. The northern tree is transliterated "punnāgara," probably the punnāga tree. In the northwest the Tibetan reads "the nāga king, son of Vāsuki." Standard lists of the eight nāga kings have Vāsuki himself, so "son of" may reflect a misreading of the Sanskrit.
