The Noble Dhāraṇī Called "The Distinguished" (Ārya Viśiṣṭavatī Nāma Dhāraṇī)

The Noble Dhāraṇī Called "The Distinguished" (Sanskrit: Ārya Viśiṣṭavatī Nāma Dhāraṇī; Tibetan: Phagpa Khyepar Chen zhé jawé Zung) is a short scriptural text of the dhāraṇī type, a genre built around protective or empowering formulas of sacred speech. Homage to all the buddhas and bodhisattvas.

Thus did I hear at one time. In the year of the snake, on the twentieth day of the first winter month, the Blessed One was staying in a temple in the town called Sañco, in the Land of Doves. At that time a monk named Tsejö went on some business to a town called Nesong. When that monk had travelled a road of a hundred leagues from a city in China, he saw there, from a great distance, a man of enormous body, thirty spans tall, with a face four spans across.

On seeing him, the monk Tsejö thought this about such a man: "Ah, this man's body is huge! He must certainly be Mañjuśrī, the Youthful." Thinking this, he set both kneecaps on the ground, joined his palms, and bowed toward where the man of enormous body stood. Then he spoke these words to him: "Are you Mañjuśrī, the Youthful? Are you here?"

Mañjuśrī said, "Monk, it is so."

The monk said, "Mañjuśrī, why are you here?"

Mañjuśrī, the Youthful, said: "Monk, here in Jambudvīpa many kinds of disease are arising among sentient beings. Yakṣas, asuras, garuḍas, and gandharvas are dwelling on one face of Mount Meru, king of mountains. Because of this the light of the sun and moon cannot be seen; the sun, the moon, the planets, and the constellations do not appear in the expanse of the sky. At that time the gods and the asuras also wage war, and when the battle is joined, the gods are defeated and the asuras are victorious.

"At that time bhūtas and hungry ghosts, taking the form of women, send diseases upon sentient beings. They bring about swellings of the throat, pox, erysipelas, stomach pains, fevers that recur every day, every second day, every third day, and every fourth day, disorders arising from wind, from bile, from phlegm, and from their combination, headaches, blisters, itching, fevers, eye diseases, and the like. With heavy rains, drought, and untimely rains they rob the abundant harvest that is to come. They rob sentient beings of their vitality. They rob them of life, strength, complexion, and form. At that time all sentient beings in Jambudvīpa are thoroughly afflicted by various diseases.

"In the fierce forms of lions, wolves, tigers, snakes, and the like, they rob sentient beings of their vitality. Because they stand ready to bring untimely ruin upon the beings of Jambudvīpa, it is hard to free beings from disease. Here in Jambudvīpa, among old, middle-aged, and young beings, thirty vast multitudes are going to die.

"For this reason I have come to you, monk. You must proclaim this Dharma discourse to all the sentient beings of Jambudvīpa. Why is that? Because all the sentient beings of Jambudvīpa hope, thinking, 'How may we be freed from all the bonds of suffering?'

"Therefore, monk, proclaim this sūtra to all sentient beings, and be earnest in it so that wholesome roots may grow in all sentient beings. Have constant, unbroken faith in the Three Jewels. Keep the deities in mind. Make offerings of fragrances, flowers, and incense to the stūpas and images of the Tathāgatas. Recite also the Dhāraṇī of the Distinguished. For seven days, keep the restoration observance with its eight branches.

tadyathā candraprabhe suprabhe prabhākare prabhodgate prabhodgate prabhavati prabhavati dharmaviśuddhe karmaviśuddhir bhavatu me svāhā.

Proclaimer of Life, suppose someone recites this vidyā-mantra for seven days, seven times each day and seven times each night. All of their karmic obscurations will then be completely purified, without exception. They should give food to seven monks. They should kindle flame from flame. At night, carrying lamps, they should respectfully circumambulate a stūpa. They should apply themselves to copying and reading the sacred Dharma.

If a great epidemic breaks out, offerings should be made from house to house, from village to village, and from town to town. They should be made with gandharasa incense, tormas, and food offerings for the gods. If whoever is sick also performs the rite in this way, they will quickly be freed from the illness.

Then Mañjuśrī, the Youthful, spoke further to the monk Proclaimer of Life: "Monk, a son or daughter of good family may uphold this Dharma discourse, read it, write it down, have it written down, or also teach it correctly and extensively to others. Whoever does so will become irreversible from unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening."

Again Mañjuśrī, the Youthful, spoke to the monk Proclaimer of Life: "This Dharma discourse should be sent from land to land, from town to town, and from village to village, so that it resounds in the ears of all people."

Mañjuśrī, the Youthful, spoke to the monk Proclaimer of Life: "Proclaimer of Life, if any son or daughter of good family reads this Dharma discourse once, they themselves will be liberated. Whoever reads it twice will be liberated together with their children and spouse. Whoever reads it three times will be liberated together with their household dependents. Whoever reads it four times, the whole surrounding country will also be liberated. Whoever reads it five times will be liberated together with the neighboring kings."

Again Mañjuśrī, the Youthful, spoke to the monk Proclaimer of Life: "This Dharma discourse should quickly be sent to other lands, so that these same benefits will arise there as well.

"Proclaimer of Life, some may hear this Dharma discourse and yet fail to act on it. They may not uphold it, read it, write it down, or have it written down. They may not master it, or teach it correctly and extensively to others. They may not send it out to other lands, or listen to it in common with all sentient beings. They may not fully dedicate it to unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening. Whoever fails in this way will be reborn into a destiny just like that of those who commit the five acts of immediate retribution, and will be reproached by all the buddhas and bodhisattvas."

Again Mañjuśrī, the Youthful, spoke to the monk Proclaimer of Life: "Proclaimer of Life, whoever does not take this as authoritative is not one in whom I first instilled faith. Now, so that all the sentient beings of Jambudvīpa may be happy, I shall go to Mount Meru, the king of mountains. There I shall strive to bring the gods and gandharvas into accord with the teaching."

Having spoken thus, Mañjuśrī, the Youthful, vanished from that very spot. The monk Proclaimer of Life rejoiced in what Mañjuśrī, the Youthful, had said, took it to heart, and departed.

This completes the noble dhāraṇī called The Distinguished.

This text appears to be of Chinese origin. The Tibetan names a city "in China" and dates the event by the Chinese zodiac. The place and personal names do not clearly correspond to any Indian names. "The Land of Doves" translates the Tibetan phug ron can literally. "Sañco," "Nesong," and the monk's name "Tsejö" are given as transliterations, since their originals are uncertain. The numerical unit dkrigs phrag, rendered here as "thirty vast multitudes," denotes an enormous number whose exact value is unclear. The phrase lha rjed cig literally reads "forget the deities." It has been rendered "keep the deities in mind," on the assumption that a negation was lost or that a verb such as "remember" was intended. The mantras are given in Romanized Sanskrit as transmitted, and their spellings are often irregular. The Tibetan reads "candra na garbhe" and "candra na cūrṇe"; these are given here as candanagarbhe and candanacūrṇe.  The monk's name, Tshe brjod, could not be securely identified with a Sanskrit form, so it is rendered literally as "Proclaimer of Life." The name list for this text shows a sound-spelling, "Tsejö," for this monk. If that form was used in the first piece, the two pieces should be harmonized. The literal rendering is kept here because house rules discourage Tibetan sound-spellings for names. The verb byab pa in the recitation instruction is taken as "recite," in line with the parallel ritual context. Mantras are transliterated as given in the Tibetan, with minimal normalization.
