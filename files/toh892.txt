The Noble Dhāraṇī Called "Spoken by Mañjuśrī" (Āryamañjuśrīsvākhyātonāmadhāraṇī)

The Dhāraṇī Called "Spoken by Noble Mañjuśrī," known in Sanskrit as the Ārya Mañjuśrī Svākhyāta Nāma Dhāraṇī and in Tibetan as Phakpa Jampal gyi Zhal ne Sungpa zhe Jawé Zung, is a short incantation text devoted to the bodhisattva of wisdom. After paying homage to the Three Jewels and to Mañjuśrī, it presents a mantra that invokes the light of wisdom, asks for the protection of the vajra-like mind of awakening, and calls for the cutting off of the afflictions that bind all beings, with gods, nāgas, and other spirits invoked as well. The text closes by describing the benefits of recitation, saying that even reciting it a single time purifies karmic obscurations accumulated over a vast span of time. It is Toh 892 in the Kangyur, where it belongs to the class of dhāraṇī texts, the collected incantations valued for protection, purification, and spiritual blessing.

In the language of India: ārya mañjuśrī svākhyāto nāma dhāraṇī
In the language of Tibet: The Dhāraṇī Called "Spoken by Noble Mañjuśrī."

Homage to the Three Jewels!
Homage to noble Mañjuśrī!

namo ratna trayāya namo bhagavate dharmadhātu nigarjita rājāya tathāgatāya arhate samyaksaṃ buddhāya namo mañjuśriye kumāra bhūtāya tadyathā oṃ jñānāloke triratna paṃśa saṃdhāraṇi bhagavati abāyaṃ dādi abāyaṃ datte me bodhicitta vajra paripālaya samanta ababhāsena hana hana sarba satva saṃtana patitaṃ kleśānucchedaya bara lakṣaṇa alaṃkṛta śarīri bodhisatva śiśūpati pālaya buddhāna deśāya deba nāga yakṣa ganadharba asura garuḍa kinnara mahoraga baśesthapāya apayaṃ dadti svāhā

The benefit of this dhāraṇī is this: even by reciting it a single time, the obscurations of karma accumulated over a thousand eons will be purified. At the time of death, one will see noble Mañjuśrī. One will also see the form of noble Avalokiteśvara, his head adorned with a topknot. In short, the benefits of this dhāraṇī are limitless.

Here ends the dhāraṇī called "Spoken by Noble Mañjuśrī."

It was translated by the Indian preceptor Jñānagarbha and the translator, the monk Lui Wangpo. It was revised and finalized by the Indian preceptor Viśuddhasiddha and the chief editor-translator, the monk Devacandra.
