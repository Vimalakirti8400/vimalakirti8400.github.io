The Noble Great Cloud, the Chapter of the Wind Maṇḍala, called The Heart of All Nāgas, known in Sanskrit as the Ārya Mahāmegha Vātamaṇḍala Sarvanāgahṛdaya Nāma Mahāyāna Sūtra and in Tibetan as Pakpa Trin Chenpo Lung Gi Kyilkhor Gyi Le'u Lu Tamche Kyi Nyingpo, is a Great Vehicle sūtra centered on the nāgas, the serpent-like beings believed to govern waters and weather. It opens with the Buddha dwelling in a jewel-crowned palace in the great ocean, surrounded by a thousand mighty nāga kings such as Nanda and Upananda. In keeping with the wider Great Cloud literature, it is concerned with the nāgas' role in bringing rain and with practices meant to secure their beneficial influence. It is Toh 1064 in the Kangyur, where it belongs among the texts associated with dhāraṇī and ritual practice.

Then, having recited the mantra seven times over the corner of his Dharma robe and tied seven knots in it, he should recite this chapter of the wind maṇḍala called the Essence of All Nāgas without interruption for twenty-one days.

The canopy, the cloth, the banners, and all the food offerings as well should be made blue. The offering set down for the nāgas should be made with the three sweet substances. Seven ladlefuls of the three sweet substances should also be offered in a fire offering with the nāga heart mantra.

The cloud kings too should be drawn, sending down streams of rain, meeting one another, with lightning flickering at their edges. As offerings here, one should set out in abundance auspicious fried cakes, pentse, meat, fish, and choice foods made with honey.

Then the Dharma preacher, clean and wearing clean clothes, should recite the chapter of the wind maṇḍala, the Essence of All Nāgas. Then, beginning from the very first day, the nāgas will rumble and roar and will give forth pleasing sounds. One should not doubt or disparage this chapter. This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

Many of the nāga kings' names could not be securely matched to Sanskrit forms, so they have been translated by meaning (for example Fully Urging, Releasing and Holding, Fine Boundary, Peacock Eye). Only well-established equivalents are given in Sanskrit. ལ་བ is taken as Kambala, since ལ་བ means "woolen blanket" (Skt. kambala). སོག་མ་མེད ("without chaff," Skt. palāla "straw") is the standard Tibetan rendering of the nāga king Apalāla. ཆར་འབྱུང (Source of Rain) occurs twice in the list, as it does in the Tibetan. The phrase འདུ་ཞིང་ཀུན་ཏུ་འདུལ་ཏོག་ཡར་དར་དིར་ཞིང་འདུག is obscure and partly onomatopoeic. It has been rendered loosely as "gather together and stay, rumbling and roaring on every side."  Several nāga king names could not be identified with confidence and are translated by meaning: Peacock Eye, Glorious Splendor, Glorious Throat, Son of the Earth Protector, Garland Bearer, Terror Causer and Great Destroyer. The names of the tathāgatas in the long list are likewise rendered by meaning, following the practice of the earlier pieces. The doer of the repeated "urge" is unstated in the Tibetan. It is given as "I", the reciter of the dhāraṇī. "The Essence of All Nāgas" is the dhāraṇī named in the text's title.  I translated the names of the nāga kings (Three-Headed, Great Expander, Radiant Crested One, Cloud Body) and of the buddhas in the homage list from their Tibetan meanings, because I could not confidently identify their Sanskrit forms. "Cloud Body" is especially uncertain, since the Tibetan here, sprin sku la, could also hide a transcription.

"The offering set down for the nāgas" renders a phrase that literally means "laying down at the nāgas' vital point." Its sense is obscure.

"Pentse" is an unidentified foodstuff and is left as it stands.

In the closing benedictions, 'byung po is taken in its broad sense of "beings" (Skt. bhūta, as in the common formula wishing loving-kindness to all bhūtas), not as a specific class of spirits. This line is not a list of spirits.

The final homage is taken as addressed to the bodhisattva Sarvanīvaraṇaviṣkambhin, whose Tibetan name exactly matches the phrase.  "Pellets of incense resin" renders པོག་པོར, which most likely means an aromatic resin incense such as guggula, made into small balls or lumps. Its exact substance is uncertain. Here མཚན means the "names" of the tathāgatas listed earlier in the sūtra, not their major marks.
