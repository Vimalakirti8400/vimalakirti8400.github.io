The Noble Great Vehicle Sūtra called The Samādhi in Which the Buddhas of the Present Stand Directly Before One (Ārya-pratyutpanna-buddha-saṃmukhāvasthita-samādhi-nāma-mahāyāna-sūtra)

The Noble Great Vehicle Sūtra called The Samādhi in Which the Buddhas of the Present Stand Directly Before One, known in Sanskrit as the Pratyutpannabuddhasaṃmukhāvasthitasamādhi Sūtra and in Tibetan as Phakpa Datar Gyi Sangye Ngönsum Du Zhukpé Tingngé Dzin, is a Mahāyāna discourse on a meditative absorption through which a practitioner comes to behold the buddhas of the present age as if they were standing right before them. Set at Rājagṛha in the Bamboo Grove at the Kalandaka Feeding Place, where the Buddha dwells with a great assembly of arhat monks, the sūtra explains how this samādhi is cultivated, the qualities and conduct that support it, and the insight into emptiness that accompanies such visionary encounters. It is regarded as one of the earliest Mahāyāna scriptures and is especially remembered for its teaching on visualizing the Buddha Amitābha, which made it influential in later devotional and meditative traditions. In the Tibetan canon it is Toh 133 in the Kangyur, where it belongs among the general sūtras of the Great Vehicle.

In the language of India: aryā pratyudpanne buddha samamukha avasthita samādhi nāma mahāyāna sūtra

In the language of Tibet: The Noble Great Vehicle Sūtra called The Samādhi in Which the Buddhas of the Present Stand Directly Before One.

The first section.

Homage to all the buddhas and bodhisattvas.

Thus did I hear at one time. The Blessed One (the Buddha) was dwelling at Rājagṛha, in the Bamboo Grove at the Kalandaka Feeding Place, together with a great Saṅgha of about five hundred monks. All of them were arhats. Their defilements were exhausted and they were free of afflictions. They had attained mastery. They had done what was to be done and accomplished their task. They had laid down the burden and attained their own aim. The fetters binding them to existence were completely exhausted. Their minds were thoroughly liberated by perfect knowledge. They had gone to the supreme far shore of every mastery of mind, and every one of them had attained the eight liberations. The one exception was a single person, the venerable Ānanda.

At that time the bodhisattva, the great being called Bhadrapāla, came out from the great city of Rājagṛha together with about five hundred bodhisattvas. All of them were householders, and every one of them had taken up the five bases of training. They came to attend upon the Blessed One in order to listen to the Dharma.

Then, in the evening, the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, rose from meditative seclusion and went to where the Blessed One was. Arriving there, he bowed his head at the Blessed One's feet, circumambulated him seven times, and sat down to one side before the Blessed One.

What is the samādhi by which bodhisattvas see the buddha fields of the buddhas, the blessed ones, who dwell in other world systems, hear their Dharma, retain it and do not forget it? They do so without having the five superknowledges, and without having the six superknowledges. Those bodhisattvas do not die and pass from this world system to dwell in those buddha fields. Rather, while remaining in this very world system, those bodhisattvas see the buddhas, hear the Dharma, and understand all of it completely. Just as I now, being in the presence of the Blessed One, am without doubt or hesitation, in the same way those bodhisattvas, even in their dreams, see the buddhas and are never parted from hearing the Dharma. When he had made this request, the Blessed One spoke to the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, saying: "Bhadrapāla, excellent, excellent! Your sphere of activity is profound. You set your mind on omniscience. You properly take up awakening. You reveal the lineage of the Tathāgata. "Bhadrapāla, there is a samādhi called Direct Presence of the Buddhas of the Present. If one takes up that samādhi and does not forget it, listens with attentive ears, and attains an undistracted mind, those special qualities will not be difficult to attain."

Then the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, said to the Blessed One: "Venerable Blessed One, therefore I request the Tathāgata to explain that samādhi. It will bring benefit to many people, happiness to many people, compassion to the world, and welfare, benefit and happiness to the great multitude of people, to gods and humans. Then the Blessed One spoke to the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, saying: "Bhadrapāla, in that case, listen well and thoroughly and keep it in mind, and I shall explain it to you."

"So be it, Blessed One," he replied. And as the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, and the world with its gods, humans, asuras and gandharvas listened to the Blessed One, the Blessed One said to them:

"Bhadrapāla, if one relies upon a single dharma, relies upon it with certainty, cultivates it and practices it many times, makes it one's path, makes it one's foundation, properly arouses it, trains in it well, thoroughly purifies it, settles in it in perfect equipoise, and thoroughly and properly undertakes it, then all one's qualities will become eminent. What is that single dharma? It is the samādhi called Direct Presence of the Buddhas of the Present. Through it, the qualities that have much learning as their forerunner will be brought to completion."

Bhadrapāla, these qualities will give rise to that samādhi. And what, Bhadrapāla, is the samādhi to which these qualities give rise? It is the samādhi called Direct Encounter with the Buddhas of the Present. And what, Bhadrapāla, is this samādhi called Direct Encounter with the Buddhas of the Present? Bhadrapāla, suppose a monk, a nun, a layman or a laywoman keeps moral discipline perfectly. Going alone to a solitary place and sitting there, that person should give rise to this thought: "In which direction does the Blessed One, the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha Amitāyus dwell, living and sustaining himself, and teaching the Dharma?" Then, in the way that he has heard it, he brings to mind: "Westward from this buddha field, past a hundred thousand koṭis of buddha fields, in the world realm Sukhāvatī, the Blessed One, the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha Amitāyus now dwells, surrounded and attended by a host of bodhisattvas. He lives, he sustains himself, and he teaches the Dharma." And with an undistracted mind he brings that Tathāgata to mind.

Bhadrapāla, it is like this. Suppose some man or woman, while asleep, sees forms in a dream. They see silver, or gold, or friends, or relatives, or blood kin, or companions, people who are pleasing, dear and not at all disagreeable. They dream that in the dream they play with them, rejoice, enjoy themselves to the full, and talk and converse with them. When they wake, they tell others everything just as they saw it, heard it, discerned it, cognized it, and spoke and conversed, and as they recall the images of the dream, they shed tears. In the same way, Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva, whether a householder or one who has gone forth, goes alone to a solitary place and sits. Bringing to mind the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha Amitāyus in the way that he has heard, with no flaw in his body of moral discipline and with undistracted mindfulness, he should keep him in mind for one day and night, or two, or three, or four, or five, or six, or for seven days and nights. If for seven days and nights he keeps the Tathāgata Amitāyus in mind without distraction, then when the full seven days and nights have passed, he will see the Blessed One, the Tathāgata Amitāyus. If he does not see the Blessed One during the day, then in a dream while he sleeps the Blessed One, the Tathāgata Amitāyus, will show him his face.

Bhadrapāla, it is like this. A woman or a man, in a dream while asleep, does not know whether they are in a house, nor know whether they are out in the open, and their eyes are not obstructed by walls, nor dimmed by darkness or haze. In the same way, Bhadrapāla, for that bodhisattva great being too, the spaces between world realms do not obstruct him; Sumeru, king of mountains, the Cakravāla, king of mountains, the Great Cakravāla, and likewise the other black mountains do not obstruct his eyes. Giving rise to his thought in just this way, that bodhisattva does not see the Tathāgata by having attained the divine eye; he does not hear the true Dharma by having attained the divine ear; he does not go to that world realm in an instant by having attained the power of miraculous abilities. Rather, Bhadrapāla, while remaining in this very world realm, that bodhisattva sees the Blessed One, the Tathāgata Amitāyus, and knows himself as though he were present in that world realm, and he also hears the Dharma. Having heard it taught, he retains all those teachings, masters them and holds them. He honors the Blessed One, the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha Amitāyus, esteems him as supreme, reveres him and makes offerings to him. And when that bodhisattva has arisen from that samādhi, he explains to others too, extensively and correctly, those teachings just as he heard them, just as he held them and just as he mastered them.

Fascicle two.

Bhadrapāla, it is like this. Suppose a man living in the great city of Rājagṛha heard that in the city of Vaiśālī there was a courtesan called Sumanā. A second man heard that there was a courtesan called Āmrapālī. A third man heard that there had once been a courtesan called Utpalavarṇā. Having heard of them, each of them became attached to one of them; yet those men did not see those courtesans. Merely by hearing of their names, their figures and their complexions, they gave rise to thoughts of desire. As they lay down to sleep while thinking of them again and again, they felt in their dreams as though they had gone into the presence of those courtesans. Just as those men, staying in the great city of Rājagṛha, had given rise to such thoughts of desire before they slept, so too, once asleep, those men dreamed that they saw those courtesans in their dreams, came together with them, engaged in intercourse, and were then freed from that craving for intercourse. When they woke, they recalled what they had experienced in the dream, just as they had seen, heard, known and understood it, and they went to where you were, Bhadrapāla, and told you about these things. Having heard about these things, you used this very teaching to teach them the Dharma in such a way that they would by all means become irreversible from unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening. And I prophesy that in a future time those men will become tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly complete buddhas, each called Suprabuddha.

Even though those persons have attained acceptance, they still see and recollect those former signs.

Bhadrapāla, in the same way, the bodhisattva who possesses this samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present, while still remaining in this very world-system, hears of the Blessed One, the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha Amitāyus. Having heard no more than the name, the form and the qualities of that Tathāgata, he recollects that Blessed One, the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha Amitāyus correctly, with undistracted mind. By attending to him again and again, he sees that Tathāgata.

Abiding in the bodhisattva's samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present, having seen that Tathāgata, he puts this question: "Blessed One, possessing what qualities are bodhisattva great beings born in this world-system?" Likewise, whatever buddhafield he wishes to be born in, he then asks that Tathāgata about it. When he has asked this, the Blessed One, the Tathāgata Amitāyus, says to that bodhisattva: "Son of good family, if one practices, fully practices, cultivates and makes much of the recollection of the Buddha, one is born in this world-system. If one practices, fully practices, cultivates and makes much of the recollection of the Buddha, one will be born in this buddhafield.

"Son of good family, what then is this recollection of the Buddha? It is attending to the Tathāgata in this way: he is the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha, endowed with knowledge and conduct, the Sugata, the knower of the world, the charioteer who tames persons, the unsurpassed one, the teacher of gods and humans, the Buddha, the Blessed One. He possesses the thirty-two marks of a great person and a body the color of gold. Like a golden image, shining, radiant and well established, he is beautifully adorned like a jeweled offering pillar. In the midst of the Saṅgha of śrāvakas he teaches the Dharma, and he teaches it in such a way that nothing is lost.

"If one asks what is not lost: he does not lose earth. He does not lose water, fire, wind, bhūtas, gods, brahmins or Prajāpati. He does not lose form. He does not lose feeling, perception, formations or consciousness.

"He does not take that Tathāgata as an object of conceit. He does not apprehend him, does not cling to him, does not fully know him, does not conceive of him, does not discriminate him, and does not fully see him. When, attending to the Tathāgata in this way without apprehending him, one attains the samādhi of emptiness, that is called the recollection of the Buddha."

That bodhisattva cultivates that samādhi, settles evenly in that samādhi, and rises from that samādhi. Then, Bhadrapāla, he goes to where you are, and when he has arrived he speaks of this samādhi. Then, Bhadrapāla, you teach him the Dharma in just such a way that the bodhisattva becomes irreversible from unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. And I too prophesy of that person: "In the future he will become the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha called Pratibhānaprāpta."

Bhadrapāla, you yourself, the elder Mahākāśyapa, the bodhisattva Indradatta, the bodhisattva Susīma, and any other bodhisattvas who have attained this samādhi have gained mastery over this samādhi.

Bhadrapāla, long ago in the past, a certain man went into a wilderness desert. Hungry and thirsty, he was overcome by torpor and sleep, and he lay down. In a dream he found plenty of food and drink. Having found it, he ate as much as he wanted, and his hunger and thirst disappeared. When he woke up, his body and belly were full. He thought, "There is indeed such a thing as this, which is like a dream." By understanding it in this way, he attained acceptance of the unarisen nature of dharmas, and he also became irreversible from unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening.

Bhadrapāla, in the same way, a bodhisattva, whether a householder or one who has gone forth, who hears that a Tathāgata dwells in this or that direction, and who attends to that Tathāgata facing that direction, will attain the vision of the Buddha. He should not form the notion of a thing; he should form the notion of space. Firmly abiding in the notion of space and attending closely to the notion of the Buddha, that bodhisattva will see the Tathāgata directly, like a fine image of beryl. That bodhisattva will see the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha in just this way.

Bhadrapāla, it is like this. Suppose a certain man leaves the land of his birth and goes to another region. When he has arrived there, he recollects his native land, and as he attends to the things he saw, heard, knew and understood there, he is overcome by torpor and sleep. He lies down, and in a dream he goes to his native land and believes that he is there himself. He sees again those things he had seen, heard, distinguished and cognized before. There he goes about and comes back. When he wakes up, he recounts these matters among his kinsmen, friends, relatives and blood relations: "I went to such and such places. I saw these things. There I experienced these things."

Bhadrapāla, in the same way, a bodhisattva great being, whether a householder or one who has gone forth, who hears that a Tathāgata dwells in this or that direction, should attend to that Tathāgata facing that direction, mindful and with undistracted mind, in order to attain the vision of the Buddha. In this way that bodhisattva will see the Tathāgata abiding as a fine image of beryl.

"Bhadrapāla, it is like this. Suppose a monk who cultivates the meditation on the unattractive sees bloated forms before him. He sees before him corpses that are bluish, festering, reddened, gnawed by animals, with the flesh stripped away, without flesh and blood, white, the color of a conch shell, or a skeleton. From the bluish corpse up to the skeleton, these have not come from anywhere, and they have not gone anywhere. No one made them, and no one stopped them. Nevertheless, Bhadrapāla, through the power of that monk's one-pointed mind, he sees a skeleton lying before him.

"In the same way, Bhadrapāla, bodhisattvas who are upheld by the buddhas and who abide in this samādhi turn their minds toward whatever direction a tathāgata, an arhat, a perfectly complete buddha is dwelling in. They do this, toward each of those directions, in order to gain the vision of the Buddha. Because they have turned their minds toward each of those directions, they see those tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly complete buddhas in each of them. Why is that? Bhadrapāla, this gaining of the vision of the buddhas is the natural outcome of this samādhi. Three things come together and combine for a bodhisattva who abides in this samādhi: the might of the Buddha, the strength of his own roots of virtue that he has generated, and his having attained the samādhi. Because of this, he sees the tathāgatas, and they appear to him.

"Bhadrapāla, for example, suppose there is some woman or man who likes to wash their hair and adorn themselves. Wishing to look at themselves, they gaze into a vessel of clear oil, or a vessel of clear water, or a well-polished mirror, or a patch of ground smeared with blue pigment, and there they see their own form. Bhadrapāla, what do you think of that? When the form of the man or woman appears in the vessel of clear oil, the vessel of clear water, the well-polished mirror, or the ground smeared with blue pigment, has that man or woman gone into it, or entered into it?"

Bhadrapāla replied, "Venerable Blessed One, that is not so. Rather, Venerable Blessed One, the reflection appears before them because the oil and the water are clear and free of murk, because the mirror is well polished, and because the great ground smeared with blue pigment is clean. The man's or woman's body has not emerged from the water, the oil, the mirror, or the great ground. It has not come from anywhere, it has not gone anywhere, it has not arisen from anywhere, and it has not ceased anywhere."

The Blessed One said, "Excellent, Bhadrapāla, excellent! You have done well, Bhadrapāla. So it is, Bhadrapāla. It is just as you have said: when forms are thoroughly pure, reflections appear. In the same way, because that bodhisattva has cultivated this samādhi well, he sees those tathāgatas with little difficulty. Having seen them, he asks them questions, and he rejoices when his questions are answered.

"He thinks, 'Where have these tathāgatas come from? Where have I gone to?' He then understands clearly that the tathāgata has not come from anywhere. Recognizing that his own body has not gone anywhere either, he thinks: 'These three realms are mind only. Why is that? Because however I conceive of things, that is how they appear. That mind is not inside, and it is not outside, nor is it found apart from both. Rather, it arises in dependence on an object. Whatever arises dependently is without real entity. Whatever is without real entity is unborn. Whatever is unborn cannot be apprehended. Whatever cannot be apprehended is empty of intrinsic nature. Whatever is empty of intrinsic nature cannot be designated. Whatever cannot be designated cannot be seen, cognized, clung to, shown, destroyed, or established.'"

Then at that time the Blessed One spoke these verses:

"The mind creates the Buddha;
By mind itself, too, he is seen.
Mind itself is my buddhahood;
Mind itself is the Tathāgata.
Mind itself is my body;
The Buddha is seen by the mind.
Mind itself is my awakening;
Mind itself is without intrinsic nature.
Mind does not know the mind,
And mind does not see the mind.
To perceive it as mind is ignorance;
Not to know it as mind is nirvāṇa.
These phenomena have no core;
They arise from conceiving thought.
Whatever conceit there is about emptiness,
That conceiving thought is here empty."

This concludes the third chapter, on the samādhi requested by Bhadrapāla.

"Bhadrapāla, such is the wisdom of the bodhisattva who abides in this samādhi. Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva great being who possesses four qualities attains this samādhi in which the buddhas of the present stand directly before one. What are the four? They are faith that cannot be stolen away, diligence that never turns back, wisdom that does not depend on others, and attending upon and honoring spiritual friends. Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva great being who possesses these four qualities attains this bodhisattva samādhi in which the buddhas of the present stand directly before one.

"Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva great being who possesses four other qualities attains this samādhi.

What are the four? They are these. For three months, not giving rise to the perception of a self even for the time of a finger snap. For three months, not being overcome by torpor and sleep even for the time of a finger snap. For three months, except to defecate and urinate, taking up diligence and keeping to walking meditation, never sitting down on the ground. And, while giving the gift of the Dharma extensively to others, not hoping for gain, honor, or renown. Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva great being who has these four qualities will attain this meditative absorption.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva great being who has four other qualities will attain this meditative absorption. What are the four? They are these. Urging sentient beings to see the Buddha, urging sentient beings to hear the Dharma, urging sentient beings to give rise to the awakening mind, and being free of jealousy. Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva great being who has these four qualities will attain this meditative absorption.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva great being who has four other qualities will attain this meditative absorption. What are the four? They are these. Wishing for this meditative absorption, one has images of the Tathāgata made, even if only as paintings. Wishing for this meditative absorption, so that it may long endure, one has it carefully written out by every means, so that it can be upheld, and gives the books away. One establishes people who are full of conceit in the Dharma that is free of conceit, namely unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. And one stands firm to guard, protect and uphold the teaching of the Tathāgata. Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva great being who has these four qualities will attain this meditative absorption.

Then the Blessed One spoke these verses:

Give rise to faith in the teaching of the Sugata;
Do not revile the Dharma taught on emptiness.
Take up diligence and give up sleep,
And for three full months never sit down.

Without relying on gain, honor, or renown,
Explain the Dharma that the Sugata spoke,
And spread his incomparable teaching.
Free of reliance, you will attain this Dharma.

Be free of jealousy and give no rise to anger;
Abandon desire, and with your mind set free,
Rest in equipoise and take delight in dhyāna.
If you apply yourself, you will attain this Dharma.

Golden in hue, bearing marks from a hundred merits,
Beautiful and perfect in every way,
Lovely to behold like a golden image:
Recollect the Buddha, marked by supreme merit.

The buddhas who have passed, or who are yet to come,
Recollect them with your palms joined.
And whatever supreme humans are present now,
To honor them, recollect the Sugatas.

With a clear and faithful mind, with garlands of flowers,
With ointments, and with food and drink,
Make offerings to the Sugata,
And this absorption will not be hard to find.

Wishing for this supreme meditative absorption,
With a delighted mind, with joy beyond compare,
With drums, with conches, clay drums and cymbals,
Make music to the incomparable relics.

Wishing for the supreme meditative absorption,
Have paintings well drawn, complete with the marks,
And make incomparable images,
Golden in color, vast and flawless.

Placing first the Dharma that brings joy,
Pure in moral discipline, seeking learning,
If you abandon every form of laziness,
You will find this absorption before long.

Harbor no malice toward anyone at all.
If you abide in love and abide in compassion,
And keep equanimity toward the pleasures of the senses,
You will find this absorption before long.

Likewise give rise to love for the teacher of the Dharma;
Always hold him in mind as the Teacher himself.
Do not be proud; give up attachment.
Never be miserly in giving the Dharma.
Take up these excellent benefits.
This ground of instruction the Sugata has taught;
This is the teaching of many buddhas;
And this absorption will not be hard to find.

This was the fourth chapter, on the meditative absorption requested by Bhadrapāla.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva great being who wishes for this meditative absorption should be respectful and reverent toward the monk who teaches the Dharma. With a mind that does not seek out faults, he should regard him as the Teacher and apply himself to this meditative absorption. Bhadrapāla, if that bodhisattva relates to that Dharma-teaching monk with a malicious mind, or with a mind of contempt, or without faith, then, Bhadrapāla, it is impossible, there is no chance, that the bodhisattva will attain this meditative absorption. And if he does not regard that Dharma-teaching monk as the Teacher, that too is impossible. Why is that, Bhadrapāla? Because through such disrespect the true Dharma will decline.

Bhadrapāla, it is like this. Suppose a man with good eyesight, on a clear and cloudless night, at midnight, turns his face upward and looks into the sky. He will see there the forms of many stars. In the same way, Bhadrapāla, for a bodhisattva great being who is embraced by the buddhas and abides in this meditative absorption, because the perception of space is very closely present and the perception of the Buddha is thoroughly empowered, then through the power of the buddhas and by having cultivated this meditative absorption, if he looks toward other world systems in the east, with little effort many buddhas will appear to his faculty of sight. With little effort, many hundreds of buddhas, many thousands of buddhas, many hundreds of thousands of buddhas, many koṭis, that is, tens of millions, of buddhas, many hundreds of koṭis of buddhas, many thousands of koṭis of buddhas, many hundreds of thousands of koṭis of buddhas, and many hundreds of thousands of niyutas of koṭis of buddhas will, with little effort, appear to his faculty of sight.

In the same way, the south, the west, the north, the four intermediate directions, the direction below and the direction above should be described in full. When he looks in this way into the ten directions, many buddhas will appear to the eye faculty of that bodhisattva great being with little effort. With little effort, many hundreds of buddhas, many thousands of buddhas, many hundreds of thousands of buddhas, many koṭis of buddhas, many hundreds of koṭis of buddhas, many thousands of koṭis of buddhas, many hundreds of thousands of koṭis of buddhas, and many hundreds of thousands of koṭis of niyutas of buddhas appeared to his eye faculty with little effort.

Bhadrapāla, in this way, the bodhisattva great beings who are upheld by the buddhas and who abide in this bodhisattva samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present will see many buddhas appear to their eye faculty with little effort. With little effort, many hundreds of buddhas, many thousands of buddhas, many hundreds of thousands of buddhas, many koṭis of buddhas, many hundreds of koṭis of buddhas, many thousands of koṭis of buddhas, many hundreds of thousands of koṭis of buddhas, and many hundreds of thousands of koṭis of niyutas of buddhas will appear to their eye faculty with little effort.

Bhadrapāla, it is like this. Because he possesses the buddha eye, the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly awakened Buddha understands everything; the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly awakened Buddha, who sees everything, has the buddha eye. Bhadrapāla, in the same way, the bodhisattva great beings who are upheld by the buddhas and who abide in this samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present will, with little effort, perfect great learning. They will perfect the perfection of generosity, the perfection of moral discipline, the perfection of patience, the perfection of diligence, the perfection of meditative concentration, and the perfection of wisdom. They will perfect moral discipline, samādhi, wisdom, liberation, and the knowledge and vision of liberation. They will perfect all the qualities of a bodhisattva. They will perfect the unsurpassed faculty of wisdom and unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening.

Then, at that time, the Blessed One spoke these verses:

Just as a man with eyes, in the solitary time of night,
Rising in the midnight watch, looks upon the pure sky,
And sees many hundreds of thousands of stars,
And, being mindful, recalls them when he thinks of them even by day,

So a bodhisattva who has gained this samādhi
Will see many hundreds of thousands of heroic buddhas;
And at the time he rises from it, he will remember them,
And will tell the assembly, "The protectors of the world are like this."

Just as my eye, that buddha eye, is pure,
Free of blur and wholly clear, and with it I see the world,
So the eye of the Victor's heirs, the bodhisattvas,
Is this stainless samādhi, and with it they see the Lord of Men.

When they behold the Ten-Powered, Lord of the two-legged, Chief of the world,
The sages never give rise to the perception of a real entity.
Hear the special qualities of the foremost bodhisattvas,
Who have destroyed the poisons, are wholly pure, and have no perception of real entities.

Listen also to those supreme and cooling teachings;
Swiftly bring to mind the blissful teaching of emptiness.
I too, having found the awakening of the Victors,
Shall explain this blissful teaching to beings, to many people.

Just as bodhisattvas, born in the realm of Amitāyus,
See many hundreds of thousands of protectors of the world,
So a bodhisattva who gains this samādhi
Will see many hundreds of thousands of heroic buddhas.

Just as the mindful, excellent monk Ānanda
Firmly retains what he has heard from me and holds it all,
So a bodhisattva who gains this samādhi
Will hear much Dharma and will hold all of it.

Having heard this samādhi, give rise to genuine joy,
Abandon all the various kinds of worldly whispered talk,
Swiftly have faith, and give the gift of Dharma;
Thus you will attain this wholly pure ground of peace.

This concludes the fifth chapter of the Samādhi Requested by Bhadrapāla.

Bhadrapāla, therefore a bodhisattva should give rise to enthusiasm and great diligence in order to attain this samādhi, that is, in order to accomplish it fully. Bhadrapāla, it is like this. Suppose a great ocean-going ship filled with jewels were to cross the great ocean, escaping every danger, and then break apart at the near shore. The people of Jambudvīpa would raise a great clamor, a great outcry and wailing over it, and in great grief they would lament, "We have lost these jewels!"

Bhadrapāla, in the same way, suppose this precious samādhi reaches the ears of any son of good family or daughter of good family, any monk, nun, layman or laywoman, and having heard it, they do not copy it into a book, do not teach it, do not recite it, do not retain it, do not read it, do not explain it, do not meditate on it, and do not earnestly put it into practice. Then, Bhadrapāla, the world with its gods will raise a great clamor, a great outcry and wailing over that.

For when they hear this kind of profound and precious samādhi, which the buddhas have praised, which the buddhas have extolled, which the buddhas have caused others to extol, and through which all the qualities of a buddha are attained, they do not copy it in writing, do not teach it, do not recite it, do not retain it, do not read it, do not explain it, do not meditate on it, and do not earnestly put it into practice. Overcome by pride, they do not understand. One should even lament with great sorrow, saying, "Those beings who do not hold to complete and extensive learning have fallen away entirely from this kind of precious samādhi!"

Bhadrapāla, I declare that those beings who become unwilling to listen to this samādhi, and who do not take up this samādhi, are, Bhadrapāla, of a nature to fall away entirely. Bhadrapāla, see how greatly those beings fall away who, having heard this kind of precious samādhi, do not copy it in writing, do not teach it, do not recite it, do not retain it, do not read it, do not explain it, do not meditate on it, and do not earnestly put it into practice. Bhadrapāla, I declare that for such beings, teachings like these are of no great purpose.

Bhadrapāla, it is like this. Suppose some person were to give a piece of fresh red sandalwood to a man of childish nature and corrupt understanding, and he were to conceive of it as unclean, to conceive of it as filthy. Then a sandalwood merchant of wise nature would say to him, "O man, do not conceive of this fresh sandalwood as unclean! Do not conceive of it as filthy! O man, just smell how delightful its fragrance is! O man, look too at how beautiful its color is!" Yet that man of childish nature and corrupt understanding, seeing the red sandalwood, would not take it himself, because he did not want to smell it, and would shut his eyes, because he did not want to look at it.

Just so, Bhadrapāla, in the future there will be monks, bodhisattvas full of conceit, undeveloped in body, undeveloped in mind, undeveloped in moral discipline, undeveloped in wisdom, of corrupt discipline, who do not take up the precious true Dharma of definitive meaning, who reject the precious true Dharma of definitive meaning, of corrupt wisdom, fallen entirely away from what is excellent, overweeningly proud because they have gained meditative concentration, relying on a person, viewing things in terms of a person, abiding in a person, clinging to the apprehension of phenomena as objects, and frightened by the teaching of emptiness. When they hear this samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present being explained, they will not lend an ear and listen to it, will not have faith in it, will not take it up, will not master it, will not keep it, and will not even read it, let alone explain it extensively to others. Because of this they do not even attain vast loving-kindness and joy, let alone practice in accordance with suchness; and on top of that, having heard it, they reject it. They have no faith in it. They do not accept it. They take no delight in it. With a mocking attitude, a scornful attitude, and a contemptuous attitude, they even say, "How these scriptures shine! How marvelous that someone like the monk Ānanda appears in the world, and that a sūtra like this appears!" And going off in secret, they say to one another, "Sūtras like these are self-made, fabricated. This was not spoken by the Buddha; it was not sanctioned by the Buddha." Saying such things, they revile it. Such foolish people should be known to have fallen entirely away from the precious true Dharma and to have rejected the precious true Dharma.

Bhadrapāla, it is like this. Just as that man of childish nature, seeing the red sandalwood, blocks his nose so as not to smell it and shuts his eyes so as not to see it, just so, Bhadrapāla, when those foolish people hear this kind of precious samādhi, they do not think to listen to it again and again, nor do they think to take it up, to master it, to keep it, or to read it; and on top of that, having heard it, they will leave that place because they do not wish to listen.

Bhadrapāla, it is like this. Suppose some jewel merchant thought to show a priceless precious beryl gem to some people of foolish nature. Those people of foolish nature, seeing the precious gem, would say to him, "O man, what is the price of this precious gem?" The jewel merchant would say to them, "Give me as much land as the light of this precious gem pervades, in all four directions, completely filled with the seven precious substances. And why? Because that is the full price of this precious gem." When those beings of foolish nature heard from him the price of the precious gem, they would burst out laughing, scoff, and sneer, and would weigh the precious gem in their two hands.

Just so, Bhadrapāla, in the future there will be monks with many conceptual thoughts and little merit, who have not generated roots of virtue, who have not rendered service to the Victors of the past, tormented by pride, tormented by jealousy, tormented by gain, honor, and renown, whose moral discipline is not pure, who have not attained the samādhis, who have not attained wisdom, of little learning, who do not keep company with teachers, who view things in terms of a person, rely on a person, abide in a person, and cling to the apprehension of phenomena as objects. They will be frightened by the teaching of emptiness. Having heard this samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present, they will not reflect on it, will not have faith in it, will not trust it, and will not take delight in it; on top of that, even when they hear it, they will burst out laughing, will scoff, and will sneer. And going off in secret, they will revile it and reject it among themselves, and they will even say, "These monks are brazen.

These monks talk nonsense. These things were never spoken by the Buddha. They are their own making, their own fabrications, assorted words and letters, mere talk, and yet they explain them under the name of sūtras. How astonishing!" And saying, "These sūtras were not spoken by the Buddha," they will lead other people to understand them in that way too. Thus those foolish people of little merit, who have not generated roots of virtue, who have not served the victors of the past, who have utterly rejected the unsurpassed precious Dharma and have become outsiders to the precious Dharma, will resort to their very poverty of merit, cultivate it, and make much of it.

Bhadrapāla, at that time there will also be certain other beings who have served the victors of the past and have generated roots of virtue. Having heard this samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present, they will take it up so as to spread it. They will master it, write it down, have it written down, expound it, and recite it, and they will also teach it extensively and correctly to others. They will have heightened faith, will reflect on it much, and will have much devotion. Their conduct will be serene. They will have a sense of shame and remorse. They will long for the training and be devoted to the profound. They will be endowed with wisdom and will be very learned. They will abide in loving-kindness and attain compassion. So that this samādhi may be heard, wherever they go they will teach it there and teach it correctly. They will form this intention: "May this samādhi of the bodhisattvas, spoken by the Buddha, by all means long endure! May it spread!"

Bhadrapāla, before this assembly with its gods, humans, and asuras, I declare this to you, I make you understand it. Compare a son or daughter of good family who fills this great trichiliocosm with the seven precious substances and offers it to the tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly complete buddhas, with a son or daughter of good family who listens to this bodhisattva samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present, and having heard it, reflects on it, has faith in it, trusts it, is convinced, "This is the very truth," and also declares in words, "It was spoken by the Buddha." The merit of the latter son or daughter of good family will increase far more.

Then, at that time, to teach this very point, the Blessed One spoke these verses:

This concludes the sixth chapter, on the overweeningly proud, from The Samādhi Requested by Bhadrapāla.

The third fascicle.

Bhadrapāla, as for those who please me now, while I am teaching this samādhi: that in the future, in the latter time, in the final five hundred years, they should hear this samādhi and reject it or revile it is impossible; there is no possibility of it, except for those who have fallen into the hands of evil friends or are separated from spiritual friends. Bhadrapāla, if even some foolish people who have come into the hands of spiritual friends hear this samādhi and do not have faith in it, do not trust it, and are not devoted to it, what need is there even to mention those who have fallen into the hands of evil friends? And why is that, Bhadrapāla? Great learning is just that hard to accomplish. Seeing the buddhas is just that hard to obtain. Bhadrapāla, devotion to the qualities of a buddha is just that difficult. Bhadrapāla, this bodhisattva samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present, which gives rise to all the qualities of a buddha, is likewise hard to accomplish.

Bhadrapāla, consider any bodhisattva, whether a householder or one who has gone forth, who hears a samādhi such as this and is not afraid, not frightened, not terrified. He does not laugh at it, does not scoff, does not sneer, and does not reject it. Beyond that, having heard it, he rejoices in it, has faith in it, trusts it, and aspires to it, and he gives rise to the wish to teach this samādhi, to take it up, to master it, to retain it, to read it, to write it down, to explain it, and to cultivate it. Bhadrapāla, all such sons or daughters of good family are seen by the Tathāgata. They are known by the Tathāgata.

"Bhadrapāla, how does the Tathāgata see such holy persons, and how does the Tathāgata know them? Bhadrapāla, such holy persons will take up this meditative absorption, master it, retain it, recite it, write it down, teach it, and cultivate it. They will not be of corrupt moral discipline. They will not be without faith. They will not be averse to the Dharma. They will not hold wrong views. They will not be attached to objects of focus. Rather, Bhadrapāla, such sons or daughters of good family will be endowed with faith. That is to say, such sons and daughters of good family who take up teachings such as these will be given to much reflection and much devotion. They will have conviction, they will love the Dharma, and they will be drawn to the profound.

"Bhadrapāla, such sons or daughters of good family will not be of little merit or few roots of virtue. Rather, Bhadrapāla, such sons or daughters of good family have done service to the Victors of the past, and their roots of virtue are blazing. Bhadrapāla, such sons and daughters of good family have not honored and served only a single buddha. They have not generated roots of virtue under only one, two, or three buddhas. Rather, Bhadrapāla, such sons of good family will have honored and served a hundred buddhas. Such sons of good family will have generated roots of virtue under a hundred buddhas. From those tathāgatas, those sons or daughters of good family will have heard this meditative absorption, rejoiced in it, and been devoted to it. In the later time, in the later age, even in the final five hundred years, when they hear this meditative absorption they will not reject it. More than that, on hearing it they will rejoice, they will offer the words 'Excellent!', they will retain it, recite it, receive its transmission, explain it, and strive in the practice of cultivating it.

"Bhadrapāla, suppose some son or daughter of good family hears this bodhisattva meditative absorption in which the buddhas of the present stand directly before one, and is not frightened, not terrified, not filled with dread, and does not reject it. More than that, on hearing it, they rejoice, they have firm faith, they have firm conviction, they are devoted, and they offer the words 'Excellent!' Having heard it, they take it up, master it, retain it, recite it, write it down, have it written down, and teach it, and even for just a few days they diligently strive in the practice of cultivation in order to cultivate this meditative absorption. Then, Bhadrapāla, those sons or daughters of good family will generate much merit on that basis. They will generate a heap of merit that is immeasurable and incalculable. Those sons and daughters of good family will become irreversible from unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. They will also attain complete fulfillment, just as they intend.

"Nevertheless, Bhadrapāla, in order to make that very point still more complete, I shall give you an example. Bhadrapāla, it is like this. Suppose a certain person came along and ground this great trichiliocosm, this world system of a billion worlds, into particles of atomic dust. Suppose he also ground into particles of atomic dust all the grasses, branches, leaves, and petals of this great trichiliocosm, down to those as small as four finger-widths. Then that person took a single particle of atomic dust from among them and divided it into as many parts as there are particles of atomic dust in all, and in that manner divided every one of those particles of atomic dust into that many parts. Bhadrapāla, what do you think? Would those particles of atomic dust be many?"

The bodhisattva Bhadrapāla replied, "Venerable Blessed One, they would be many. Venerable Sugata, they would be many."

The Blessed One said, "Bhadrapāla, suppose some son or daughter of good family were to fill as many buddha-fields as there are parts of those particles of atomic dust with the seven precious substances and present them as an offering to the tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly and completely awakened buddhas. Would they generate much merit on that basis?"

He replied, "Venerable Blessed One, it would be much. Venerable Sugata, it would be much."

The Blessed One said, "Bhadrapāla, have faith in this. You should understand it. Bhadrapāla, there is a son or daughter of good family who fills that many buddha-fields with the seven precious substances and presents them as an offering to the Tathāgata. But, Bhadrapāla, suppose a son or daughter of good family hears this bodhisattva meditative absorption in which the buddhas of the present stand directly before one, and examines it, has firm faith in it, has conviction in it, and is devoted to it; and having examined it, having firm faith, conviction, and devotion, takes it up, masters it, retains it, recites it, writes it down, and also explains it extensively to others, teaches it, and correctly expounds it. The heap of merit of this son or daughter of good family grows far greater than those earlier roots of virtue. What need then to mention one who abides striving in the practice of cultivation even for as long as it takes to milk a cow?

"Bhadrapāla, I do not even speak of the measure of the heap of merit of those sons or daughters of good family who hear this meditative absorption, take it up, master it, retain it, recite it, write it down, and also make it extensively understood by others, teach it, and correctly expound it. What need then to mention, Bhadrapāla, the son or daughter of good family who accomplishes this meditative absorption, trains in suchness, and applies themselves diligently to suchness?"

Then, at that time, the Blessed One spoke these verses:

"Those who fill these world systems of the great thousand

Than that, whoever hears a Dharma such as this,
whatever bodhisattva, wishing for much merit,

Suppose someone cut this world-system up like atoms,

no measure can be found for his merit.

This concludes the seventh chapter, the chapter on the samādhi requested by Bhadrapāla.

Bhadrapāla, how, then, should a bodhisattva great being cultivate this samādhi? Bhadrapāla, just as I am now seated before you teaching the Dharma, in the same way, Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva should bring to mind those tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly complete buddhas as seated on the seat of a buddha and teaching the Dharma. He should bring the tathāgatas to mind as endowed with the supreme of every aspect, of excellent form, beautiful, lovely to behold, and possessed of a perfect body. He should regard each one of the major marks of a great being on the body of a tathāgata, arhat, perfectly complete buddha as produced by a hundred merits. He should also take hold of their features. He should also inquire thoroughly into the crown of the head, whose top cannot be seen. Having inquired, he should take hold of the features of the major marks of a great being. Having taken hold of them, he should train in this way: "Ah, how wondrous is the beauty of those tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly complete buddhas! I too, in a future time, will come to possess such a perfect body as this. Such marks as these will be complete in me. I too will come to possess moral discipline such as this, and likewise samādhi, likewise wisdom, likewise liberation, and likewise the seeing of the wisdom of liberation. I too, in the same way, will fully and perfectly awaken to unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening. Having fully and perfectly awakened, I will teach the Dharma to the fourfold assembly and to the world with its gods." Thus he should train.

Having thus brought this to mind as endowed with the supreme of all aspects, he should further train in this way, thinking: "What is that thing called 'I'? And what is that thing called 'mine'? What, too, is awakening? And who is it that fully and perfectly awakens to awakening? Does one fully and perfectly awaken to awakening with the body, or does one fully and perfectly awaken to awakening with the mind? If one were to say that one fully and perfectly awakens to awakening with the body, the body is inert matter, motionless, without agency, without volition, like grass, wood, stone, a wall, or a mirage, while awakening is formless, indemonstrable, invisible, and cannot be made known. How then could one, with a body that is inert, motionless, without agency, and without volition, fully and perfectly awaken to an awakening that is formless, indemonstrable, invisible, and cannot be made known? And if one were to say that one fully and perfectly awakens to awakening with the mind, the mind is formless, indemonstrable, invisible, cannot be made known, and is like an illusion, while awakening is formless, indemonstrable, invisible, and cannot be made known. How then could one, with a mind that is formless, indemonstrable, invisible, cannot be made known, and is like an illusion, fully and perfectly awaken to an awakening that is formless, indemonstrable, invisible, and cannot be made known?" In this way he should train.

Bhadrapāla, in this regard the bodhisattva should train in this way, thinking: "One does not fully and perfectly awaken to awakening with the body. One does not fully and perfectly awaken to awakening with the mind. Nor does what is without mind fully and perfectly awaken to awakening by means of mind. Nor does what is without form fully and perfectly awaken to awakening by means of form. Nor does mind fully and perfectly awaken to awakening by means of mind. Nor does form fully and perfectly awaken to awakening by means of form."

Bhadrapāla, why is that? The body of the Tathāgata has completely abandoned all grasping at extremes. Therefore the Tathāgata dwells contemplating the body in the body, and dwells contemplating the mind in the mind.

Bhadrapāla, the wisdom that fully knows all phenomena, a wisdom that is formless, indemonstrable, invisible, cannot be made known, and is undefiled, neither fully and perfectly awakens nor fails to fully and perfectly awaken. Why is that? Since the bodies of the tathāgatas are undefiled, the minds of the tathāgatas are undefiled, the form of the tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly complete buddhas, is also undefiled, and the feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness of the tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly complete buddhas, are also undefiled, what need is there even to mention that the moral discipline, the meditative absorption, the wisdom, the liberation, or the knowledge and vision of liberation of the tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly complete buddhas, are undefiled? Bhadrapāla, all the qualities of the tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly complete buddhas, are undefiled. Whatever true Dharma the tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly complete buddhas, have taught, teach, or will uphold, that Dharma too is undefiled. Childish and unskilled people do not know it, but the wise fully realize it.

Bhadrapāla, at that time the bodhisattva should train in this way, thinking: "Does one awaken to awakening with the body, or does one fully and perfectly awaken to awakening with wisdom?" Bhadrapāla, in this regard the bodhisattva, the great being, should train in this way, thinking: "One does not awaken to awakening with the body, nor with wisdom either." Why is that? That wisdom is undefiled, and though it is thoroughly sought in every way, it is not found. For that reason he becomes equanimous with regard to the search for awakening. Why is that? Awakening, too, is undefiled, and though it is thoroughly sought in every way, it is not found. Though he thoroughly searches his own body and his own mind, he does not find them. In the same way, though he thoroughly searches all phenomena, he does not find them. He does not find any "who fully and perfectly awakens to awakening." He does not find any "by which one awakens to awakening." He neither finds nor sees any "what is to be fully and perfectly awakened to." When he examines all phenomena in this way, he does not truly see them. Although he does not truly see all phenomena as being by nature at peace, they are not by nature at peace. Why is that? Because what is unborn is not something that has been pacified. With regard to all phenomena that are unborn and at peace, he conceives of them as peaceful phenomena. With regard to all peaceful phenomena, he conceives of them as free from peace. Having examined in this way, he should not conceive of phenomena as "peaceful," nor should he conceive of them as not peaceful. Bhadrapāla, why is that? Because all phenomena are unborn and not fully established.

Bhadrapāla, suppose, for example, that some person, when no fire had been kindled and none was blazing, were to say, "I shall put out this fire." Bhadrapāla, what do you think of that? Would that person be speaking truthfully?

Bhadrapāla replied, "No indeed, venerable Blessed One."

The Blessed One said, "Bhadrapāla, it is the same, because all phenomena are not found. Whoever says, apart from worldly convention, 'I shall fully know all phenomena. I shall abandon all phenomena. I shall directly realize all phenomena. I shall cultivate all phenomena. I shall pacify all phenomena. I shall attain all phenomena. I shall attain the fruit of stream-entry.

"I will attain the fruit of once-returner. I will attain the fruit of non-returner. I will attain arhatship. I will attain solitary awakening. I will fully and manifestly awaken to unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. Having fully and manifestly awakened to unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening, I will teach the Dharma. I will completely free sentient beings from saṃsāra." If he were to say this, would he be speaking as one who speaks truly?

Bhadrapāla replied, "No indeed, Venerable Blessed One."

The Blessed One said, "Therefore, Bhadrapāla, sons or daughters of good family who wish for perfect and complete awakening, sons or daughters of good family who wish for solitary awakening, and those who wish for arhatship should examine those phenomena in that way. Having examined those phenomena in that way, they should not conceive of them as 'phenomena are at peace,' nor should they conceive of them as 'phenomena are not at peace.'

"Why is that, Bhadrapāla? If anyone conceives of all phenomena, which are nothing whatsoever, unborn and not established at all, as 'all phenomena are at peace,' that is one extreme. If one conceives of them as 'all phenomena are not at peace,' that is the second extreme. Bhadrapāla, not apprehending, not conceiving, not establishing, not attending to and not engaging in these two extremes of peace and non-peace is called the Middle Way, by way of enumeration and in accordance with the conventional teaching of the world. Ultimately, however, neither extreme nor middle can be apprehended in it.

"Why is that, Bhadrapāla? Because all phenomena are like space and equal to nirvāṇa. They are not annihilated, not destroyed, impermanent, and not everlastingly abiding; they do not abide in any place, they do not abide in any direction, they are without signs, they are incalculable. Since even the wise do not approach them through calculation and do not apprehend them, all phenomena are called incalculable.

"Bhadrapāla, in this way a bodhisattva who sees those tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly and completely awakened buddhas should not become attached to them. Why is that? Bhadrapāla, all phenomena are not something to be attached to; in them there is no attachment. They are called root-severed, rootless and without support.

"Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva should cultivate this bodhisattva samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present in such a way that, whenever he sees those buddhas, those blessed ones, he does not hold them as supreme, does not apprehend them, and does not grasp them wrongly. Why is that? Because, Bhadrapāla, the Tathāgata has declared, 'All phenomena are ungraspable, utterly at peace, like space, and equal to nirvāṇa.'

"Bhadrapāla, it is like this: the wise do not take hold of an ingot of gold that has been thoroughly heated and thoroughly smelted until it is like a glowing lump of iron. Why is that, Bhadrapāla? Because it is by nature hot. Bhadrapāla, although it is gold, the finest of precious things, still, Bhadrapāla, because it is so very hot, they do not take hold of it. In the same way, Bhadrapāla, when a bodhisattva, a great being, sees those tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly and completely awakened buddhas, he should not become attached to them. Nor should he become attached to form, feeling, perception, formations or consciousness. Nor should he become attached to moral discipline, samādhi, wisdom, liberation, or the knowledge and vision of liberation. He should not become attached to any of the qualities of a buddha. He should not become attached in any way at all. Why is that, Bhadrapāla? Because attachment produces all the phenomena of suffering, whose very nature is saṃsāra.

"Yet, Bhadrapāla, when a bodhisattva, a great being, sees those tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly and completely awakened buddhas, he should aspire to their surpassing qualities in this way: 'How marvelous are the great wisdom, the wisdom of a buddha, the wisdom of a tathāgata, the self-arisen wisdom, the wisdom equal to the unequaled, the wisdom that surpasses all three worlds, of the tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly and completely awakened buddhas, who are endowed with such wondrous qualities!' Yet the bodhisattva, the great being, should not become attached to that wisdom to which he is to aspire here. Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva, a great being, should not become attached even to this samādhi. Why is that? Bhadrapāla, these phenomena become directly manifest as free of attachment."

Then, at that time, the Blessed One spoke these verses:

In a polished mirror or a vessel of oil

One who knows this attains this samādhi.

Taking the body of the Victor as your object,
listen to the Dharma that is pure by nature.

then this samādhi will not be hard to find.
If one abides in the perception of space,
the perception of atoms will cease.
One who knows phenomena as free of thought, unmade and indestructible,
attains this samādhi.
Knowing all forms to be free of conceptual thought,
wherever the eye may look, one is not attached.
Like seven suns risen in this world,
so will he see the buddhas.
The eye is pure, the ear is perfectly pure;

one retains inconceivably vast learning.
That which is the samādhi of not seeing,
that which is the samādhi of all beings,
by which one does not see, by which one does not perceive:
here many other non-Buddhists (tīrthikas) come to ruin.
When the perceptions of signs have been dispelled,
his mind is pure, and he sees the buddhas.
Having seen, he once again does not see,
and he will become a buddha, chief of the world.
Before him there is no earth, nor any water,
no fire, no wind, and no space.
Yet he perceives the buddhas present
and teaching the sacred Dharma.
Just as those who desire the Dharma
look upon me as I sit teaching the Dharma,
so for him, apart from the buddhas and the supreme Dharma,
no other object arises.
For those endowed in this way,
apart from the many buddhas teaching
this stainless samādhi they have well upheld,
no other hearer or teacher at all is perceived.
There has been no buddha in the past
who did not teach this peaceful, noble samādhi,
nor will there be any in the future,
nor is there any present who will not teach it.
I too, who have now appeared in the world
as the highest of humans for the benefit of living beings,
recollecting these buddha guides,
teach this peaceful samādhi, so hard to see.
One who, heedless of body and likewise of life,
wishes to seek the qualities of the buddhas,
by abiding in this samādhi
will not find buddha awakening hard to attain.
The wise one who wishes to see
the many supreme humans of a single nature and to question them
should hurry, hurry, and swiftly
cultivate the stainless, pure samādhi.
Whoever has set out for the benefit of beings
and seeks vast, inconceivable learning
should hurry, hurry, and swiftly
cultivate the stainless, pure samādhi.
In this there is no attachment, no aversion;
in this there is no delusion, no envy;
in this there is neither knowing nor not knowing.
Therefore, come to know this peaceful samādhi.

This concludes the eighth chapter of the Samādhi Requested by Bhadrapāla.

When the Blessed One had spoken thus, the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, said to the Blessed One, "Venerable Blessed One, it is wonderful that the Blessed One has taught this profound samādhi in this way. Venerable Blessed One, if bodhisattva great beings who have gone forth from the household wish, having heard this samādhi, to receive its transmission or to cultivate it, then, Blessed One, abiding in which qualities should such a bodhisattva receive the transmission of this samādhi and cultivate it?"

When he had asked this, the Blessed One said to the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, "Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva who has gone forth from the household and who, having heard this samādhi, wishes to receive its transmission or to cultivate it, should make his moral discipline completely pure. He should keep his moral discipline free of fault."

The bodhisattva Bhadrapāla asked, "Venerable Blessed One, how is the moral discipline of a bodhisattva who has gone forth from the household completely pure and free of fault?"

The Blessed One said, "Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva who has gone forth from the household and who, having heard this samādhi, wishes to receive its transmission or to cultivate it, should be restrained by the vow of individual liberation (prātimokṣa). He should be perfect in conduct and in his sphere of activity, see danger in even the slightest misdeed, and keep his deportment completely pure. Having properly taken them up, he should train in the bases of training. He should have devotion to the profound and acceptance of what cannot be objectified. On hearing the teachings of emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness, he should not be afraid, he should not be terrified, and he should not become frightened. Bhadrapāla, it is in this way that the moral discipline of a bodhisattva who has gone forth from the household is completely pure and free of fault."

The bodhisattva Bhadrapāla asked, "Venerable Blessed One (the Buddha), in what way is the moral discipline of a bodhisattva who has gone forth from the household life not completely pure, and in what way is that moral discipline faulty?"

The Blessed One said, "Bhadrapāla, some bodhisattvas who have gone forth from the household life live the holy life while abiding in form, and live the holy life while abiding in feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness. They say, 'Through this moral discipline, austerity, and holy life of mine, may I become a god, or one among the gods.' This, Bhadrapāla, is the moral discipline of a bodhisattva gone forth from the household life that is not completely pure; it is moral discipline that has faults. That is to say, because they hold the notion of existence to be supreme, wish to indulge in sense pleasures, and wish to take up a place of birth in existence, their moral discipline is not completely pure.

"Therefore, Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva who has gone forth from the household life and who, having heard this samādhi, wishes to explain it or to meditate on it, should make their moral discipline completely pure. They should make their moral discipline faultless. They should keep their moral discipline unadulterated. They should keep their moral discipline unbroken. They should keep their moral discipline unblemished. They should make it moral discipline without reliance, moral discipline without holding it as supreme, moral discipline without any objectifying reference, moral discipline that is not destroyed, moral discipline praised by the wise, and moral discipline praised by the noble ones.

"They should delight in giving. That is, they should apply diligence to the gift of the Dharma, the supreme gift, the unsurpassed gift. They should establish mindfulness. They should be endowed with faith. They should be gentle, endowed with a sense of shame, and perfect in conscience. They should not be excessively attached to gain, honor, and verses of praise. They should be without stinginess and without jealousy, should possess the qualities of ascetic practice, and should keep few belongings. They should not pursue worldly talk; giving up such talk entirely, they should pursue talk that transcends the world. They should be grateful and repay kindness. They should be quiet and speak little. They should be respectful and reverent. That is to say, since a spiritual friend is hard to find, they should be eager to serve their master and their preceptor.

"Toward the person from whom they will hear such teachings as these and receive them, and have them written down in volumes, and on whose verbal teaching they depend, and toward the person from whom they master such a sūtra as this, or will master it, or copy it in volumes, or have it written, they should generate the notion that this is their father. They should generate the notion that this is a spiritual friend. They should generate the notion that this is the Teacher. For the sake of the meaning of such teachings as these, and in order to bring awakening to full maturity, they should generate toward that person still greater joy, faith, and respect.

"Bhadrapāla, if sons or daughters of good family belonging to the bodhisattva vehicle, the śrāvaka vehicle, or the pratyekabuddha vehicle do not generate joy, faith, and respect toward a monk who expounds such teachings as these, and do not generate the notion of a spiritual friend nor the notion of the Teacher, then, Bhadrapāla, it is impossible, there is no occasion, that those sons or daughters of good family of the bodhisattva vehicle, the śrāvaka vehicle, or the pratyekabuddha vehicle will master such teachings that they have not yet mastered, or that the teachings they have already mastered will remain, will not go to waste, and will not disappear. It is also impossible that a son of good family of the bodhisattva vehicle will attain this samādhi. And why is that, Bhadrapāla? Because through disrespect the true Dharma will decline.

"Bhadrapāla, if sons or daughters of good family of the bodhisattva vehicle, or of the śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha vehicle, generate joy, faith, and respect toward a monk who teaches such teachings as these, and toward those from whom they will master such a Dharma discourse as this, who have mastered it, and who have written it down in volumes, and if they establish the notion of a spiritual friend and the notion of the Teacher, then, Bhadrapāla, it is possible that those sons or daughters of good family of the bodhisattva vehicle, the śrāvaka vehicle, or the pratyekabuddha vehicle will master teachings they have not mastered, and that the teachings they have mastered will remain, will not go to waste, and will not disappear. That is indeed possible.

"Therefore, Bhadrapāla, you should have faith in this. You should understand it. In this way, you should generate joy, faith, and respect toward the monks who teach such teachings as these. You should generate the notion that they are spiritual friends and the notion that they are the Teacher. This is my instruction.

"Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva who has gone forth from the household life and who wishes to receive the transmission of this samādhi and wishes to meditate on it should be free from remorse. They should delight in the wilderness, incline toward the wilderness, tend toward the wilderness, and settle in the wilderness. They should take no delight in the settled countryside. Not wanting the houses of friends or the houses of those who give food, they should have no regard for their own lives. Giving up the body, they should not be attached to the body, and should attain the perception of the wilderness. They should not rely on gain, honor, and verses of praise, and should firmly uphold the true Dharma. They should not be attached to their bowl and robes, and should not hoard them. They should go for alms without needing to seek invitations as a guest. They should be scrupulous and conscientious. They should not take gold as their own. Being free of the outbursts of affliction, they should be free from remorse. Having abandoned anger, they should abide in loving-kindness. Having abandoned harmfulness, they should abide in compassion. Having abandoned discontent, they should abide in joy. Having abandoned all signs, they should abide in equanimity. Having undertaken diligence, they should aspire to the trainings.

Not overcome by torpor and sleep, one should engage in walking meditation. Therefore, Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva who has gone forth from the household life, has heard this samādhi, and wishes to take it up and cultivate it should abide in these qualities. He should take up this samādhi, and he should cultivate it.

When he had said this, the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, said to the Blessed One (the Buddha): "Venerable Blessed One, it is wonderful that the Tathāgata has taught and spoken these vast and excellent teachings. Venerable Blessed One, the lazy bodhisattvas who will appear will hear this samādhi and be frightened, be terrified, and become utterly terrified. Even when such excellent teachings are explained, they will not gain vast joy, delight, and faith. They will think, 'We shall accomplish this samādhi under some other tathāgata, arhat, perfectly complete buddha. For now our bodies are emaciated and we have many illnesses.' When they hear this samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present, they will become discouraged and utterly discouraged, and they will not even exert diligence to accomplish this samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present.

"Then again, Venerable Blessed One, there will appear bodhisattva great beings who abide in mindfulness with diligence undertaken; who seek the Dharma, respect the Dharma, uphold the Dharma, teach the Dharma, speak the Dharma, and take up the Dharma; who practice the Dharma in accordance with the Dharma; who give up body and life; who do not rely on gain, honor, and verses; who do not seek renown for their good qualities; who are not attached to bowl and robes; who take no delight in villages, towns, market towns, regions, kingdoms, provinces, and royal courts; who incline toward solitude, tend toward solitude, and have arrived at solitude. When they hear this bodhisattva samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present, they will not become discouraged, will not become utterly discouraged, will not be frightened, will not be terrified, and will not become utterly terrified. More than that, they will gain vast joy, delight, and faith. They will also undertake great diligence in order to grasp, master, retain, read, write out, teach, and cultivate this samādhi. Venerable Blessed One, these sons or daughters of good family will not seek great learning from buddhas who have not yet come. Nor will they put off their striving to accomplish the distinctive mantras, qualities, and teachings.

"That being so, Venerable Blessed One, in the future there will appear sons or daughters of good family who have served former victors and have undertaken diligence. When they hear such a samādhi as this, spoken by the Tathāgata, they will think, 'Even if right here our bodies, skin, flesh, blood, bones, sinews, and marrow dry up and wither away completely, that is better. We will not become lazy like this and die. We will not give up diligence without having mastered, explained, and cultivated such vast and excellent teachings as these.' Thinking this, they will undertake diligence. Such holy persons, who constantly undertake diligence, upon hearing a sūtra such as this spoken by the Tathāgata, will gain exceedingly vast joy, delight, and faith the moment they hear it."

When he had said this, the Blessed One said to the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla: "Excellent, Bhadrapāla, excellent! Moreover, Bhadrapāla, the teaching in accordance with the Dharma that you have explained is unerring; it is just so. Excellent! I too rejoice in it, and in whatever I rejoice, the buddhas, the blessed ones of the past, future, and present, also rejoice."

Then, at that time, the Blessed One spoke these verses:

"Whatever training of the prātimokṣa I have taught,
Train in all of it and resort to the wilderness;
If you always practice the qualities of purification,
This samādhi will not be hard to find.

Give up all seeking to be invited as a guest,
And completely give up attachment to tastes;
Regard the one from whom you hear it as the Teacher,
And cultivate the perception of equality and sameness.

Give up jealousy, desire, and pride,
Give up attachment, aversion, and delusion,
Properly generate a mind devoted to meditative concentration,
And enter into this samādhi.

Free of jealousy, having given up attachment,
Fully restrained, having given up anger,
Having made the Victor's body your object of focus,
Enter into this samādhi.

Not giving up diligence, not overcome by sleep,
Not keeping company with relatives and friends,
Devoted to walking meditation, shunning crowds and companions,
Cultivate that stainless samādhi!"

This is the ninth chapter of the samādhi requested by Bhadrapāla.

The fourth section.

When he had said this, the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, said to the Blessed One: "Venerable Blessed One, if some nun who has properly entered the Great Vehicle hears this samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present and wishes to take it up or to cultivate it, Venerable Blessed One, abiding in what qualities should she take up this samādhi and cultivate it?"

When he had asked this, the Blessed One said to the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla: "Bhadrapāla, a nun who has properly entered the Great Vehicle, has heard this samādhi, and wishes to take it up or to cultivate it should always be respectful. She should always be free of jealousy and free of anger. She should destroy pride, be without arrogance, and undertake diligence. She should be without laziness and give up torpor and sleep.

She should strive in the practice of not lying down to sleep. She should give up all seeking after gain, honor, acclaim, robes, food, bedding, medicine for the sick, and requisites. Her livelihood should be completely pure, and she should not cling even to body and life. She should always long for the Dharma and strive to seek much learning. Her mind should be free of attachment, free of aversion, and free of dullness. She should abandon the side of Māra. She should give up massaging, adorning, and applying cosmetics to the body. She should not be attached to her bowl and robes. She should not be attached to fame, should be free of slander, and should give up all fickleness.

Therefore, Bhadrapāla, a nun who has truly entered the Great Vehicle and who, having heard this samādhi, wishes to receive it or to cultivate it, should regard the master as the Teacher and receive this samādhi. Abiding in these qualities, she should cultivate this samādhi.

Then the Blessed One spoke these verses:

A nun who longs for this samādhi,
If she is always respectful and gives up jealousy, anger,
Pride, and likewise conceit,
Will not find this samādhi hard to obtain.

If you seek this samādhi,
Take up diligence and give up sleep,
Give up every kind of seeking,
And, not clinging even to life, long for the Dharma!

Whoever wishes to hold this samādhi
Should not dwell with a mind tormented by afflictions,
Possessed of attachment and aversion;
Never let yourself be caught by the noose of Māra!

Whoever wishes to hold this samādhi
Should not behave with deceit,
And should give up massaging, ornaments, slander,
And all fickleness.

For the sake of a bowl or for the sake of praise
She gives not even a moment's thought.
Regard the one from whom you hear it as the Teacher,
And hold the perception that they are equal and alike.

This was the tenth chapter of The Samādhi Requested by Bhadrapāla.

When the Blessed One had said this, the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, said to the Blessed One, "Venerable Blessed One, a bodhisattva who is a householder, wearing white clothes and living at home, who, having heard this samādhi, wishes to receive it or to cultivate it for even one day or two days: Venerable Blessed One, abiding in what qualities should he receive this samādhi and cultivate it?"

The Blessed One said, "Therefore, Bhadrapāla, if a bodhisattva who is a householder, wearing white clothes and living at home, having heard this samādhi, wishes to receive it or to cultivate it for even one day, or two days, or even just the time it takes to milk a cow, that householder bodhisattva, wearing white clothes and living at home, should be endowed with faith. He should be free of stinginess and give freely. He should delight in distributing gifts, and give away all his possessions without hoping for any karmic ripening. He should go for refuge to the Buddha. He should go for refuge to the Dharma. He should go for refuge to the Saṅgha. He should keep his moral discipline completely pure. He should take up the five bases of training. He should have no other god, and his intention should follow the Buddha. Being faultless in moral discipline, properly adopting and practicing the ten paths of virtuous action, and leading others to adopt them as well, he should turn away from drinking alcohol. He should not be haughty. Speaking to others of the faults of alcohol, not giving alcohol to others, and disparaging sensual desire to others as well, he should practice celibacy. He should be free of jealousy. His livelihood should be completely pure. He should not crave sons. He does not give rise to craving for daughters. Do not give rise to craving for your wife! He should not crave wealth. He should not crave a home. He should delight in going forth, and his mind should be inclined toward going forth. Keeping the fast of eight branches, he should be inclined toward this samādhi and should stay at a monastery. He should have a sense of shame and of modesty, his mind should be inclined toward awakening, and he should not wish for any other vehicle. He should not disparage monks who possess moral discipline. He should be respectful and courteous toward those who lead the pure life. Toward the master from whom he received this samādhi he should be deeply devoted, holding him extremely dear, with faith and respect. He should regard him as a spiritual friend. He should also regard him as the Teacher. He should offer him every requisite for his comfort. He should live with gratitude, repaying kindness, and with respect. Bhadrapāla, in this way a householder bodhisattva living at home should apply himself to this samādhi. Abiding in these qualities, he should cultivate this samādhi."

Then the Blessed One spoke these verses:

A bodhisattva living at home
Who wishes to hold this samādhi
Should always keep his moral discipline completely pure,
Firmly give rise to the mind of renunciation,
Take up the five bases of training,
Observe the eight-branched poṣadha,
And, without having left his home, stay at a monastery;
Thus should he enter into this samādhi.

He should not pour alcohol for anyone,
And should give up drinks the Buddha has not praised;
Abiding in these trainings,
He should receive this samādhi.

Not craving sons, not craving daughters,
Not craving a wife, nor craving a home,
A layman possessed of a sense of shame,
He should enter into this samādhi.

Keep yourself free of sickness, and with no one
Keep company or talk; give up all idle chatter.
Abiding in faultless patience,
Enter into this samādhi.

When entering this noble samādhi,
Always be respectful toward the Buddha and the Dharma,
With a mind clear, unwavering, and free of jealousy,
Be eager to serve and honor the Saṅgha.

This was the eleventh chapter of The Samādhi Requested by Bhadrapāla.

When he had spoken these words, the bodhisattva mahāsattva Bhadrapāla said to the Blessed One (the Buddha), "Venerable Blessed One, if a laywoman who has correctly entered the Great Vehicle hears this samādhi and wishes to receive its transmission or to cultivate it, Venerable Blessed One, in what qualities should she abide in order to receive the transmission of this samādhi and to cultivate it?"

When he had asked this, the Blessed One said to the bodhisattva mahāsattva Bhadrapāla, "Therefore, Bhadrapāla, a laywoman who has correctly entered the Great Vehicle, and who hears this samādhi and wishes to receive its transmission or to cultivate it, should take up the five precepts.

"Bhadrapāla, the laywoman should go for refuge to the Three and should have no other gods.

"Bhadrapāla, the laywoman should completely give up auspicious signs and omens, and should be without deceit.

"Bhadrapāla, the laywoman should keep her conduct free of pretense and should be without stinginess.

"Bhadrapāla, the laywoman should delight in giving out gifts and should long for the Dharma.

"Bhadrapāla, the laywoman should be inquiring by nature, respectful, and courteous. When she sees monks and nuns, she should offer them a seat.

"Therefore, Bhadrapāla, if a laywoman who has correctly entered the Great Vehicle hears this samādhi and wishes to receive its transmission or to cultivate it, that laywoman should abide in such qualities as these, and so receive the transmission of this samādhi and cultivate it."

Then, at that time, the Blessed One spoke these verses:

"The laywoman who seeks this samādhi,
Approved by the buddhas and praised by the Sugatas,
Should take up the five precepts
And so enter into this samādhi.

Having no other gods than the Buddha,
The Dharma, and likewise the Saṅgha, being respectful,
And giving up every wrong path,
She should enter into this samādhi.

Not killing living beings, not taking what is not given,
Not speaking any falsehood,
Never engaging in sexual misconduct,
She should receive the transmission of this samādhi.

Without stinginess, not hoping for a ripened reward,
Having no pretense in her conduct,
Giving up envy, pride, and anger,
She should receive the transmission of this samādhi.

When she sees monks and likewise nuns,
She rises respectfully and offers them a seat;
Longing for the Dharma, she seeks to know what is virtuous;
She should receive the transmission of this samādhi."

This concludes the twelfth chapter, on the laywoman, from the Samādhi Requested by Bhadrapāla.

When he had spoken these words, the bodhisattva mahāsattva Bhadrapāla said to the Blessed One, "Venerable Blessed One, it is wonderful that the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha, has taught these vast and excellent qualities, abiding in which one may receive the transmission of this samādhi or cultivate it, for the sake of bodhisattvas who have gone forth from the household, bodhisattvas of great aspiration, those of excellent superior intention, those who have taken up diligence, those who do not abandon effort, and those who have correctly set out for unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening.

"Venerable Blessed One, in the future, when the Tathāgata has passed into nirvāṇa, will this samādhi be practiced here in Jambudvīpa? Will it be spread?"

The Blessed One said, "Bhadrapāla, after I have passed completely into nirvāṇa, this samādhi will be practiced in Jambudvīpa for four thousand years. It will be spread. After that it will go into a hollow in the earth.

"Then again, in the last five hundred years, in the later time, in the final five-hundred-year period of that later age, when the holy Dharma is being destroyed, when renunciants are being destroyed, when the holy Dharma is being rejected, when beings are being destroyed, when kingdoms are in turmoil, when the time has come for the side of those with moral discipline to decline, when the time has come for the side of those of broken discipline to increase, when the time has come for the side of the holy Dharma to decline, when the time has come for the dharmas of the unholy side to increase, when the time of exhaustion has come, then a few beings will appear whose roots of virtue blaze so that they will obtain a sūtra such as this, who have done service to the victors of the past, who have generated roots of virtue, whose roots of virtue have ripened, and who have planted the seeds. For the sake of such beings as these, through the power of the Buddha, this samādhi will be practiced in Jambudvīpa. It will be spread.

"And when they hear this samādhi, they will obtain vast joy, delight, and faith. Having heard it, they will take it up, they will hold it, they will read it, they will master it, they will write it down, they will teach it, they will spread it, and they will strive in the practice of meditation. At the very least, they will write it in a volume and keep it."

Then the bodhisattva mahāsattva Bhadrapāla, the bodhisattva mahāsattva Ratnākara, and the Licchavi youth, having heard from the Blessed One of the changing and destruction of this holy Dharma in the later time, wept from the force of the Dharma and shed tears. Then the bodhisattva mahāsattva Bhadrapāla, the bodhisattva mahāsattva Ratnākara, and the Licchavi youth wiped away their tears, rose from their seats, draped their robes over one shoulder, and placed their right knees on the ground. Bowing with palms joined toward the Blessed One, they said to the Blessed One, "Venerable Blessed One, even in the final century after the Tathāgata has passed into nirvāṇa, in the later time, in the final five-hundred-year period of that later age, when the holy Dharma is being rejected, we will take up sūtras such as these. We will master them. We will write them down. We will hold them. We will read them. We will spread them. We will strive in the practice of meditation.

Why? Because, Venerable Blessed One (the Buddha), we can never have enough of listening to this profound sūtra spoken by the Tathāgata, of questioning it, taking it up, mastering it, keeping it, reading it, explaining it, spreading it widely, and meditating on it.

Then the bodhisattva great being, the householder Mahāsusārthavāha, the bodhisattva great being Guhagupta the merchant's son, the bodhisattva great being the youth Naladatta, the bodhisattva great being Susīma, the bodhisattva great being Indradatta, and the bodhisattva great being Varuṇa heard from the Blessed One that in the final five hundred years, in the later time, at that later age of the final five hundred years, this true Dharma would decline and be destroyed. Moved by the force of the Dharma, they wept and shed tears.

Then the bodhisattva great being, the householder Mahāsusārthavāha, Guhagupta the merchant's son, the youth Naladatta, the son of good family Susīma, Indradatta, and the bodhisattva great being Varuṇa wiped away their tears and rose from their seats. They draped their robes over one shoulder, set their right knees on the ground, bowed with palms joined toward the Blessed One, and said this to the Blessed One:

"Venerable Blessed One, in the future, after the Tathāgata has passed into nirvāṇa, in the final five hundred years, in the later time, at that later age of the final five hundred years, even when the true Dharma is being rejected, we too shall take up such sūtras as these. We shall master them. We shall write them down. We shall keep them. We shall read them. We shall also explain them extensively to others. We shall meditate on them. Venerable Blessed One, we shall make this unsurpassed, perfect awakening, which the tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly awakened buddhas fully accomplished over hundreds of thousands of millions of billions of eons, endure for a long time. We shall make it vast and widespread. Why? Because, Venerable Blessed One, we shall truly take up teachings not heard before, and we shall truly proclaim them. Venerable Blessed One, we shall meditate on the profound Dharma in which no worldly people will place their trust, and we shall teach it."

Then from that assembly some five hundred beings, monks, nuns, laymen and laywomen, rose from their seats with respect and reverence. Having heard from the Blessed One of the destruction of the true Dharma in the final five hundred years, in the later time, at that later age of the final five hundred years, and of the time when the true Dharma will not be accepted, they wept and shed tears, moved by the force of the decline of the true Dharma.

Then those five hundred or so beings wiped away their tears, draped their robes over one shoulder, set their right knees on the ground, bowed with palms joined toward the Blessed One, and said this to the Blessed One:

"Venerable Blessed One, after the Tathāgata has passed completely into nirvāṇa, in the final five hundred years, in the later time, at that later age of the final five hundred years, we too shall take up such sūtras as these. We shall meditate on them. We shall also proclaim them widely to others. We shall also serve these holy beings with our own bodies. At that time we shall also listen to such sūtras as this, spoken by the Tathāgata. There too we shall uphold the true Dharma. And with this precious jewel of the true Dharma we shall benefit others who are vessels for the true Dharma. Blessed One, please entrust this samādhi to these holy beings. Blessed One, please commend this samādhi to these holy beings."

Then at that moment the Blessed One smiled a smile the color of gold. Its light filled the boundless world systems and rose up as far as the world of Brahmā, outshining even the light of the sun and moon. Then it returned, circled the Blessed One three times to the right, and vanished into the crown of the Blessed One's head.

Then the venerable Ānanda rose from his seat, draped his upper robe over one shoulder, set his right knee on the ground, bowed with palms joined toward the Blessed One, and praised the Blessed One with fitting verses such as these:

"Pure in moral discipline, wholly pure in your sphere of conduct,
Of great miraculous power even among the mighty,
Exalted supreme above all beings,
You are beautiful as the stainless sun.
Unattached, endowed with knowledge, your mind utterly liberated,
With the voice of the kalaviṅka bird, god of gods,
Unshaken by any adversary,
What is the cause and condition for this smile?
Compassionate for the sake of benefit, chief among two-footed beings,
Please make your pronouncement, clarifying the ultimate meaning.
When one hears the pleasing sound of your voice,
One gains the noble joy untainted by worldly things.
Supreme Victor, Guide, supreme among persons,
You do not smile without a cause.
What is the cause and condition for this smile?
Compassionate one, I ask you for the benefit of the world."

From the smile of the Protector of the World,
whose great purpose will come about today?
Who will abide steadfast in the wisdom of the buddhas?
Who today will attain supreme kingship?
Who today will abide steadfast in merit?
Who will uphold this treasury of the Dharma?
Who today will take up the great crown
and bind it on his own head with his own hand?

Then the Blessed One (the Buddha) again spoke to the venerable Ānanda in verse:

Ānanda, look at these five hundred gathered companions,
who sit before me with joyful minds
and declare in words,
"May we obtain this Dharma!"
"When shall we become like this?" they say,
and they gaze at me as well.
"We shall uphold this supreme awakening,"
they declare in words, seated before me.
In the future, in the latter time, when such a time has come,
in order to proclaim these sūtras,
these eight wise ones and the others,
these five hundred, have also risen up.
One should have faith in this and understand it:
here my wisdom is unobstructed.
It is not before a single Victor
that these have risen with palms joined.
Recall the past: in former births
fully eighty thousand buddhas appeared,
and in order to proclaim these teachings,
before them too these eight and the others rose.
And far earlier even than that,
there appeared eighty thousand myriads of koṭis of buddhas
of pure moral discipline and great renown;
from them too they received this vast Dharma.
My vast scriptures as well
they seek out and fully uphold.
With minds joyful and delighted beyond compare,
they urge many toward the mind of awakening.

In the latter time, when I have passed into nirvāṇa,
they will spread all the relics far and wide,
and holding well to these awakenings of the buddhas,
they will write them down and place them in caskets,
and deposit the sūtras in stūpas, in the earth, in rocks and mountains,
and in the hands of gods and likewise of nāgas.
Having done so, they will depart
to take up a way of life like that of the gods.
In the latter time, they will leave
all their various families and descend again,
and holding well to these awakenings of the buddhas,
they will act in accordance with their aspiration.
Longing for this Dharma, they will go from land to land
and will find this excellent sūtra.
Having found it, their minds supremely joyful,
they will give it to many people, relying on nothing in return.
Giving up their bodies and even their lives,
those wise ones are insatiable in their longing for the Dharma.
At odds with every opponent,
they will give the vast and excellent Dharma.

Apart from all these five hundred
who have risen up before me,
there is no one at all
who will uphold this sūtra, bear it, and recite it.
Eight monks, and likewise in the north,
many who take it up, uphold the Dharma and delight in it,
who long for the Dharma, benefit the world and are loving,
will appear in order to receive it.
Those who uphold it there most extensively,
and who appear not relying on gain or fine words,
are these eight and the others, the five hundred monks,
all of them, complete.

These monks, nuns, laymen,
and laywomen, wise and free of avarice,
who rise up and uphold the Dharma in the latter time,
will all become Victors, supreme among humans,
endowed with every inconceivable quality,
golden in color, bearing the marks of a hundred merits.
For others' benefit, with compassion, they will free many beings,
bestowing happiness and dispelling all afflictions.
After they die and pass on from here,
they will never go to the wrong and fallen states.
In all their births they will gather together
and will please those supreme among humans.
They have abandoned the eight unfree states;
thereby all the lower realms are abandoned;
thereby they have gathered merit beyond measure;
the limit of their qualities cannot be told.

In this buddha-field they will bring delight.
Gathering before whoever becomes a buddha,
out of compassion, for the sake of beings,
they seek the unsurpassed path of peace and show reverence.
Gathered together in a single eon,
having made offerings to the Lord of two-legged beings,
for the sake of unsurpassed and stainless awakening
they will attain the noble patience free of entanglement.
And then too, likewise upholding the Dharma,
in the latter time and likewise in the middle time,
having lived this holy life there,
they will please the Buddha Amitāyus.

However many makers of light, who work for the world,
appear in this Fortunate Eon,
these too will uphold their way of Dharma:
heroes abiding throughout the three times.
To the many koṭis of buddhas yet to come,
inconceivable and beyond measure,
they will make vast offerings,
and will please the buddhas for the sake of awakening.
They will touch the peace of awakening;
gathering there, they will show reverence.
Until many myriads of eons have passed,
for that long they will be the very seed.

This householder Bhadrapāla,
Ratnākara, a very mine of jewels,
Susārthavāha, Guhagupta, and the brahmin youth
will see buddhas as many as the sands of the Ganges.
They, and even beyond them,
will uphold the Dharma of many koṭis of buddhas;
the measure of those many koṭis of eons
cannot be told, nor can its end be found.

Those great beings who, even in a dream,
Hear the names and the methods of these heroes
Will become great victors.
Those who, having seen them or else having heard of them,
Take delight in them or else have faith,
Will all, without doubt, attain awakening;
What need is there to speak of those who serve and honor them?
They uphold this inconceivable Dharma;
Their names, and likewise their life spans, are inconceivable;
Their radiance is beyond conception, their qualities inconceivable;
Their wisdom and gnosis are likewise inconceivable.
Seeking unsurpassed awakening,
To the buddhas of the past, pure in moral discipline,
Who appeared, inconceivable, as many as the sands of the Ganges,
They offered vast gifts as well.
Even if many millions of buddhas were to describe it,
Their merit would have no measure at all.
Their sons who are like them, and their friends as well,
Will attain awakening; of this there is no doubt at all.
Ānanda, those who take up, hold, or read this sūtra,
And these, the full five hundred,
Without any doubt give rise to joy in it.
Ānanda, if any make firm their aspiration
To seek out this sūtra,
Give up sleep, and set about diligence,
It will not be hard for them to find this samādhi.
Having taken up sūtras such as this,
They gain the supreme gain and leave the lower realms behind;
They make the Sugata's teaching fruitful,
And become entirely free of all unfavorable states.

This concludes the thirteenth chapter, on Generating Enthusiasm, from the Samādhi Requested by Bhadrapāla.

Then the bodhisattva great being Bhadrapāla, the bodhisattva great being the Licchavi youth Ratnākara, the bodhisattva great being the merchant's son Guhagupta, the bodhisattva great being the brahmin youth Naladatta, the bodhisattva great being the son of good family Susīma, the bodhisattva great being the householder Susārthavāha, the bodhisattva great being Indradatta, and the bodhisattva great being Varuṇa, together with those five hundred or so bodhisattvas, offered five hundred pairs of cotton cloth to the body of the Blessed One because the teaching had been so well spoken. They also strewed jewels over the Blessed One, and they also requested the Blessed One to accept their service and honor.

Then the Blessed One, with a talk on the Dharma, thoroughly delighted those five hundred or so bodhisattva great beings led by Bhadrapāla, inspired them, roused them, and gladdened them. With joyful minds, faultless minds, gentle minds, eager minds, faithful minds, and minds free of obscurations, they sat before the Blessed One with their palms joined.

Then the bodhisattva great being Bhadrapāla said to the Blessed One, "Blessed One, possessing how many qualities does a bodhisattva great being attain this samādhi?"

When he had asked this, the Blessed One said to the bodhisattva great being Bhadrapāla, "Bhadrapāla, possessing four qualities, a bodhisattva great being attains this samādhi. What are the four? They are as follows: not relying on the secret words of other non-Buddhists (tīrthikas); not being attached to the qualities of sensual desire; not being outdone in the qualities of ascetic practice and in frugality of requisites; and disparaging the places of birth among the destinies of existence. Bhadrapāla, possessing these four qualities, a bodhisattva great being attains this samādhi.

"Bhadrapāla, whatever bodhisattva keeps this samādhi, reads it, writes it down, holds it, masters it, and also teaches it correctly and extensively to others, Bhadrapāla, you should know that there are five hundred qualities which that bodhisattva will obtain in this very life. What are the five hundred? Bhadrapāla, it is like this: just as a body that abides in loving-kindness cannot be harmed by poison, cannot be harmed by weapons, does not die by water, is not burned by fire, and kings looking for an opening find none, in just the same way, Bhadrapāla, the bodhisattva who holds this samādhi cannot be harmed by poison, cannot be harmed by weapons, is not killed by water, is not burned by fire, and kings looking for an opening find none.

"Moreover, Bhadrapāla, if the conflagration at the end of the eon should occur, and the bodhisattva who holds this samādhi were to leap into that mass of fire, that mass of fire would be extinguished, just as, for example, a great pot of water extinguishes a mass of fire.

"Bhadrapāla, apart from the maturation of past karma, that a king, thieves, fire, water, people, nāgas, vetālas, yakṣas, rākṣasas, lions, tigers, dogs, foxes, wolves, humans, nonhumans, pretas, or kumbhāṇḍas should create obstacles for the bodhisattva who holds this samādhi, obstructing his body, his life, his alms bowl, his robes, his celibate conduct, his teaching, his recitation, his meditative concentration, or his proper attention, is impossible and out of the question; there is no possibility of it.

"Bhadrapāla, apart from the maturation of past karma, that eye disease, ear disease, nose disease, tongue disease, bodily disease, mental disease, or any form of illness other than these should strike the monk who holds this samādhi and put an end to that bodhisattva's life is impossible and out of the question; there is no possibility of it."

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, the gods protect the bodhisattva who upholds this samādhi. The nāgas also protect him. The yakṣas also protect him. The gandharvas also protect him. Humans also protect him. Nonhumans also protect him. The asuras also protect him. The garuḍas also protect him. The kinnaras also protect him. The mahoragas also protect him. The Four Great Kings, Śakra (Indra), lord of the gods, Brahmā, lord of the Sahā world, and the god Susīma also protect him. Other gods of great splendor, of very great splendor, also protect him. The bodhisattva great beings also protect him. The buddhas, the blessed ones, also protect the bodhisattva who upholds this samādhi.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, that bodhisattva great being becomes dear to the gods, the nāgas, the yakṣas, the asuras, the garuḍas, the kinnaras, the mahoragas, Śakra, the Brahmās, the Great Kings, the bodhisattvas, and the buddhas, the blessed ones.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, the gods proclaim the praises of that bodhisattva great being. The nāgas also proclaim his praises. The yakṣas also proclaim his praises. Humans also proclaim his praises. Nonhumans also proclaim his praises. The asuras, garuḍas, kinnaras, and mahoragas also proclaim his praises. Śakra, Brahmā, and the Great Kings also proclaim his praises. The bodhisattvas and the buddhas, the blessed ones, also proclaim the praises of that bodhisattva great being.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, the gods wish to see the bodhisattva who upholds this samādhi. The nāgas also wish to see him. The yakṣas also wish to see him. The gandharvas also wish to see him. The kinnaras also wish to see him. Humans also wish to see him. Nonhumans also wish to see him. The asuras, garuḍas, and mahoragas also wish to see him. The bodhisattvas and the buddhas, the blessed ones, show their faces to the bodhisattva who upholds this samādhi, even if only in dreams. They also call him by name.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, the bodhisattvas also wish to see the bodhisattva who upholds this samādhi. The buddhas, the blessed ones, also wish to behold him.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, the gods come to see the bodhisattva who upholds this samādhi. The nāgas also come to see him. The yakṣas, asuras, garuḍas, kinnaras, mahoragas, and humans also come to see him. Śakra, lord of the gods, Brahmā, lord of the Sahā world, and the god Susīma also come to see him.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, sūtras that have not been taught and not been heard will resound for the bodhisattva who upholds this samādhi, and he will hear their voice, even if only in dreams.

Bhadrapāla, I could speak in praise and proclaim the good qualities of the bodhisattva who upholds this samādhi, who upholds this samādhi, masters it, keeps it, reads it, writes it out, or teaches it, for an eon or for more than an eon; what need is there even to mention one who puts it into practice?

Then, at that time, the Blessed One spoke these verses:

To describe in full the qualities
Of a bodhisattva who teaches
This samādhi spoken by the Sugata
Is like taking one grain from sands as many as the Ganges's.

Whoever upholds this samādhi
Cannot be harmed by fire or by weapons;
Thieves will do him no harm,
And kings will not be unkind to him.

Though venomous snakes, enraged and terrifying,
Should harm people with malicious minds,
For one who enters this samādhi,
By his power they become free of venom.

Humans, nāgas, yakṣas, and rākṣasas,
Hostile adversaries, enraged and terrifying,
Can never withstand the power
Of those who enter this samādhi.

The fierce beasts of prey that dwell in the wilderness,
Wolves, jackals, and likewise lions and tigers,
Will become companions and servants
To one who lives in a wilderness dwelling.

Though yakṣas, piśācas, and terrifying stealers of vitality
Should harm people with malicious minds,
For one who enters this samādhi,
By his power they are utterly cowed.

Whoever teaches this samādhi
Has no disease of the ears and no disease of the body;
His faculties of sight will not decline;
His words will be clear, and he will speak with eloquence.

Whoever enters this samādhi
Will never have hell or the lower realms;
Disease will not strike his body,
And he will never have fear of untimely downfall.

If he recites it and teaches it to others,
Gods, nāgas, kumbhāṇḍas,
Asuras, and mahoragas protect him,
And even those who would harm him grow devoted.

If he recites it and teaches it to others,
Gods, humans, nāgas, asuras,
Yakṣas, and kinnaras loudly proclaim his praise,
And the buddhas too praise him like an only son.

If he recites it and teaches it to others,
No doubt about the Dharma will arise in him;
No doubt about awakening will ever arise in him;
And none will appear whose form is equal to his.

The one who recites this samādhi,
Even when kings are in turmoil, and likewise beings,
And a wilderness of famine has arisen,
Will suffer neither harm nor hunger.

The one who recites this samādhi,
Even when Māra draws close to beings,
Is not afraid; not a hair on his body stirs;
His good qualities become inconceivable.

Harms, misfortunes, and afflictions,
However many I have described,
Except for the ripening of former karma,
Can find no hold upon his body.

In the final age of decline, whoever has these vast sūtras come into his hands,
place him before you as you would an eldest son;
praise him, rise to greet him, and speak pleasant words to him as well.
Be heedful, and set about your efforts with diligence;
act in harmony with one another, and practice the Dharma too.
Whoever holds this, reads it and teaches it
is explaining these things for your sake.

This concludes the fourteenth chapter, In Praise of the Preacher of the Dharma, from the Samādhi Requested by Bhadrapāla.

Fascicle five.

Again the Blessed One said to the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla: "Bhadrapāla, I directly recall that in the past, countless, vast, immeasurable, inconceivable and incalculable eons ago, at that time, on that occasion, there appeared in the world a tathāgata, an arhat, a perfectly complete buddha called King of Bliss, perfect in knowledge and conduct, the Sugata, knower of the world, unsurpassed guide of beings to be tamed, teacher of gods and humans, a buddha, a blessed one.

"Bhadrapāla, at that time there was a merchant's son called Bhadradatta. Surrounded by twenty thousand living beings and leading them, he went together with them to where the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha King of Bliss was. When he arrived, he bowed his head at the feet of the Blessed One, the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha King of Bliss, circled him three times, and sat down to one side. Having sat down to one side, the merchant's son Bhadradatta asked the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha King of Bliss about this samādhi in full.

"Bhadrapāla, then the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha King of Bliss, knowing the superior intention of the merchant's son Bhadradatta, taught this samādhi extensively and explained it correctly.

"Bhadrapāla, then the merchant's son Bhadradatta, having heard this samādhi, retained it. Having retained it, he meditated on it. Having meditated on it, he shaved off his hair and beard under the Blessed One, the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha King of Bliss, put on saffron robes, went forth from home into homelessness, and practiced the pure life. For eighty thousand years he remained meditating on this samādhi. He retained everything he had heard from the Blessed One, the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha King of Bliss, and he also retained everything he had heard from the tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly complete buddhas of the past, so that he came to possess inconceivable learning.

"Then at another time, when his life came to an end and his body broke up and he died, he was reborn in a happy destiny, in the heavenly world, sharing the same fortune as the gods of the Heaven of the Thirty-Three. Having been reborn there, in that very world-system he pleased a second tathāgata, called Lightning God. In that same eon he was born into a royal family like a great sāla tree, and having gone out from that royal family like a great sāla tree, he went forth under the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha Lightning God, and for eighty thousand years he meditated on this samādhi and lived the pure life.

"He then pleased a third tathāgata, arhat, perfectly complete buddha, called King of Light Rays. In that same eon he was born into a brahmin family like a great sāla tree, and having gone out from that brahmin family like a great sāla tree, he went forth under the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha King of Light Rays, and for eighty-four thousand years he meditated on this samādhi and lived the pure life. Later, eight eons after that, the merchant's son Bhadradatta fully awakened to unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening.

"Bhadrapāla, if you should have doubts, misgivings or uncertainty, thinking that the one who was the merchant's son called Bhadradatta at that time, on that occasion, was someone else, you should not regard it in that way, Bhadrapāla. Why is that? It was the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha Dīpaṃkara who at that time, on that occasion, was the merchant's son called Bhadradatta.

"Bhadrapāla, see how the merchant's son Bhadradatta sought the Dharma, desired the Dharma and had aspiration for the Dharma, and for that reason swiftly and fully awakened to unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening. Bhadrapāla, see how this samādhi of the bodhisattva, in which the buddhas of the present stand face to face, gives rise to the wisdom of a buddha in bodhisattvas, great beings, how their learning becomes like an ocean, and how greatly beneficial it proves to be.

"Therefore, Bhadrapāla, you should take up this samādhi. You should master it. You should write it down in letters. You should keep it. You should read it. Having taken it up and mastered it, you should explain it correctly and extensively to others as well. You should teach it. You should recite it. You should also strive in the practice of meditating on it. Why is that? Because, Bhadrapāla, when this samādhi is relied upon again and again, meditated upon and cultivated much, it leads to the attainment of the wisdom of a buddha, and one will attain the wisdom of a tathāgata, self-arisen wisdom, the wisdom of omniscience, inconceivable wisdom, wisdom equal to the unequaled, unsurpassed wisdom and wisdom beyond which there is nothing higher; apart from attaining these, one will not attain any other dharma.

"Again, Bhadrapāla, whatever one speaking truly would call 'that which has become the eye,' 'that which gives birth to bodhisattvas,' or 'that which gives rise to the qualities of a buddha,' Bhadrapāla, it is of this samādhi that one speaking truly would say those things.

"Again, Bhadrapāla, whatever one speaking truly would call 'the family of the Buddha,' 'the family of the Dharma,' 'the family of the Saṅgha,' 'the ground of the Buddha,' 'the family of learning,' 'learning like an ocean,' 'the accumulation of learning,' 'the basis of learning,' 'the accumulation of good qualities,' 'the generating of patience,'

"It generates loving-kindness; it attains compassion; it is the Dharma that generates awakening." Bhadrapāla, those who speak in this way speak as one who speaks correctly about this samādhi.

Again, Bhadrapāla, those who would say, as one who speaks correctly, "It is the Dharma that dispels the darkness of the world with its gods, humans and asuras; it is the Dharma that makes a great illumination," speak as one who speaks correctly about this samādhi.

Again, Bhadrapāla, those who would say, as one who speaks correctly, "It is the Dharma that generates the ten powers of the Tathāgata, the four fearlessnesses of the Tathāgata, the four correct discernments, and the eighteen unshared qualities of a buddha," speak as one who speaks correctly about this samādhi.

Bhadrapāla, see how vast is this samādhi of bodhisattvas, the direct presence of the buddhas of the present. Through it bodhisattva great beings, while remaining right here, see the buddhas of other world systems, hear the Dharma, and see the Saṅgha. Therefore, Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva great being who wishes to accomplish this precious samādhi should make firm his discipline in the four applications of mindfulness.

Bhadrapāla, how should a bodhisattva great being make firm his discipline in the four applications of mindfulness? Bhadrapāla, here a bodhisattva great being should contemplate the body as body, but should not examine the conceptual thoughts connected with the body. He should contemplate feelings as feelings, but should not examine the conceptual thoughts connected with feelings. He should contemplate the mind as mind, but should not examine the conceptual thoughts connected with the mind. He should contemplate phenomena as phenomena, but should not examine the conceptual thoughts connected with phenomena.

Bhadrapāla, apart from the tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly awakened buddhas, the irreversible bodhisattva great beings, and the śrāvakas who have realized with the body, who would have faith in this samādhi? And why is that, Bhadrapāla? Because all childish ordinary beings are confused about this samādhi of bodhisattvas, the direct presence of the buddhas of the present.

And why is that, Bhadrapāla? One should attend to those very phenomena and look at those very phenomena. One should also attend to the buddhas, the blessed ones, look at the tathāgatas, and listen to the Dharma, yet one should not cling to them. And why is that, Bhadrapāla? All these phenomena are empty by their intrinsic nature and pure by their intrinsic nature; from the very beginning they are utterly at peace.

Bhadrapāla, because all these phenomena depend on an object of reference, they cannot be referred to as objects. All these phenomena are void of that very attention. Because all these phenomena cannot be apprehended by their intrinsic nature, they cannot be grasped. Because all these phenomena are like space, they are unattached. Because all these phenomena are free of the notion of a self and of a sentient being, they are utterly pure. Because all these phenomena arise under the power of causes, they are free of afflictions. Because in all these phenomena no life force and no person can be found, they cannot be counted. Because all these phenomena are luminous by their intrinsic nature, they are equal to nirvāṇa. Because in all these phenomena no entity can be found, they are near at hand.

Bhadrapāla, if any son or daughter of noble family wishes to cultivate this samādhi, that person should by all means see the buddhas, the blessed ones, and cultivate the factor of awakening that is mindfulness, endowed with recollection of the Buddha. He should also hear the Dharma and cultivate the factor of awakening that is discernment of phenomena among phenomena. Not apprehending a self and not being conceited about phenomena, he should in this way, through engagement with the aspect of signs, enter into the engagement with signlessness.

And why is that, Bhadrapāla? Because one who perceives things as entities will not see the Buddha. One who perceives phenomena as phenomena will not see the Dharma. One who hopes for a karmic ripening will not perfect giving. One who delights in meditation will not have pure moral discipline. One who is stingy with the Dharma will not become learned. One who relies on persons will not attain complete nirvāṇa. One who delights in idle talk will not see solitude. One who delights in a dwelling place will not attain the fruit. One with attachment will not see faults. One who delights in malice will not be certain of patience. One who is hostile and resentful will not see benefits.

A son or daughter of noble family belonging to the śrāvaka vehicle will not directly realize this samādhi of bodhisattvas, the direct presence of the buddhas of the present, through which patience is attained. In a bodhisattva stinginess does not arise. One who has an object of reference will not cultivate emptiness. A lazy person will not attain clear realization. One who relies on desires will not achieve calm abiding. One who relies on supports will not accomplish meditation.

Therefore, Bhadrapāla, so that this samādhi may by no means disappear, I entrust it to the world with its gods.

When the Blessed One taught this discourse on the Dharma, one hundred and eighty billion sons of the gods who dwell in the desire realm and the form realm generated the mind set on unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. Eight thousand living beings as well, gods, humans and asuras, generated the mind set on unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening.

All of them, too, will have their minds liberated in the presence of tathāgatas as numerous as the sands of the Ganges River. Those who first generated the mind set on unsurpassed awakening will, though under different names, all with the same lifespan, fully awaken to unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. If that is so, Bhadrapāla, what need is there to mention those who pleased me when I was formerly practicing the conduct of a bodhisattva? What need is there even to mention those who were my companions when I was formerly practicing the conduct of a bodhisattva? They will swiftly and fully awaken to unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening.

Bhadrapāla, when this teaching was being given, the vision of wisdom arose in countless beings, and the minds of eight hundred monks were liberated from the defilements, without further grasping.

Then, at that time, the Blessed One (the Buddha) spoke these verses:

Those who come to uphold this samādhi
Have wisdom without the slightest limit.
Their discipline is boundless, free of dust and stain;
Their discipline is pure, and their minds are wholly pure.

Those who come to uphold this samādhi
Will never in any way decline in wisdom.
Having heard the samādhi, they will never decline;
Like the moon, they display every quality.

Those who come to uphold this samādhi
Praise buddhas who are inconceivable.
They see the Dharma, inconceivable as well,
And inconceivable gods protect them.
Inconceivable buddhas appeared in the past,
And they taught the inconceivable Dharma.
Whoever comes to uphold this samādhi
Has seen them all and honored them.

Whoever comes to uphold this samādhi:
Those Victors who free beings from all suffering,
Who have appeared for the sake of the world and remain,
Those Victors are shown reverence by that person.

To honor with a mind of faith
The many inconceivable buddhas yet to come,
Any bodhisattva who wishes to see them
Should take up this supreme samādhi.

Whoever comes to uphold this samādhi,
Those people's gain is inconceivable.
They have come well into the human world;
They have gone forth well and eaten alms well.

In the time to come, whoever
Holds this supreme samādhi in hand
Has gained the highest gain most excellently,
And has grasped the inconceivable Dharma as well.

This is the fifteenth chapter, on the samādhi requested by Bhadrapāla.

Then the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, rose from his seat, draped his upper robe over one shoulder, and placed his right knee on the ground. Bowing toward the Blessed One with his palms joined, he said to the Blessed One, "Venerable Blessed One, out of compassion, would you accept my meal tomorrow, together with the Saṅgha of monks?"

Out of compassion, the Blessed One consented to the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, by remaining silent.

Then the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, understanding that the Blessed One had consented by remaining silent, bowed his head at the Blessed One's feet, circumambulated him three times, and departed from the Blessed One's presence. He went to where the nun Mahāprajāpatī was, and having arrived, he bowed his head at the feet of the nun Mahāprajāpatī Gautamī and sat down to one side. Having sat down to one side, the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, said to the nun Mahāprajāpatī Gautamī, "Noble lady, out of compassion, would you accept my meal tomorrow, together with the Saṅgha of nuns?"

Out of kindness, the nun Mahāprajāpatī Gautamī consented to the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, by remaining silent.

Then the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, understanding that the nun Mahāprajāpatī Gautamī had consented by remaining silent, bowed his head at the feet of the nun Mahāprajāpatī Gautamī, circumambulated her three times, and departed. Having left, he went to where the Licchavi youth Ratnākara was, and having arrived, he said to the Licchavi youth Ratnākara, "Kinsman, here at Rājagṛha, in the Bamboo Grove at the Kalandaka feeding place, however many laymen have gathered, whether from this Rājagṛha or from other parts of the land, invite them all in my name, together with their retinues, as guests for tomorrow's midday meal."

Then the Licchavi youth Ratnākara announced to all the laymen, however many, who had gathered in that assembly, "The bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, invites you, together with your retinues, as his guests tomorrow."

Then the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, the Licchavi youth Ratnākara, the householder Mahāsusārthavāha, the merchant's son Guhagupta, the brahmin Naladatta, the householder Varuṇa, the householder Indradatta, and other householders as well rose from their seats. They bowed their heads at the Blessed One's feet, circumambulated the Blessed One three times, and bowed their heads at the feet of the Saṅgha of monks too. Leaving the Bamboo Grove, they went to the great city of Rājagṛha, to Bhadrapāla's house. When they arrived, that very night they prepared abundant delicious food to be chewed, to be eaten, to be savored and to be sipped, for all of them and even for the beggars. They also laid out and cooked food of a hundred flavors.

Why was this? Because great bodhisattvas abide in a vast intention free of contempt, and have entered into an equal attitude toward all beings.

Then Śakra, lord of the gods, Brahmā, lord of the Sahā world, the god Susīma, and the Four Great Kings also conjured up many people. In a way that would stir world-weariness in those householders, they set to work to benefit them toward awakening and to bring them to full maturity.

Then the bodhisattva Bhadrapāla, the Licchavi youth Ratnākara and his companions, and the other householders, each together with their own relatives, thoroughly swept the whole great city of Rājagṛha, every place that needed sweeping. They hung silk tassels, perfumed it with censers of incense, and had scattered flowers strewn about.

Then the bodhisattva great being Bhadrapāla and his relatives adorned the great city of Rājagṛha and prepared a meal of a hundred flavors. When that night had passed, surrounded by his relatives and with them going before him, he went together with them to where the Blessed One was. When he arrived, he bowed his head at the Blessed One's feet, circled the Blessed One three times, and announced that it was midday, saying, "Venerable Blessed One, the time for the meal has come. Venerable Blessed One, the hour for the meal has come. The meal is prepared; please know that now is the time for it."

Then, in the morning, the Blessed One put on his robe and lower garment and took up his alms bowl. Together with the Saṅgha of monks and the Saṅgha of nuns, and together with the assemblies of laymen and laywomen, surrounded by them and with them going before him, he went to the house of the bodhisattva great being Bhadrapāla.

Then the bodhisattva great being Bhadrapāla thought, "How good it would be if the Blessed One were to perform such a miracle that this house of mine would become vast and spacious, like blue beryl, so that all the people of the city could see it, and all this great assembly could sit at their ease!"

Then the Blessed One, knowing with his mind the thought in the mind of that bodhisattva great being, performed just such a miracle: the house of the bodhisattva great being Bhadrapāla became vast and spacious, like blue beryl, so that all the people of the city could see it plainly, and that whole assembly could sit at their ease.

Then the Blessed One entered the house of the bodhisattva great being Bhadrapāla. Having entered, he sat down on the seat prepared for him, together with the Saṅgha of monks and the Saṅgha of nuns and the assemblies of laymen and laywomen.

Then the bodhisattva great being Bhadrapāla, the Licchavi youth Ratnākara, the householder Mahāsusārthavāha, Guhagupta the son of a guild master, the brahmin Naladatta, the householder Indradatta, and the householder Varuṇa saw that the Blessed One was seated, and saw that the Saṅgha of monks, the Saṅgha of nuns, and the assemblies of laymen and laywomen were seated as well. With their own hands they satisfied and served the Buddha and the Saṅgha of monks, the Saṅgha of nuns, and the assemblies of laymen and laywomen with an abundance of fine food, hard foods, soft foods, and things to savor, possessing a hundred flavors. When with their own hands they had satisfied and served them with an abundance of fine food, soft foods, hard foods, and things to savor, possessing a hundred flavors, and saw that the Blessed One had finished his meal, withdrawn his hand, and put away his bowl, they took suitable seats and sat before the Blessed One, facing him.

Then the Blessed One, with a talk on the Dharma, delighted the bodhisattva great being Bhadrapāla, the Licchavi youth Ratnākara, the householder Mahāsusārthavāha, Guhagupta the son of a guild master, the brahmin Naladatta, the householder Indradatta, the householder Varuṇa, and the four assemblies. He led them to take it up, he roused them, and he gladdened them. Having delighted them with a talk on the Dharma, having led them to take it up, having roused them, and having gladdened them, he rose from his seat and departed together with the Saṅgha of monks.

Then the bodhisattva great being Bhadrapāla, having finished attending to the meal, together with his friends, relatives, and kinsmen, surrounded by a full hundred thousand beings and with them going before him, went together with them to where the Blessed One was. When he arrived, he bowed his head at the Blessed One's feet, circled him three times, and sat down to one side. The bodhisattva great beings Ratnākara, Mahāsusārthavāha, Guhagupta, Naladatta, Susīma, Indradatta, and Varuṇa together bowed their heads at the Blessed One's feet, circled him three times, and sat down to one side.

Then the bodhisattva great being Bhadrapāla, seeing that the whole assembly was seated, said this to the Blessed One: "Venerable Blessed One, possessing how many qualities will bodhisattva great beings obtain this bodhisattva samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present? Possessing how many will they listen to it with attentive ears, retain it so that the mind is not distracted, and not forget it, and having retained it, also obtain it?"

When he had said this, the Blessed One spoke these words to the bodhisattva great being Bhadrapāla: "Bhadrapāla, possessing five qualities, bodhisattva great beings will obtain this bodhisattva samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present.

lend their ears and listen, hold it in mind so that the mind is not distracted, and do not forget it; and having held it in mind, they will attain it.

Bhadrapāla, what are the five? Here, the bodhisattva great being has patience toward the profound Dharma. He has patience toward the dharma of non-arising, which knows no exhaustion and is free from exhaustion, which is inexhaustible and utterly inexhaustible, which is without turbidity and has wholly passed beyond turbidity, which is stainless and free from stain, which is without dust, free from dust, beyond dust, and free from all dust. Bhadrapāla, if he is endowed with this first quality, the bodhisattva great being attains this samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, the bodhisattva great being disparages all formations. Apart from relying on awakening, he does not wish for any place of birth. He wishes to behold the buddhas, the blessed ones. He does not rely on any of the shrines of the non-Buddhists (tīrthikas), nor on any of the mantras of the non-Buddhists. He does not wish for the enjoyments of the senses. He delights in pure conduct: having abandoned the practice of sexual union, he does not rely on desire even in mere thought, let alone in the joining of the two organs. Bhadrapāla, if he is endowed with this second quality, the bodhisattva great being attains this samādhi.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, the bodhisattva great being cultivates the immeasurable states of mind. His mind is very well collected, and he has come to have a mind free of malice. His mind is impartial toward all sentient beings, and he gathers all sentient beings by the four means of gathering. What are the four? Generosity, pleasant speech, beneficial conduct, and consistency of aims; these are the four. Bhadrapāla, if he is endowed with these three qualities, the bodhisattva great being attains this samādhi.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, the bodhisattva great being is endowed with great loving-kindness, and he practices great compassion, great joy, and great equanimity. He does not reproach a monk or a nun who has committed a transgression, and since he is by nature accepting, he is patient. He is respectful and deferential toward his preceptor and his master, and regards them as the Teacher. He himself does not speak of a self as a self; he does not speak of a sentient being, does not speak of a life-force, does not speak of a person, does not speak of one who is nourished, does not speak of a man, does not speak of one born of Manu, and does not speak of a son of Manu. And he also urges others toward this Dharma. Bhadrapāla, if he is endowed with these four qualities, the bodhisattva great being attains this samādhi.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, the bodhisattva great being diligently practices the Dharma in accordance with the Dharma. His conduct of body is completely pure, his conduct of speech is completely pure, his conduct of mind is completely pure, his view is completely pure, and his livelihood is completely pure; and he is very learned. He is skilled in words whose intent is in harmony with the buddhas, the blessed ones. Bhadrapāla, if he is endowed with these five qualities, that bodhisattva great being attains this samādhi. Bhadrapāla, if he is endowed with those five qualities, the bodhisattva great being attains this samādhi.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, if the bodhisattva great being is endowed with five other qualities, he attains this samādhi. What are the five? Bhadrapāla, here the bodhisattva great being is a master of giving who practices giving. His mind is without grudging and without stinginess; he gives freely, with open hand, delighting in distributing gifts. He gives away all his possessions without hope of reward in their ripening. He has tender love for sentient beings, he is free from regret, and having given a gift he does not regret it. Bhadrapāla, if he is endowed with this first quality, the bodhisattva great being attains at the very least the hearing of this samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, the bodhisattva great being is a master of giving who practices giving, in that he gives the unsurpassed gift of the Dharma. He bestows the unsurpassed gift of the Dharma spoken by the Tathāgata: words and letters spoken with perfect certainty, unobstructed words and letters, definitive words that conceal nothing, which teach the profound way of the ultimate. Abiding in that, he becomes perfect in patience and perfect in gentleness. Even when others scold and revile him, he does not become angry, does not insult them, does not harm them, does not act out of bewilderment, does not become disheartened, does not grow full of wrath, and is fearless. Bhadrapāla, if he is endowed with this second quality, the bodhisattva great being attains this samādhi.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, the bodhisattva great being, having heard this samādhi, should take it up so that the true Dharma may long endure and so that it may spread. He should master it, keep it, and read it; he should also explain it correctly and extensively to others; he should write it down in a volume and keep it; and he should not be stingy with the Dharma. Bhadrapāla, if he is endowed with these three qualities, the bodhisattva great being attains this samādhi.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, the bodhisattva great being is without envy and without resentment, free from torpor and sleep, and free from the obscurations; he does not praise himself and does not disparage others. Bhadrapāla, if he is endowed with these four qualities, the bodhisattva great being attains this samādhi.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, the bodhisattva great being is endowed with faith and has abundant serene confidence. He is respectful and deferential toward the elder, the middle-aged, and the young. He is grateful, and he repays what has been done for him; he regards even a small kindness done to him as a great favor, let alone many. He abides in truthful speech and does not speak otherwise. Bhadrapāla, if he is endowed with those five qualities, the bodhisattva great being attains this samādhi.

Then, at that time, the Blessed One (the Buddha) spoke these verses:

One who develops acceptance of the profound Dharma,
Who finds fault with every realm of rebirth,
And who desires none of the six destinies:
Such a one swiftly attains the samādhi.

One who does not rely on any opposing disputants,
Who will not even listen to whispered talk, much less hold to it,
And who has grown weary of the five pleasures of the senses:
Such a one swiftly attains the samādhi.

One who is pure in discipline and dwells in celibate conduct,
Who never gives rise to thoughts of women,
Who has abandoned desire and become a child of the Sugata:
Such a one swiftly attains the samādhi.

One who gives gifts without hoping for their ripened fruit,
Who gives without clinging and, having given, is not pained,
Who, having given, makes not the slightest aspiration
Except to touch the wisdom of the buddhas,
Who gives out of compassion for beings and, having given,
Feels no sorrow and no regret,
And who always abides in giving, gentleness and restraint:
Such a one will find this samādhi.

Whoever, as an unstinting patron, gives,
Giving gifts that are excellent and pleasing,
And having given, is glad at heart:
Such a one will find this samādhi.

Those who uphold the excellent sūtras
Spoken by the Sugata, profound and peaceful,
And who become patrons of the Dharma:
Such a one will find this samādhi.

One who always abides in the Dharma,
Who abides in patience and gentleness, free of jealousy,
And who does not become angry when others are angry:
Such a one will find this samādhi.

Those who wish for this samādhi
And, so that this Dharma may long endure,
Teach it to others, write it down and preserve it:
Such a one will find this samādhi.

One who never becomes stingy with the Dharma,
Who does not teach for the sake of gain and honor,
But only to become a child of the Sugata:
Such a one will find this Dharma.

One without jealousy, who has fully cast off the obscurations,
Who is free of sleep and free of all torment,
Who does not praise himself or disparage others:
For him the perception of no-self is not hard to find.

One endowed with faith, without deceit or guile,
Who has faith in the Buddha, the Dharma and the Saṅgha,
Who is grateful and protects what has been done for him:
For him it is not in the least hard to find.

One who always speaks truthful words,
And who never lets a good deed go to waste,
Whether it be small, or slight, or great, or fine:
For such a seeker of the Dharma it will not be hard to find.

For any person who upholds this Dharma,
Guarding pure discipline and what has been done,
If even awakening is not hard to find,
What need is there to speak of this peaceful samādhi?

This was the sixteenth chapter of the Samādhi Requested by Bhadrapāla.

"Bhadrapāla, I clearly recall that in the past, as soon as I saw the Tathāgata Dīpaṃkara, I obtained this samādhi from him. As soon as I had obtained this samādhi, in that very instant immeasurable, countless buddhas, blessed ones, appeared before my eyes. I heard them teaching the sacred Dharma, and I retained all of it just as I had heard it. Those buddhas, those blessed ones, prophesied to me: 'Young brahmin, in a future time you will become the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the Perfectly Complete Buddha called Śākyamuni, perfect in knowledge and conduct, the Sugata, the knower of the world, the unsurpassed guide of beings to be tamed, the teacher of gods and humans, a buddha, a blessed one.'

"Therefore, Bhadrapāla, you should train in this Dharma, which leaves behind both noble ones and childish beings and is free of all signs. Then those of you who abide in this samādhi will attain unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening without difficulty."

Then, at that time, the Blessed One spoke these verses:

Having seen the Buddha Dīpaṃkara,
I cultivated the samādhi,
And having seen many buddhas,
I also attained the supreme Dharma.

Just as those who have made merit
Accomplish their good intentions,
You too, practice the Dharma,
And you will attain supreme awakening.

The inconceivable koṭis of buddhas,
Without end and without limit,
Who were seeking peaceful awakening,
Whom I honored in the past:
I found that awakening
And fully turned the wheel of Dharma.
Those I have established in awakening
Are many koṭis of beings.
Gods, nāgas and yakṣas,
Gandharvas and kinnaras,
Crying, "How wonderful, the Buddha is inconceivable!"
Make offerings to me with their offerings.

Whatever wise people there may be
Who wish to rely on the Buddha's Dharma
Should show earnest aspiration
In order to become greatly learned.

Thirty koṭis of buddhas,
Supreme among humans, Śākya Lion,
Who were seeking peaceful awakening,
Whom I honored in the past:
Those inconceivable buddhas
Did not then give me prophecy,
For I had not come to abide
In wisdom that has no reference point.

Know that, in brief, it was likewise
With the foremost buddhas, the protectors,
Many koṭis upon koṭis,
Whom I honored in the past:
Though the tathāgatas, knowing my mind,
Gave me prophecy,
I had not come to abide
In the inconceivable wisdom of the buddhas.

Therefore, make excellent effort
Toward the ultimate,
And train as well in the supreme wisdom
Of the buddhas, which is inconceivable.

Having trained in this sūtra,
In the domain of the protectors of the world,
One comes to realize unsurpassed, supreme wisdom,
Inconceivable.

How wonderful! Knowing unsurpassed wisdom
And the inconceivable wisdom of the buddhas,
Bhadrapāla's mind is joyful and delighted,
And filled with reverence.
Saying, "In the future we too
Will become later holders of the Dharma,"
Those five hundred, in their full number,
Also became joyful at heart and reverent.

This was the seventeenth chapter of the Samādhi Requested by Bhadrapāla.

The sixth fascicle.

When he had said this, the bodhisattva mahāsattva Bhadrapāla said to the Blessed One, "Venerable Blessed One, how should a bodhisattva cultivate this samādhi?"

The Blessed One said, "Bhadrapāla, if any son of good family or daughter of good family wishes to cultivate this samādhi, they should not dwell on form. They should not dwell on feeling, on mind, or on phenomena.

They should not abide in birth. They should not abide in any phenomena at all. They should not abide in emptiness, in signlessness, or in wishlessness. They should not abide in loving-kindness.

Bhadrapāla, what then is samādhi? It is attending earnestly to those phenomena, but not attending to them wrongly.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, consider the bodhisattva who dwells contemplating the body as body and does not conceptualize the conceptual thought bound up with the body, and who dwells contemplating feeling, mind, and phenomena as phenomena and does not conceptualize the conceptual thought bound up with phenomena. That bodhisattva will attain this samādhi. And why is that? Bhadrapāla, when a bodhisattva great being dwells contemplating the body as body and does not conceptualize the conceptual thought bound up with the body, and dwells contemplating feeling, mind, and phenomena as phenomena and does not conceptualize the conceptual thought bound up with phenomena, then no phenomena at all are apprehended. Because no phenomena are apprehended, he does not think and does not conceptualize. Bhadrapāla, one who does not think and does not conceptualize sees no phenomenon whatsoever. Bhadrapāla, that in which no phenomenon whatsoever is seen is to be known as unobscured wisdom. Bhadrapāla, that unobscured wisdom, Bhadrapāla, is what is called samādhi.

Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva who possesses this samādhi sees immeasurable, countless buddhas. He also hears the sublime Dharma, and having heard it, he fully comprehends it. He sees the unobscured wisdom of liberation of those tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly complete buddhas, and he also attains unimpeded wisdom.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, the bodhisattva dwells contemplating the body as body, and having so dwelt, he sees no phenomenon whatsoever. Having not seen, he does not think and does not conceptualize, and yet he is neither blind nor deaf. In the same way he dwells contemplating feeling, mind, and phenomena as phenomena, and having so dwelt, he sees no phenomenon whatsoever. Having not seen, he becomes free of reliance. Not relying on anything, he cultivates the path. Because he has cultivated the path, he has no doubt about phenomena. Being without doubt, he comes to see the buddhas. And having seen the buddhas, liberation comes about, because no phenomena arise.

And why is that? Bhadrapāla, if a bodhisattva engages in the perception of phenomena, that very thing becomes a view of it as his object. That very thing becomes a view of existence, a view of a self, a view of a sentient being, a view of a life principle, and a view of a person. That very thing becomes a view of aggregates, a view of elements, a view of sense bases, a view of signs, a view of entities, a view of causes, a view of conditions, and a grasping at objects.

And why is that? Bhadrapāla, the bodhisattva sees all phenomena as empty of intrinsic nature, and all phenomena as signless, ungraspable, and without grasping. Such bodhisattvas do not apprehend any phenomena. They do not form conceits about them. They do not see them. If one asks how they do not apprehend, do not form conceits, and do not see, Bhadrapāla, it is like this. For example, the non-Buddhists (tīrthikas), or the śrāvakas of the non-Buddhists, abide in the perception of a self; they abide in the perception of a sentient being, a life principle, and a person; they abide in the perception of all phenomena; they apprehend phenomena, form conceits about them, and see them. Bhadrapāla, the bodhisattva does not see in that way.

And if one asks, Bhadrapāla, how then the bodhisattva does see, Bhadrapāla, it is like this. For example, just as the tathāgatas, the irreversible bodhisattvas, the pratyekabuddhas, and the śrāvaka arhats see all phenomena, so, Bhadrapāla, should the bodhisattva great being look upon all phenomena. Having seen them, he should not delight in them. Being without delight, he does not become one who indulges in delight; free of delight, the bodhisattva who has completely destroyed delight cultivates this samādhi.

Bhadrapāla, it is like this. For example, just as space is formless, indemonstrable, without abiding, utterly pure, and free of afflictions, so the bodhisattva looks upon all phenomena, and his eye becomes unobstructed with regard to conditioned and unconditioned phenomena. For the bodhisattva whose eye is unobstructed, phenomena become directly present. When he brings them to mind, he sees the tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly complete buddhas like an adorned pillar of gold; like the king of light rays rising on high; like the full moon encircled by stars; like a wheel-turning king surrounded by his mighty host; like Brahmā seated on Brahmā's throne in the Brahmā world; like Śakra (Indra), king of the gods, surrounded by the gods of the Heaven of the Thirty-Three; like a fire blazing on a mountain peak; like a king of physicians dispensing medicine; like the maned lion, king of beasts, who fearlessly terrifies all the jackals; like the supreme goose soaring in the sky; like Sumeru, king of mountains, rising high out of the ocean; like the Himalaya, king of mountains, flourishing with every flower and medicinal herb; like the Cakravāla mountains held up and kept standing by what is born of the son of the wind; like a mass of wind supporting the masses of earth and water; like a mass of water supporting the mass of earth.

Then, at that time, the Blessed One spoke these verses:

The beautiful peak of Sumeru,
Stainless, free of stain, and pure,
Equal to the sky and like it,
Is richly adorned by the gods.

Bhadrapāla, in the same way the bodhisattva great being takes as his object those tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly complete buddhas, radiant and resplendent with majesty and glory throughout all the world systems of the great trichiliocosm; he sees them, and he also hears them teaching the Dharma.

Having heard it, they also retain it, master it, and keep it. When that bodhisattva has arisen from this samādhi, he will teach it and explain it extensively to others, just as he heard the Dharma, just as he retained it, and just as he mastered it.

Bhadrapāla, in this way this samādhi greatly benefits bodhisattvas: it produces many good qualities of both worldly and transcendent dharmas. Therefore, Bhadrapāla, a son or daughter of good family who desires awakening should take up this samādhi, master it, keep it, read it, and write it out. They should explain it extensively to others, teach it, and proclaim it. They should also strive in the practice of meditating on it.

Then the Blessed One (the Buddha) spoke these verses:

Free of taint, free of dust, the stainless Buddha,
Without attachment, is worshipped by many people.
With beaten drums and gongs, the sound of heavenly music,
With hundreds of instruments the Benefactor is paid homage.
They strew him with scattered flowers,
And many people worship the stūpas of his relics.
Divine garlands, perfumes, and various parasols
Are hung up by those who seek this samādhi.

Whoever, having taken his stand in the conventional,
Opens up the vast Dharma, hard to see and magnificent,
And recollects the Victor of unattached wisdom,
Will never fall away from emptiness.

Like the stainless moon or the risen sun,
Or like Brahmā dwelling in the Brahmā world,
He recollects the Victor with a pure mind
And never abandons emptiness.

Just as the king of the gods of the Thirty-Three
Outshines the Thirty-Three by his appearance,
So the Victor outshines the world,
Radiant, resplendent, and brilliant in glory.

As fire spreads through the finest of groves,
And a fire fed with butter blazes with leaping flames,
Sending out hundreds of thousands of rays,
So behold the Victor, abounding in the marks.

Just as a physician who draws out thorns
Treats the sick for their illnesses,
So the Victor, lion among humans, supreme physician,
The Sugata, speaks words suited to each.

He roars the lion's roar to the assembly;
The Buddha, the bull among men, is unafraid before the whole world.
Like the forest-dwelling lion before the foxes,
He overwhelms all opponents.

Just as the supreme kings of swans,
Flying in the sky, are white, pure, and beautiful,
So the Sugata is golden in color,
And the eldest of the Sugata's sons are recollected too.

Just as Himavat, king of mountains, is radiant
And brilliant, or like a king of the kṣatriya caste,
Or like a pillar adorned with various jewels,
So regard the Victor, adorned with the marks.

Unmoving and unbroken, like a vajra,
As the Cakravāla mountains hold back the winds,
So the Sugata upholds the eon and the directions,
Beneficial, loving, and great in Dharma.

Just as the earth rests upon water,
Just as water rests upon wind,
And just as wind rests upon space,
So the Victor abides in all good qualities.

Just as Sumeru, king of mountains of the Thirty-Three,
Is radiant and brilliant, flourishing in every way,
So the Sugata, who is like Sumeru,
Dwells beautiful amid a mass of jewels.

If one recollects the Victor with an unattached mind,
That dustless, pure samādhi, free of darkness,
In which nothing fails to appear, becomes one's eye;
Such a one is free of all perceptions of entities.

If one recollects the Victor with a pure mind,
One is free of dust and stain, and anger is abandoned;
One is free of ignorance, and one's wisdom is pure;
One's dustless wisdom will arise.

If one recollects the Victor of pure moral discipline,
One's wisdom is free of blur and pure;
The view of "I" and self will no longer be found in one,
And perceptions of entities will never arise.

Such a one is free of views about the continuum of existence,
Free of the path of doubt, with wisdom wholly pure;
Never will one have perceptions of form,
And one hears the Dharma, the cool reality of the path of peace.

Earth and water, fire and wind
Have no nature of their own; all are empty.
Having heard the teaching of the supreme Victor,
Such a one gives rise to no perception of entities.

Having cut off all knowing in terms of entities,
And having understood all beings to be empty,
Just as a bird does not cling to the sky,
One's mind will never become attached.

By making images of the supreme Victor,
One abides in the power of miracles, with a sharp mind,
Appears in all worlds throughout the ten directions,
And possesses all immeasurable good qualities.

Though one gives away hands and feet, likewise head and eyes,
Kingdom, horses and oxen, everything,
And all the treasures of the world,
One never gives up the aggregate of moral discipline.

Having generated compassion, to benefit the whole world,
One teaches the precious, foremost Dharma;
Though one abandons all conditioned things without remainder,
One never gives up the mind of awakening.

Monks and children of the Victor who have rightly entered,
Likewise nuns and laymen,
And laywomen endowed with faith and free of attachment,
If they practice recollection, will attain this state.

Whoever upholds this peaceful samādhi,
That son of the Sugata, skilled in samādhi,
Will gain these many good qualities,
And many kinds of unsurpassed states will arise.

This is the eighteenth chapter, the chapter on the samādhi requested by Bhadrapāla.

Therefore, Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva who wishes to explain or to attain this samādhi should, from the very beginning, eliminate perceptions of signs. Having eliminated perceptions of signs, he should not fall into overweening pride. The bodhisattva, having abandoned overweening pride and with a mind free of signs, should receive the transmission of this samādhi, and should not dispute. How then does one become free of dispute? Not rejecting emptiness: this is freedom from dispute. Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva who makes freedom from dispute his chief concern should receive the transmission of this samādhi. Bhadrapāla, if any son or daughter of good family wishes to receive the transmission of this samādhi, they should come to possess ten qualities and then receive the transmission of this samādhi.

What are the ten? They are as follows. Bhadrapāla, a son or daughter of good family who has destroyed pride; who is without jealousy of the gain, honor, and praise of others; who is respectful and deferential; who is grateful and repays kindness; who is not stingy and is free of regret; who is endowed with faith and examines much; who exerts diligence and engages in walking meditation; who goes for alms without looking to invitations as a guest; and who, through inclination toward the profound, should receive the transmission of this samādhi. From whatever son or daughter of good family one wishes to receive the transmission of this samādhi, toward that person one should establish the perception of the Teacher, and so receive the transmission of this samādhi, recite it, and retain it. Bhadrapāla, if a son or daughter of good family is endowed with these ten qualities, they should receive this samādhi.

One who has engaged in this way holds eight qualities. What are the eight? They are as follows: moral discipline that is pure because it is thoroughly purified; view that is pure through being endowed with wisdom; wisdom that is pure because there is no further arising; giving that is perfectly pure because one seeks nothing in return from anyone around one; learning that is pure because one is utterly free of forgetting; diligence that is pure because one attains awakening; the attainment of honor through not depending on it; and not being shaken by any sentient being from unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening. Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva fully holds these eight qualities.

If one asks what the fearlessnesses of the Tathāgata are that a bodhisattva will attain when he enters this samādhi, Bhadrapāla, the fearlessnesses of the Tathāgata that a bodhisattva will attain when he enters this samādhi are these four. What are the four? They are as follows.

I have become perfectly and completely awakened, yet someone might say of me, "This one has not fully awakened to this dharma." I see no ground at all on which anyone in the world, whether an ascetic, a brahmin, a god, Māra, Brahmā, or anyone else, could speak and challenge me in accordance with the Dharma. Because I see no such ground, I abide having attained security, I abide having attained freedom from anxiety, I abide having attained ease, I abide having attained fearlessness. Entering into the assembly, I rightly roar the lion's roar. I turn the wheel of Brahmā, which no one in the world, whether ascetic, brahmin, god, Māra, Brahmā, or anyone else, has turned in accordance with the Dharma. This, Bhadrapāla, is the first of the Tathāgata's fearlessnesses of the tathāgatas, which a bodhisattva will attain when he enters this samādhi.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, my defilements are exhausted, yet someone might say, "His defilements have not been completely exhausted." I see no ground at all on which anyone in the world, whether an ascetic, a brahmin, a god, Māra, Brahmā, or anyone else, could speak and challenge me in accordance with the Dharma. Because I see no such ground, I abide having attained security, I abide having attained freedom from anxiety, I abide having attained ease, I abide having attained fearlessness. Entering into the assembly, I rightly roar the lion's roar. I turn the wheel of Brahmā, which no one in the world, whether ascetic, brahmin, god, Māra, Brahmā, or anyone else, has turned in accordance with the Dharma. This, Bhadrapāla, is the second of the Tathāgata's fearlessnesses of the tathāgatas, which a bodhisattva will attain when he enters this samādhi.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, someone might say of me, "What you have taught to be obstructive things will not be obstructive if one engages in them." I see no ground at all on which anyone in the world, whether an ascetic, a brahmin, a god, Māra, Brahmā, or anyone else, could speak and challenge me in accordance with the Dharma. Because I see no such ground, I abide having attained security, I abide having attained freedom from anxiety, I abide having attained ease, I abide having attained fearlessness. Entering into the assembly, I rightly roar the lion's roar. I turn the wheel of Brahmā, which no one in the world, whether ascetic, brahmin, god, Māra, Brahmā, or anyone else, has turned in accordance with the Dharma. This, Bhadrapāla, is the third of the Tathāgata's fearlessnesses of the tathāgatas, which a bodhisattva will attain when he enters this samādhi.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, concerning the Dharma I have taught, which leads to noble deliverance and through which the suffering of one who practices it is completely exhausted, I see no ground at all on which anyone in the world, whether an ascetic, a brahmin, a god, Māra, Brahmā, or anyone else, could speak and challenge me in accordance with the Dharma. Because I see no such ground, I abide having attained security, I abide having attained freedom from anxiety, I abide having attained ease, I abide having attained fearlessness. Entering into the assembly, I rightly roar the lion's roar. This, Bhadrapāla, is the fourth of the Tathāgata's fearlessnesses of the tathāgatas, which a bodhisattva will attain when he enters this samādhi.

If one asks what the eighteen unshared qualities of a buddha belonging to the tathāgatas are, which a bodhisattva will attain when he enters this samādhi, Bhadrapāla, they are as follows. From the night on which the Tathāgata fully and perfectly awakens to unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening until the night on which he passes completely into nirvāṇa in the element of nirvāṇa without any remainder of the aggregates, throughout that time the Tathāgata has no error. He has no noisy clamor. He has no forgetfulness. He has no unsettled mind. He has no perception of diversity. He has no equanimity that has not discerned. He has no decline in aspiration. He has no decline in diligence. He has no decline in mindfulness. He has no decline in samādhi. He has no decline in wisdom. He has no decline in liberation. He has no decline in the knowledge and vision of liberation.

His seeing, through wisdom unattached and unobstructed, extends to past time. His seeing, through wisdom unattached and unobstructed, extends to future time. His seeing, through wisdom unattached and unobstructed, extends to present time. All the activities of the Tathāgata's body are preceded by wisdom and follow after wisdom. All the activities of the Tathāgata's speech are preceded by wisdom and follow after wisdom. All the activities of the Tathāgata's mind are preceded by wisdom and follow after wisdom. Bhadrapāla, these are the eighteen unshared qualities of a buddha, those of the tathāgatas, which a bodhisattva will attain if he abides in this samādhi.

Then the Blessed One (the Buddha) spoke these verses:

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva should rejoice in this samādhi with four kinds of rejoicing. What are the four? They are as follows: "Just as the tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly complete buddhas of the past, when formerly practicing the conduct of a bodhisattva, heard this samādhi, received its transmission, and rejoiced in order to perfect great learning for the sake of unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening, so too do I, having heard this samādhi, rejoice in order to perfect great learning for the sake of unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening." Bhadrapāla, this is the first rejoicing of a bodhisattva.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla: "Just as the tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly complete buddhas who have not yet come, when they come to practice the conduct of a bodhisattva, will hear this samādhi, receive its transmission, and rejoice in order to attain great learning and to perfect great learning for the sake of unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening, so too do I rejoice." Bhadrapāla, this is the second rejoicing of a bodhisattva.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva should say this: "Just as, in the present time, the tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly complete buddhas of the present, when formerly practicing the conduct of a bodhisattva, heard this samādhi, received its transmission, rejoiced, perfected great learning, and fully awakened to unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening, and just as the bodhisattvas of the present rejoice in this samādhi, so too do I, having heard this samādhi of the present, rejoice in order to perfect great learning for the sake of unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening." Bhadrapāla, this is the third rejoicing of a bodhisattva.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva should say this: "May all this merit of mine that comes with rejoicing, shared in common with all sentient beings, become unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening! May this samādhi also be attained! May great learning be perfected!" Bhadrapāla, this is the fourth merit of a bodhisattva that comes with rejoicing.

Bhadrapāla, bodhisattvas who obtain this merit that comes with rejoicing and this samādhi will perfect the factors conducive to awakening and swiftly awaken fully to unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening. Bhadrapāla, if a bodhisattva rejoices in this samādhi with those rejoicings and dedicates it, I shall set out just an example of how great a heap of merit he will hold.

"Bhadrapāla, consider this example. Suppose there were a certain person who could live for many hundreds of years, who lived for many hundreds of years, and who had a power of speed like the wind. Without stopping, he went eastward for a full hundred years. In the same way he went toward the south, the west, the north, the direction below, and the direction above. Bhadrapāla, what do you think? That person who could live a hundred years and lived a hundred years, whose power of speed was like the wind, went without stopping in each of the six directions for a hundred years apiece. Would anyone be able to count, measure, conceive or estimate how many leagues that person had traveled?"

The bodhisattva Bhadrapāla replied, "Venerable Blessed One, apart from the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha, the elder Śāriputra and the irreversible bodhisattvas, no one could do that."

The Blessed One said, "Bhadrapāla, suppose some woman or man were to fill with gold and silver the whole range traveled by that person who lived a hundred years and went as swiftly as the wind, and were to offer it to the tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly complete buddhas, but did not hear this samādhi. And suppose some bodhisattva, having heard this samādhi, were to rejoice with those four rejoicings, and having rejoiced, were also to dedicate it for the sake of unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening, out of a wish for great learning. Bhadrapāla, the former heap of merit would not come near even a hundredth part of this heap of merit. It would not come near a thousandth part, a hundred-thousandth part, a part in a hundred koṭis, or a part in a hundred thousand koṭis of niyutas. It would not come near it in number, in fraction, in counting, in comparison or in cause."

The seventh and final fascicle.

"Bhadrapāla, see how greatly beneficial for bodhisattvas is the heap of merit that comes with rejoicing in this samādhi. Therefore, Bhadrapāla, by this enumeration too it should be understood that the heap of merit that comes with rejoicing in this samādhi is of great benefit to bodhisattvas.

"Bhadrapāla, long ago, in times past, more incalculable even than incalculable eons, vast, immeasurable and inconceivable, at that time and on that occasion there appeared in the world the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha called Siṃhamati, endowed with knowledge and conduct, the Sugata, the Knower of the World, the charioteer who guides beings to be tamed, the unsurpassed, the teacher of gods and humans, the Buddha, the Blessed One.

"Bhadrapāla, at that time this Jambudvīpa was prosperous, flourishing, happy, blessed with good harvests, delightful, and filled with many people and with humankind. Bhadrapāla, at that time all the great cities in this Jambudvīpa were made of the seven precious substances. Each had ninety koṭis of households and measured twelve leagues, and there were sixty-eight thousand of them. Bhadrapāla, at that time this Jambudvīpa measured sixty-eight thousand leagues and was broad and vast.

"Bhadrapāla, at that time in this Jambudvīpa, in the place where that Tathāgata was born, there was a great city called Good Arising, and in that great city lived sixty koṭis of living beings.

"Bhadrapāla, at that time the first assembly of śrāvakas of that Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha Siṃhamati was a gathering of ninety koṭis of arhats. After seven days had passed, the second assembly of śrāvakas was a gathering of ninety koṭis of śrāvakas. After two weeks had passed, the third assembly of śrāvakas was a gathering of ninety koṭis. Ninety koṭis of pure bodhisattvas also gathered. After that, that Blessed One came to have śrāvakas beyond all counting.

"Bhadrapāla, at that time and on that occasion those people took up the ten virtuous courses of action, just as beings will come to possess the ten virtuous courses of action under the teaching of the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha Maitreya. Bhadrapāla, at that time the lifespan of beings was eighty-four thousand years.

"Bhadrapāla, at that time in that great city there was a wheel-turning king called Excellent Goer, endowed with the seven precious things and with a full thousand sons. He went into the presence of that Blessed One, the Tathāgata Siṃhamati, and served him with honor. Bhadrapāla, then that Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha Siṃhamati, knowing the intention of King Excellent Goer, taught this samādhi and explained it perfectly. Bhadrapāla, then King Excellent Goer, having heard this samādhi, rejoiced. Having rejoiced, he scattered a handful of jewels over that Blessed One. Then he died and passed on, and through that root of virtue, after that Tathāgata had passed into nirvāṇa, he was born here in Jambudvīpa into that very same royal household.

"Bhadrapāla, at that time, within that Tathāgata's teaching, there appeared a Dharma-preaching monk called Supreme Jewel, who taught this samādhi extensively to the fourfold assembly and proclaimed it perfectly. Bhadrapāla, at that time the one who had been King Excellent Goer became the prince called Brahmadatta, and he heard that monk explaining this samādhi. As soon as he heard it, he gained faith, and with a mind of faith he covered that monk with a hundred thousand pieces of gold and a pair of cotton cloths. Bhadrapāla, then Prince Brahmadatta, having heard this samādhi from that monk, made that covering because the monk had explained it so well, and he generated the mind set on unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening.

Having generated the aspiration for unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, he wished for this samādhi. Longing for the Dharma, he went forth with faith from home into homelessness under that monk, the Dharma preacher, together with a full thousand beings. They shaved off their hair and beards and put on saffron robes. After going forth, for eight thousand years he served and followed that monk together with his own retinue of those thousand beings. Yet apart from merely getting to listen to it, he did not obtain this samādhi from that monk. Even so, having heard it only once, not twice, he rejoiced in it with those four kinds of rejoicing. Through that root of virtue, he and that full thousand beings together pleased sixty-eight thousand buddhas. In every birth he also expounded this samādhi and proclaimed it widely to others, and so he gradually pleased another sixty-eight thousand buddhas as well. Through that very root of virtue joined with rejoicing, and through obtaining this samādhi, he completed the factors of awakening. He fully awakened into unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening and became the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha called Steadfast Hero, and he established countless hundreds of thousands of koṭis of niyutas of beings in unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening. Those thousand beings too completed the factors of awakening through this very root of virtue that had been matured by the samādhi. They fully awakened into unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, and all of them likewise became tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly complete buddhas called Steadfast Hero, and they established countless hundreds of thousands of koṭis of niyutas of beings in unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening.

Bhadrapāla, see how greatly meaningful is the heap of merit joined with rejoicing in this samādhi! If even uttering words of rejoicing brings about unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening, Bhadrapāla, what need is there to speak of a bodhisattva who, having heard this samādhi, takes it up, masters it, retains it, reads it, proclaims it widely to others, and remains diligent in the practice of meditation? Bhadrapāla, who is there who, having heard this samādhi, would not rejoice in it, would not receive its transmission, would not master it, would not retain it, would not proclaim it widely to others, and would not remain diligent in the practice of meditation? And why is that? Bhadrapāla, it is because by hearing this samādhi all the factors of awakening of bodhisattvas become complete. Therefore, Bhadrapāla, you should have faith in it. You should understand it.

If bodhisattvas endowed with excellent superior intention, who seek awakening, hear that this samādhi is to be found about a yojana away, then, Bhadrapāla, that bodhisattva endowed with excellent superior intention, who seeks awakening, should travel that distance of about a yojana in order to hear this samādhi. Having heard it, he should take it up, master it, retain it, read it, teach it widely to others, explain it and proclaim it. He should also delight in meditating on it.

Bhadrapāla, never mind about a yojana. Whether it is about two yojanas, or about three yojanas, or about four yojanas, or from about five yojanas up to about ten yojanas; Bhadrapāla, never mind about ten yojanas either. Suppose a bodhisattva hears, "A hundred yojanas from here, this samādhi of the bodhisattvas, the direct presence of the buddhas of the present, is being practiced in a certain region of the land," hearing that this samādhi is to be found about a hundred yojanas away. Even then, Bhadrapāla, that bodhisattva endowed with excellent superior intention, who seeks awakening, should go those hundred yojanas, with a mind free of discouragement, free of laziness, free of fear, free of weariness and free of distress, and with unslackening diligence, even just to hear a little of this samādhi. What need is there to speak of going in order to receive its transmission, to master it, to proclaim it and to meditate on it? And why is that? Bhadrapāla, it is because the factors of awakening of bodhisattvas depend on this samādhi.

Bhadrapāla, when those bodhisattvas endowed with excellent superior intention, who seek awakening, have gone those hundred yojanas, they should generate toward the master from whom they heard this samādhi the perception that he is the Teacher. They should serve that son of good family, the Dharma preacher, with every kind of service, and follow after him. Even just to hear this samādhi they should follow after him for one year, or two, or three, or four, or five, or ten years, or a hundred, or for as long as they live. What need is there to speak of doing so in order to receive its transmission, to master it, to write it out, to retain it, to proclaim it, or to meditate on it?

Bhadrapāla, that son or daughter of good family should give up their own inclinations, and having given them up, should act in accordance with that master's mind. They should follow the master with respect and with reverence. They should follow after him by doing as he instructs. They should follow after him without disregarding his instructions, with steady affection, having abandoned fickleness, and without looking for faults. They should also generate the perception that he is a spiritual friend. They should also firmly establish toward him the perception that he is the Teacher.

Bhadrapāla, if that bodhisattva is so earnest toward that master, then, unless he has previously done and accumulated karma that leads to being deprived of the Dharma, it is impossible and there is no chance that he would fail even just to hear this samādhi; that cannot happen. That bodhisattva should have a sense of conscience toward that master, and should be grateful and acknowledge what has been done for him.

If, in this way, the words and letters of that samādhi are to endure for a long time and remain free of confusion, then, Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva who hears that this samādhi is to be found in another village, or hears that it is to be found in the fields of another village, should go there once he has heard of it, even if only to listen to it. What need is there to speak of going in order to receive its transmission, to master it, to retain it, to read it, to write it out, to cultivate it in meditation, and to proclaim it properly to others?

Bhadrapāla, you should have faith in this and understand it: a bodhisattva who, by seeking this samādhi, gains even just the hearing of it is to be known as irreversible from unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. He is so through the root of virtue of seeking this very samādhi, the root of virtue of pursuing the Dharma, the root of virtue of longing for the Dharma, and the root of virtue of undertaking diligence. If that is so, Bhadrapāla, what need is there to speak of a bodhisattva who receives the transmission of this samādhi, masters it, retains it and reads it, and who, having received, mastered, retained and read it, teaches it to others, proclaims it, proclaims it correctly, and abides diligently in the practice of meditation?

Then, at that time, the Blessed One spoke these verses:

I recall a tathāgata of the past, supreme among humans,
Who was called Siṃhamati.
There a lord of men, a king,
Heard this samādhi at that time.
When the king had heard this samādhi,
He was seized by delight and joy beyond compare,
And over Siṃhamati, the Buddha supreme among humans,
He scattered a handful of jewels.

"May this samādhi, well spoken by the Blessed One,
Bring benefit to all sentient beings!
Today I go to you for refuge."
So he thought, and so he also spoke aloud.
Through that karma, the king,
After he died, was born again in that same place,
And he met a monk called Ratna,
Renowned in every direction and of great power.
From him, too, he heard that very samādhi,
And hearing it, he felt a joy most vast.
Feeling it, he set out for awakening with joy,
Covered the monk with gold and cloth,
And, longing for this samādhi,
Went forth together with a thousand living beings.
For a full hundred thousand years
All of them followed that monk.
This samādhi, like an ocean of learning,
They heard but once, not twice.
Their mindfulness never grew weary,
And they sought this peaceful samādhi everywhere.
Because all of them performed this deed,
They pleased fully sixty-eight thousand
Buddhas of great power,
And from them they heard this samādhi.
Therefore they honored other sugatas too,
Fully sixty-eight thousand of them.
To the Sugata Siṃhamati
He declared, "I rejoice."
Through the merit he had made, that king
Became the buddha called Dṛḍhavīrya.
Inconceivable myriads of koṭis of living beings,
All of them, he freed from birth and death.
A full thousand, too, trained after him,
Became buddhas, and were renowned
In the world with its gods as Steadfast Hero.

If even by the mere sound of the samādhi one reaches awakening,
Relying on no existence whatever,
Then that those who teach this samādhi extensively
Will attain awakening,
What need is there to say? There is never any doubt.
This peaceful samādhi spoken by the Buddha,
Even if it should be a hundred leagues away,
One who seeks awakening, by day and by night,
Should never stay put, but go and listen to it.
If one goes and does not find it,
Even so one's merit is immeasurable;
If that itself gives rise to one's awakening,
No need to speak of one who goes and hears it.
Therefore, whoever longs for this samādhi
Should first call to mind Brahmadatta.
A monk who possesses this samādhi
Should be attended without a weary mind.

I recall that in a former birth,
For a full thirty-six thousand years,
I constantly attended a Dharma preacher,
Yet never once heard this samādhi.
In the past there appeared a buddha called Satya.
At that time there was a young monk of great learning,
And after the Sugata's teaching had declined,
He then upheld this very samādhi.
I was then a king of the kṣatriya caste.
"A certain monk upholds this samādhi;
Go to him and listen well!"
Hearing of this samādhi thus in a dream,
I quickly rose and, in great haste,
Searched for the monk who upheld it.
Having found him, thinking, "Might I perhaps
Hear it from this renunciant even once?"
For a full sixty-six thousand years
I honored that monk with joy.
Yet because Māra had laid his influence upon it,
I did not hear it even once.
Therefore, to monks and nuns,
To laymen and to laywomen,
I say, "When you hear of the samādhi, take it up at once!"
I urge you to have faith and to understand.

There, after remaining a full eon,
I attended and relied on the holder of the samādhi;
For a thousand eons I served him well,
And then he explained this samādhi.
To that very monk begging alms in the village,
Who thoroughly practices this stainless samādhi,
Food and drink and bedding,
And likewise clothing, were given by the koṭi.
Drink and food, bedding and clothing,
Many koṭis of them, leave all these aside:
If one gets to give one's own flesh,
And that is good conduct, what need to speak of food and drink?
Though one always serves as a servant and attends him,
And seeks this peaceful samādhi,
And though the wise, having found it, act on it swiftly,
Even so one has not repaid his kindness.

Even if sought for many ten millions of eons,
This samādhi is extremely hard to find.
If even its sound is heard only rarely,
What need to speak of reciting and teaching it?

One may fill world systems as many as the Ganges' sands
With jewels and give them all away,
But whoever receives the transmission of a few verses of this,
The measure of that one's merit is not easy to grasp.

One may for ten millions of eons as many as the Ganges' sands
Constantly abide in diligence, having taken it up,
But whoever receives the transmission of a few verses of this,
The measure of that one's merit is not easy to grasp.

One may for ten millions of eons as many as the Ganges' sands
Constantly cultivate meditative concentration without reliance,
But whoever receives the transmission of a few verses of this,
The measure of that one's merit is not easy to grasp.

One may for ten millions of eons as many as the Ganges' sands
Constantly abide in wisdom,
But whoever receives the transmission of a few verses of this,
The measure of that one's merit is not easy to grasp.

This is the twenty-third chapter of The Samādhi Requested by Bhadrapāla.

Therefore, Bhadrapāla, a faithful son or daughter of good family should listen to this samādhi with respect. Having heard it, they should take it up, master it, write it down, keep it, and read it. Having taken it up and mastered it, they should also proclaim it widely to others. They should write it out well and preserve it. Why is that? Bhadrapāla, after I have passed away, in a future time, many bodhisattvas will appear who are faithful, clear-minded, and given to much reflection. For the benefit and happiness of sentient beings, they will seek great learning. Seeking great learning, they will travel through the directions and intermediate directions in search of the true Dharma. Bhadrapāla, for the sake and benefit of such beings, who strive for the Dharma, who are devoted to the Dharma, who long for the Dharma, who uphold the Dharma, and who hold to the most extensive teachings, this samādhi should be received and taught. Blessed by the blessing of the tathāgatas, it should be written out well in great volumes, sealed with the seal of the tathāgatas, and preserved.

And what, Bhadrapāla, is the seal of the tathāgatas? It is this: all phenomena are without agent, without action, uncompounded, without attachment, beyond apprehension, emptiness, signless, wishless, without characteristics, nondual, without entry, without support, beyond counting, beyond designation, without taking up, without casting away, without abiding. All phenomena are without permanence and without annihilation; they are the exhaustion of causes, the exhaustion of suffering, the exhaustion of things; unborn, unceasing, unabiding, indestructible; without path and without fruit of the path; undisturbed by all the noble ones, abandoned by all the childish, cast aside by the ignorant, and embraced by the wise. Bhadrapāla, this is the seal of all the tathāgatas, the seal that the tathāgatas have set upon whatever the Tathāgata has spoken.

Therefore, Bhadrapāla, a son or daughter of good family who strives for awakening and who wishes to receive the transmission of this samādhi in that later time, that final age, should receive its transmission, recite it, and proclaim it widely to others.

Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva endowed with four qualities will strive even just to hear this samādhi, so what need is there to speak of receiving its transmission and reciting it? What are the four? Having previously purified the superior intention; having previously made aspirations; being devoted to the wisdom of omniscience; and taking up every approach of the Dharma with the thought, "How might I hold and teach this treasury of the precious Dharma, which the tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly awakened buddhas accomplished over many hundreds of thousands of millions of billions of eons?" Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva endowed with these four qualities will strive to hear, take up, and keep this samādhi.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva endowed with four qualities will strive, in seeking this samādhi, even merely to write it down. What are the four? They are these: bringing the Buddha to mind; being skilled in recollecting the Dharma again and again; bringing to mind the cutting off of all apprehension of phenomena; and being skilled in dismantling all signs. Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva endowed with these four qualities will strive, in seeking this samādhi, even merely to write it down.

Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva endowed with four qualities should be known as diligent in seeking this samādhi. What are the four? They are these: through the knowledge that determines all phenomena to be things that cannot be grasped, a bodhisattva should be known as diligent in this samādhi. Through being skilled in cultivating the Buddha's body as the object of meditation, a bodhisattva should be known as diligent in this samādhi. Through bringing to mind the Buddha as the object within every object, a bodhisattva should be known as diligent in this samādhi. Through being skilled in the conviction that all phenomena are devoid of entities, a bodhisattva should be known as diligent in this samādhi. Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva endowed with these four qualities should be known as earnestly diligent in seeking this samādhi.

Bhadrapāla, in the later time, in the final five hundred years, when the true Dharma is in turmoil, suppose someone strives to seek this samādhi in order to benefit all sentient beings and out of compassion for all sentient beings, and gives rise to the thought, "How can this samādhi become widespread, vast, and long-enduring?" That son of good family generates great merit. Apart from knowing that this heap of merit inclines toward omniscience, no one can reach the end of that heap of merit.

Through the power of their aspirations, their heap of merit will swiftly be directed toward omniscience. They will be endowed with a great accumulation of roots of virtue and will emerge from abiding in selflessness. Leaving aside those who have donned the great armor for immeasurable, countless, measureless, inconceivable eons, or for even longer, all of them will have everything fulfilled just as they intended. All of them will go forth to deliverance in accordance with the particular character of their conduct and their accumulations.

Bhadrapāla, a son or daughter of good family who has entered the bodhisattva vehicle, who wishes to attain the perfection of all dharmas and the perfection of the wisdom of omniscience, and who wishes to attain pure roots of virtue, should apply themselves in the later time, in the final period when great fear arises, at the time when the true Dharma is being destroyed, to writing down this samādhi, receiving its transmission, reciting it, and proclaiming it widely to others as well.

Why is that? Bhadrapāla, the wisdom of omniscience, knowing all the aspects of all dharmas, is contained within this samādhi. By accomplishing skill in the recollection of the Buddha, one realizes all dharmas as having the nature of non-entity, and this is the recollection of the Buddha. Bodhisattvas who abide in it are devoted to every object of focus as a focus on the Buddha. They make every object of focus culminate in the Tathāgata. They know that every object of focus, every entity, is by nature hollow. However they focus on the tathāgatas and bring them to mind, in just that way their seeing of the tathāgatas becomes pure. For those who abide like this, there is no direction in which the Tathāgata does not become directly present; in every direction they purify their seeing of the Tathāgata. Once their seeing of the Tathāgata has been purified, if with little effort they take this very world system as their object of focus, they will see those tathāgatas directly, right here. Their direct seeing of the tathāgatas becomes immeasurably pure, and immeasurable tathāgatas appear within their field of vision.

Because the nature of all objects of focus is ungraspable, one object of focus does not grasp all objects of focus, nor do all objects of focus grasp one object of focus. When the nature of all dharmas is closely examined, it is established as being without entity, because it is utterly at peace.

Bhadrapāla, the seeing of all tathāgatas of those who abide like this will become pure, and once it has become pure they do not become conceited and do not elaborate. They will bring to completion the cultivation of this samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present. They will also abide in the complete fulfillment of all the factors conducive to awakening.

Then, at that time, the Blessed One spoke these verses:

Is the entry into wisdom just as it is.
That son of the Teacher, chief of the world,
Strives with his mind toward this samādhi.

Whoever recollects the Buddha as an object of focus
Will come to see the many meanings of that object.
For them this peaceful samādhi, hard to see,
Will become directly present in the later time.

The wise who do not rely on any dharma
And strive in mind for the benefit of beings,
In the later time, the time of great fear,
Will apply themselves to this samādhi.

Constantly sets mindfulness firmly
On this Dharma hard to see, and strives,
Into their hands this samādhi will come.

Whoever, with mindfulness and realized understanding,
Always places the mind on the Buddha as object,
Diligent in yoga, not dependent on others,
Will abide in this great samādhi.

Abiding in words hard to see in order to care for others,
Weary in mind of all the world,
The wise who desire no existence whatsoever

Whoever longs for awakening, compassionate in order to help,
Contemplates the bodies of the tathāgatas,
And makes aspirations toward the peaceful Dharma,
They strive to rely on this samādhi.

The Sugata's son who has drawn out the thorn,

To them this supreme meaning is explained.

Whoever is by nature free of ignorance,
Who dispels the abode of ignorance through knowledge,
And applies themselves diligently to this supreme meaning,
To them this peaceful samādhi is explained.

Abiding in purity, without any affliction at all,
Longing for boundless wisdom,
Having found supreme joy in this definitive meaning,
To them this peaceful liberation is explained.

Whoever is free from every fetter,
Steadfast and compassionate toward all that lives,
Without malice, relying with delight on the path of peace,
To them this samādhi is rightly proclaimed.

This is the twenty-fourth chapter of The Samādhi Requested by Bhadrapāla.

Bhadrapāla, these four qualities are what lead bodhisattvas to attain this samādhi. What are the four? They are reverence for the Buddha; keeping unshakable faith in mind; reverence for the Dharma and the wish to hear it; and speaking in accord with the Dharma. Bhadrapāla, these four qualities lead bodhisattvas to attain this samādhi.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, there are four qualities that lead bodhisattvas to attain this samādhi. What are the four? They are the intention focused on the Buddha, with a mind free of distortion; examining the essential nature of the object as virtuous by its intrinsic nature, being free of doubt about the Dharma; being rightly intent on all phenomena as empty of essential nature, empty of intrinsic nature, and utterly empty, through examining skill with regard to things; and being skilled in dismantling perceptions, so that, through examining the knowledge that cuts through completely, one dismantles things as hollow. Bhadrapāla, these four qualities lead bodhisattvas to attain this samādhi.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, there are four qualities that lead bodhisattvas to attain this samādhi. What are the four? They are being embraced by all the buddhas; being skilled in upholding the sacred Dharma, and protecting and safeguarding the sacred Dharma; establishing all sentient beings in happiness, with a mind of compassion for all sentient beings; and being skilled in dismantling the perception of sentient beings. Bhadrapāla, these four qualities lead bodhisattvas to attain this samādhi.

Therefore, Bhadrapāla, in the later time, in the final age, in the last five hundred years, when the sacred Dharma is in great turmoil, bodhisattvas should be ready to give up even body and life, and should take up this samādhi, master it thoroughly, and keep it.

Bhadrapāla, bodhisattvas who strive to become skilled in upholding all teachings will preserve this treasury of the precious Dharma, which the tathāgatas accomplished over endless tens of millions of eons, so that it may remain for a long time. They will also be able to teach, analyze, and proclaim a Dharma such as this.

Bhadrapāla, bodhisattvas have these grounds for diligence: through the sustaining power of diligence, bodhisattvas will engage in aspiration in order to fully ripen the roots of virtue of sentient beings.

Bhadrapāla, those sons or daughters of good family who in that later time, in the final age, preserve these teachings and rightly uphold them have set out to benefit all sentient beings, to take care of all sentient beings, and to rightly embrace all sentient beings. They will be embraced by the tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly complete buddhas. Even though such a bodhisattva dwells in another world system, the buddhas, the blessed ones residing in other world systems, will keep him in mind. Out of longing for the Dharma, seeking the Dharma, and in order to accomplish the Dharma, he will strive for this samādhi in the later time, in the final age.

Those who uphold such a discourse on the Dharma as this in the later time, in the final age, will be fully embraced by the qualities of bodhisattvas and the riches of bodhisattvas. Those who remain in order to uphold such teachings as these in the later time, in the final age, will swiftly take hold of the ten powers of a tathāgata, the four fearlessnesses, the eighteen unshared qualities of a buddha, and indeed all the qualities of a buddha.

Then the Blessed One spoke these verses:

It is for them that this dhāraṇī is taught.

Those who, for the sake of the precious sublime Dharma,
Strive without fear,
Whose hearts are loving and who are compassionate,

Praised by the buddhas has been taught.

Those who have been blessed

Those who abide in the knowledge of exhaustion,

Suchness and the meaning of nonconceptuality,
And in whom there is no elaboration at all,
Will also uphold the Dharma.

The gentle one who knows
That all phenomena are without an agent
Will proclaim that nature of reality
In which there is nothing, and nothing to be taken up.

Those who know that all phenomena,
Without grasping and free of anything grasped,
Are completely pure from the very beginning,
Abide in the domain of the Dharma.

Those who thus know, with regard to phenomena,
That all phenomena are without essence,
Will teach this very Dharma;
That is said to be the entry.

Those who fully know the nature of reality
As nameless and free of names,
Without elaboration and unarisen,
Will proclaim emptiness as well.

Whoever has come to know this Dharma
As without abiding and without going,
Pure like the expanse of space:
In them the samādhi will arise.

Those who are free of perception and of conceiving objects,

That seal is no seal at all;

This was the twenty-fifth chapter of The Samādhi Requested by Bhadrapāla.

Therefore, Bhadrapāla, sons or daughters of noble family should make conscientiousness their foremost concern and should properly gather these teachings. They should also apply themselves to its benefits. They should also be endowed with appropriate attention, and should completely abandon inappropriate attention. In that way, this profound Dharma, which is hard to see and hard to realize, which gives rise to all the qualities of the buddhas, and which brings about the attainment of the wisdom of the self-arisen ones and of unobstructed wisdom, will not be difficult to find.
