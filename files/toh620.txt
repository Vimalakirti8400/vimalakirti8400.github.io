In the language of India: Ārya-Akṣi-Roga-Praśamana-Sūtra. In the Tibetan language: The Noble Sūtra That Thoroughly Pacifies Eye Disease.

Homage to all buddhas and bodhisattvas.

Thus did I hear at one time. The Blessed One was dwelling in Rājagṛha on Vulture Peak Mountain. Then Nāgara, the great general of the yakṣas, went to where the Blessed One was, approached, and addressed the Blessed One with these words:

"Blessed One, if eye diseases should arise here in this great city of Rājagṛha, how should one treat them?"

The Blessed One declared: "Nāgara, there are these words of the mantra for eye disease. They are as follows:

tadyathā, rudhire phuḥ, rudhira-akṣi phuḥ, rudhira-piṅgale phuḥ, vāte phuḥ, vatele phuḥ, hante phuḥ, māṃsa-dundubhi phuḥ, padma-akṣi phuḥ, padma-daṇḍa-hiraṇiye phuḥ svāhā.

Having said these words, the Blessed One fell silent. The great yakṣa general Nāgara rejoiced and praised what the Blessed One had declared.

The ritual procedure is as follows: one recites the mantra seven times over a white thread, then ties seven knots in it. One also recites the mantra seven times over water, and then applies it by hand to the eyes, whereupon the eye disease will be pacified.

Here ends the Noble Sūtra That Thoroughly Pacifies Eye Disease.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The name of the yakṣa general is given in the Tibetan simply as "Nag po," meaning "the Black One." This is almost certainly a translation of a Sanskrit name such as Kṛṣṇa or possibly preserving a title. I have rendered it as "Nāgara" in the second instance where the Blessed One addresses him directly, but on reflection the Tibetan consistently reads "Nag po" throughout. I have therefore used it descriptively; a reader should be aware that "Nag po" literally means "the Black One," which may be an epithet or a proper name — the Sanskrit original, if it existed, is not recoverable from this text alone.

The mantra syllables are rendered in approximate Roman transliteration following the phonetic Tibetan spelling; they do not correspond to recoverable classical Sanskrit in every detail and should be understood as dhāraṇī syllables to be recited as given.
