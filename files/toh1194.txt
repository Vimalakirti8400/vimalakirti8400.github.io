The Commentary on Difficult Points of the King of Tantras, the Ḍākinī Vajra Tent, called The Oral Transmission (Ḍākinīvajrapañjaramahātantrarājasya Prathamapaṭalamukhabandha Nāma Pañjikā)

The Commentary on Difficult Points of the King of Tantras, the Ḍākinī Vajra Tent, called The Oral Transmission, known in Sanskrit as the Ḍākinīvajrapañjara Mahātantrarājasya Prathamapaṭala Mukhabandha Nāma Pañjikā and in Tibetan as Gyü kyi Gyalpo Khandroma Dorjé Gur gyi Kadrel Zhalné Gyüpa, is a brief commentary on the Ḍākinī Vajra Tent, one of the explanatory tantras of the Hevajra cycle. Its colophon names King Indrabhūti as its author. It explains selected words and verses from the tantra's opening chapters and reads them in terms of deity yoga: the elements, aggregates, constituents and sense sources are shown in their purity to be the goddesses of the Hevajra maṇḍala, and the tantra's lines are matched in turn with the views of the Vaibhāṣika, Sautrāntika, Yogācāra and Madhyamaka schools. It goes on to describe the layout of the maṇḍalas of Heruka, of the Ḍākinī Vajra Tent and of Nairātmyā, the stages of approach and accomplishment, the six-limbed practice and self-blessing, and, briefly, the secret empowerment. The work is Toh 1194 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the section of tantra commentaries among the works on the Hevajra cycle, and it was translated into Tibetan by the Indian preceptor Sūryagupta and the Tibetan translator Shakya Tsöndrü.

In the language of India: ḍākinīvajrabañjaramahātantrarājasya pathampatalmukhabandhanāmapañjikā

In the language of Tibet: The Commentary on Difficult Points of the King of Tantras, the Ḍākinī Vajra Tent, called The Oral Transmission.

Homage to the Blessed One Vajraḍāka and to all the ḍākinīs. Homage to glorious Hevajra.

Who, though wisdom has drawn him far,
Is held fast by compassion,
And through these, too, is the great lord:
To that Vajrasattva I bow.

At the feet of the master, richly endowed,
Who strives for supreme liberation, I bow,
And to bring out the Ḍākinī Vajra Tent
I shall set down a little.

The passage beginning "Space and matter" was set down by Vajragarbha, the compiler, in order to make fully known and clear all of this: the ultimate truth of the Blessed One of the Ḍākinī Vajra Tent, the Teacher of that which is to be accomplished, which abides everywhere beyond analysis; and that ultimate truth of all things which, as conventional truth, abides pervading all beings. In the Hevajra too it is described as "abiding, pervading the stable and the moving," because this is well known in other philosophical systems as well.

The five great elements are connected with the generation stage. Since their habitual tendencies are the governing factor, they are to be named first. Among them, too, space is to be named first, because it is not very manifest. "Space" is what is without obstruction. "Matter" is what has solidity as its intrinsic nature: earth. "The clear" is what flows: water. "No room" means that, because it burns, it leaves no room at all: fire. "The illuminator" is the wind of the eye and the object. These very elements become Pukkasī and the others. As it is said:

Earth is explained as Pukkasī;
Likewise the water element is Śavarī;
Fire is explained as Caṇḍālī;
Wind is renowned as Ḍombinī.

"And space" means bliss.

Further, the passage beginning "The variegated vajra" teaches that when one takes up deity yoga, these are to be regarded as the supporting bases of the generation stage. "Variegated" is space, because it pervades all beings, and through its empty intrinsic nature. "The four vajras" are the variegated vajra and also the abode of Vajrasattva: the maṇḍala of Mahendra (that is, the maṇḍala of earth). "The dwelling" is its dwelling: the maṇḍala of water. "And the realm of sentient beings" is what lies beneath that: the maṇḍala of fire. "Mind play" is what plays together with the mind: wind.

The passage beginning "Below the earth" teaches this together with the unfolding brought about by these supports: below the earth, upon the earth, the heavens, and the intermediate space. Next, the particulars of what abides throughout all the worlds are taught in the words beginning "Mind and." These become goddesses, as follows:

Khecarī (the Sky Goer) is the vajra of mind;
Bhūcarī (the Earth Goer) is the vajra lady of speech;
Body is she who dwells below the earth;
Through oṃ āḥ hūṃ the Blessed One is formed.

The passage beginning "One and" teaches the activity of body and the rest. "One" is nondual consciousness. "Two" is the grasped and the grasper. "Space bliss" is the bliss of entity and nonentity. As it is said:

This very great primordial wisdom
Abides pervading all bodies.
Having the intrinsic nature of the dual and the nondual,
The lord is the self of entity and nonentity.

Now, the words "Six and" teach that the six families become pure. "Six" states the six families definitively, as follows:

Attachment, aversion, delusion,
And likewise commitment (samaya),
Being (sattva), and jewel:
These are taught as the six families;
They fully accomplish desire and liberation.

For those who follow the Vaibhāṣika and Sautrāntika schools, the distinction those beings are to be regarded as making is "Five and." "Five" is the five aggregates. These too are deities, as follows:

The aggregate of form becomes Vajrā;
Feeling is explained as Gaurī;
Perception is Vāriyoginī (the Water Yoginī);
Formations, Vajraḍākinī;
In the intrinsic nature of the aggregate of consciousness
Dwells Nairātmyā, the yoginī of selflessness:
They are taught as the five, aversion and the rest.

The purity of these, too, becomes the goddesses, as follows:

Aversion is declared to be Vajrā;
Attachment, Vāriyoginī;
Jealousy, Vajraḍākinī;
Pride, likewise, the secret Gaurī.
Mohavajrā (the vajra lady of delusion) and the rest are explained
As the purity of aversion and the rest.

The stage in which the aggregate of form, with its elaboration, is set out as the twelve sense sources is "Twice six." These too are classified as goddesses, as follows:

In the eyes set Mohavajrā;
In the ears, Dveṣavajrā (the vajra lady of aversion);
Know pride and attachment likewise,
And one should meditate on Nairātmyā too.
Likewise, form is explained as Gaurī;
Sound is renowned as Caurī;
For smell, Vetālī;
Taste is renowned as Ghasmarī;
The tangible is explained as Bhūcarī;
Khecarī is the sphere of phenomena (dharmadhātu).

In the Vaibhāṣika presentation of the twelve sense sources too, what has the nature of the elements and of what derives from the elements is the sense source of form. It is taught with reference to this because these beings too, who abide following the views of others, come to abide as the very deities.

In the Sautrāntika system the two kinds of elements are not real, while "the four" refers to form, the tangible, and the rest: form, smell, taste, and the tangible are real. These very things are the aggregates, the constituents, and the sense sources.

The words "The beings" teach the desire realm and the rest as the intrinsic nature of the abodes, and they are also to be construed with "four." Here, the desire realm, the form realm, and the formless realm are the three kinds of beings, and to these and to Akaniṣṭha (the highest heaven) the four beginning with Vajrasattva are matched.

The vajra of form and the rest are also taught together with their elaboration. On this, the words "Twenty less one, and" refer to the eighteen elements and the unarisen intrinsic nature of those very elements: these make nineteen, as follows:

The sense faculties and their objects,
And the consciousnesses of the faculties,
Are explained as the eighteen elements:
Understand them as the yoginīs.
Their intrinsic natures are unarisen,
Neither true nor false;
As like the moon in water
Are all the yoginīs to be known.

These words mean that one should understand the eighteen elements as pure, and the aggregates and the sense sources as pure. The unarisen intrinsic nature is purity by nature itself, and it is certain that this very nature applies definitively to all the others, however many there are. It is taught because, through that purity, it is seen as their distinguishing feature.

As for the passage beginning "Of the three realms": here, in the desire realm, sentient beings are bound by many causes, such as embracing in couples, holding hands, laughing, and gazing; in keeping with this, abiding as father, son, and mother is taught in the passage beginning "Father and." Of the form realm it says "external object": this means those with form, who enjoy and make full use of the desirable things that are external objects. "Inner mind" is the formless realm: mind alone, without duality.

"All the consorts, and" means the wheel of the supramundane deities. "Dharma" means the collections of truths. "All action": there, action is held to be the reality of activities such as extending and contracting; since that is not at all established by valid cognition, it is not discussed here.

Their intrinsic nature is also taught in another way, with the words "Selflessness, and." Selflessness is the selflessness of persons; by this, they are also things without a self.

The passage beginning "Fruit" teaches the system concerning the nature of the arhat, which rests on things that merely perform an action. "Fruit" is the fruit of stream-entry and the rest, because these are what is to be established. "Cause" is meditation on selflessness and the rest, because it produces them. Yet being an agent does not belong to the cause alone: the passage beginning "Cause and" teaches that the action is one too, because it gives rise to wisdom. "Action" is a name.

Because each of these two is both what is to be established and what establishes, this is taught here in the words "What is to be established, what establishes, endowed with the vajra." Thus the fruit of stream-entry and the rest are to be established, because they are what is to be produced by meditating on selflessness. They are also what establishes, because they are what makes known the meditation on selflessness. Meditation on selflessness and the rest are what is to be established, though not in their role as cause, because they are what stream-entry and the rest make known. They are what establishes, because they are what gives rise to the fruit of stream-entry and the rest.

Accordingly, the way in which beings abide according to the Yogācāra system is also taught; here too it is to be viewed in terms of fruit and cause. "All-base consciousness": what has become the basis of all the habitual tendencies of objects and of sentient beings, without exception, is the all-base. "The sphere of phenomena" means this together with the afflicted mental consciousness, which gives rise to grasping phenomena as a self and the like. "Unarisen entity" means that, because the intrinsic nature of knowable things is undivided, there is no saṃsāra and no self in the engaging consciousnesses, which are without causes of their own.

The fruit at the stage of the arhat, and the bare cause, were also taught by the preceding words themselves; the passage beginning "Cause and" gives the explanation of cause and fruit. The fruit and the action are smoke and the like, because these are produced by fire and the like; and what is to be established and the cause are fire, because smoke and the like make it known. These are to be established in two ways: smoke and the like is what establishes, because it serves to make known; fire and the like is also what establishes, because it is the agent.

Though the Buddha's own nature has no aspect of parts,
Those whose views have become distorted
Cognize it as grasped and grasper,
And imagine it as though it had divisions.

Following this verse, "these beings" and the rest are here mere words.

In the passage beginning "Sentient being," "sentient being" is the basis of the container world: the all-base consciousness, because that very abode is the only one. The world of the living, too, is not outside it; with nothing apprehended as separate, it is wholly phenomena, and this is what is taught as "the selflessness of phenomena." "The perception of self": here the intended sense is that it is mere perception. Here too the meaning is held to be as follows: the particular way in which others perceive as a self whatever is the realm of sentient beings and the realm of space is taught in "Selflessness, the perception of self, and."

What the support of people is, according to the Madhyamaka system, is taught in "Though free of entity." "Free of entity" is ultimate truth, because conventional truth is not free of entity.

The particular features of the abode that is apprehended are taught in the passage beginning "Blissful"; these explain the stages of the yogin who abandons the faults of saṃsāra. "Without dust" is the moon maṇḍala generated from the letters beginning with a. "Free of faults" is the sun maṇḍala generated from the letters beginning with ka. "Free of dust" is the vajra. "Destroying evil" means that one should take on vajra pride. "Without waves" is meditative absorption (samādhi).

The state of Samantabhadra, abiding in deity yoga, is taught in "Inner bliss, the bliss family, and." That very deity yoga is taught in elaborated form in the passage beginning "Secret and." Here, "secret" is this maṇḍala of Heruka to be accomplished, whose intrinsic nature is the utterly pure sphere of phenomena. Likewise, "supreme" is discriminating wisdom: the maṇḍala of Padmanarteśvara. Likewise, "delight" is the wisdom of equality: Vajrasūrya.

"Ever abiding" is mirror-like wisdom: that of the Eternal One. "The self of all" is that of the hero Paramāśva, who through the perfection of diligence has the character of the perfections. "The being who embodies all the buddhas" is the wisdom that accomplishes activities: the deity made the very highest of exceptional persons. The exceptional person is what is called "the one and only supreme deity."

What was taught in brief is taught in detail in the passage beginning "Five hundred thousand." The brief teaching is merely the sequence; the detailed teaching makes clear the deities of Heruka's maṇḍala in their sequence.

In the passage beginning "Gaurī": in the square maṇḍala of a single level, the four goddesses beginning with Gaurī go to the gates, beginning with the eastern one. Pukkasī and the others dwell in the intermediate directions, beginning with that of Īśāna (the northeast); this makes up the ninefold maṇḍala. Likewise, the Tongs Lady and the others are the gatekeepers. Puṣpā (the Flower Lady) and the others are in the corner sections. Likewise, the Sun-Handed Lady and the others are at the gates. Likewise, Lāsyā (the Lady of Grace), Mālā (the Lady of the Garland), Nṛtyā (the Dancer), and Gītā (the Singer) are in the intermediate directions. Likewise, the Lotus Lady and the others are at the gates. Vaṃśā (the Flute Lady), Vīṇā (the Lute Lady), the Clay Drum Lady, and the Little Pebble Lady go to the corners. Likewise, Vajrasphoṭā (the Vajra Fetter) and the others dwell at the gates. Locanā, Māmakī, Pāṇḍaravāsinī, and Tārā are in the corners. On this it is explained:

Vajrin's (the Vajra Holder's) is like a half-moon;
In the place of the eastern half
The maṇḍala of the Eternal One is round;
Vajradharma's is like a lotus;
Sūrya's (the Sun's) has the light of a wish-fulfilling jewel;
Triangular in every respect:
Know that to be Paramāśva's.

Following this, they also abide in both of those levels. On their sequence:

The intent of this Madhyamaka
Is to host beings as guests with the inexhaustible.

"Inexhaustible" is inexhaustible liberation. "To host as guests" means to tend them as guests: with that inexhaustible liberation, beings are sustained with service and honor.

"Utterly empty" is emptiness, the absence of entities, whose character is to have entirely discarded even the conventional, which is everywhere spoken of as skillful means; for it does not employ even the conventional, which is held to be skillful means and is generated as an unreal body. "In order to reverse grasping at a self" means so that clinging to the self as supreme and grasping at the intrinsic natures of phenomena no longer appear. The intent is: "O, all that is conventional should be made fully clear." Thus it is explained in this very tantra:

Focusing on the pride of a buddha,
One should meditate on the luminous sphere of phenomena.

This was the Oral Transmission of the first chapter.

The particulars of offering are taught in the passage beginning "Then, the vajra." "The pure abode" is the wheel of great bliss. "The great abode" is the wheel of enjoyment. "Completely filled by all the buddhas" is the wheel of Dharma. "The pure and delightful city" is the wheel of the body, that is, the physical body. Speech and mind, too, are likewise shown to have the intrinsic nature of not arising as entities.

The passage beginning "Vajra chain" is the great essence of all the buddhas, outer and inner: it is approach (sevā) in its outer nature. Likewise, "Binding with the vajra fetter" is near approach. "They are stirred, filled with delight" is accomplishment. "The buddhas" means "they became buddhas": this is great accomplishment. The intent is that to attain such a fruit is to attain the great object of aspiration.

That one who abides in this way also abides as an unsurpassed bodhisattva is taught in the passage beginning "The Victor, the protector Maitreya." The practice of subjugation is taught in the passage beginning "By binding with the vajra chain." "Definitively condensed from the five hundred thousand" refers to the thirty chapters of the Manifest Awakening of Vajragarbha, and the rest.

The particulars that involve dance are taught in the passage beginning "The city of the source of phenomena." The commitment of vajra practice is the lotus and what arises from it. It is to be performed after one has attended upon the goddess with the vajra state itself and with those songs, because this is the very means of going to liberation. "Clay drums, graceful play, and the rest at the end" means that at the end one should also make music with clay drums and the like. "The seven should be purified" means:

With the four abodes of the buddhas made complete,
Having meditated on the five aspects,
Arisen from Samantabhadra,
The means of accomplishing one's own supreme deity
And the maṇḍala likewise are to be set out.
Offering, praise, tasting the nectar, and the rest
Are to be meditated on in sequence,
And one should apply them as the six limbs.

The eighth is the stage of self-blessing; that is the subtle yoga, which has already been explained. Another stage of self-blessing is taught in the passage beginning "Throat." "If drawn out at once" means causing it to rise upward. Its particular features are taught in the passage beginning "Immediately."

On the passage beginning "Bhūcarī and," someone may say: "Well then, since Bhūcarī is the vajra of speech, and she who dwells below the earth was explained as the vajra of body, does what is said here, which differs from what was explained before, not contradict it?" It does not. Bhūcarī has no connection with the vajra of body, nor is she who dwells below the earth connected with the vajra of speech, because they are seen not to be adjacent.

The intrinsic nature of the four elements is taught by way of the goddesses of the intermediate directions in the passage beginning "Awakening." "The mind of awakening, that is, the vase" means that the intrinsic nature of the water element is Śavarī. "Mountain" is the intrinsic nature of the earth element: Pukkasī. "Hearth" is the purified fire element: Caṇḍālī. "Great banner" is the very essence of wind: Ḍombinī.

The teaching of the sequence of the maṇḍala also points to the urging done by Caṇḍālī and Pukkasī, and also to the reality of loving-kindness and compassion. In this sense, too, it is to be explained as follows. "The mind of awakening" is what flows. "Mountain" is the lotus. "Hearth" and "great banner" are the vajra, because it brings about melting.

Nothing has been set down for the third chapter, since there is nothing in it to be transmitted orally. These were the Oral Transmission of the fourth chapter.

The Oral Transmission of the fifth chapter is given, on the passage beginning "Then." "It is the source of the maṇḍalas" refers to the condensed maṇḍalas of the deities' bodies. Its explanation is given in the words "Or dwells at the center of the eights": that is, the sets of eight goddesses, each set blessed by its own principal deity. The meaning is that one should also meditate on another maṇḍala, which abides at their center. One draws the maṇḍalas of the Ḍākinī Vajra Tent just as they are, and then in their center, too, the maṇḍala of the Ḍākinī Vajra Tent made up of nine deities. In the directions beginning with the east of the central maṇḍala are the maṇḍalas of Vairocana, Ratnasambhava, Padmanarteśvara, and Paramāśva, each consisting of nine. In this way each is held to take the form of whichever lord it belongs to. The maṇḍalas drawn in this way are to be drawn in the directions, beginning with the east, of that very central maṇḍala.

"Or in the five elements" means this: when one wants maṇḍalas with two levels, one draws them with two levels and makes their central level consist of five. In the directions beginning with the east of that center, too, each of these is made fivefold, like the central level, and so it is to be made a maṇḍala of twenty-five deities. The other two, as well, are said to become maṇḍalas of glorious Heruka, each consisting of nine, and the maṇḍala of Nairātmyā, consisting of thirteen; both of these, too, are condensed maṇḍalas.

The maṇḍala indicated by the gatekeeper goddesses of both maṇḍalas is taught in the passage beginning "The gatekeepers": Lāsyā, Nṛtyā, and the others are set out. "Renowned as the elephant and the rest" refers to the hand emblems of Heruka explained above. "The expanse of space" is the source of phenomena (dharmodaya). Its space is space itself, and its very essence is the abode of supreme liberation.

These were the Oral Transmission of the fifth chapter. The sixth is not explained.

"The body arisen from blood and semen" is merely illustrative: it comes to arise in these impurities of the realm of faults as well, but it does not arise in the form and formless realms in the way it comes about there. When it is said that birth takes place in the three realms, that there is no fourth beyond below the earth, upon the earth, and the heavens, and that the cause of birth for those who dwell there is that, then, like words spoken by an intoxicated mind, this is said with regard to what is known in the world; it is not what is known from the treatises.

The passage beginning "Lovely-faced one," and the words "Richly endowed with lotus flowers," speak briefly of the secret empowerment. "With the red lotus" speaks of wisdom-gnosis (prajñājñāna).

These were what is called The Explanation of Suchness from the Oral Transmission, which unravels whatever is difficult in the first part of the King of Tantras, the Ḍākinī Vajra Tent.

The Commentary on Difficult Points of the Ḍākinī Vajra Tent, composed by King Indrabhūti, is complete.

It was translated by the great Indian preceptor, the venerable Avadhūtīpa called Sūryagupta, and the translator, the monk Shakya Tsöndrü.

Evident scribal slips are read as corrected: zhabs "feet" for zhags in the second homage verse; nang na "among" for na ngan; mkha' "space" for mkhan; me "fire" for mi in the verse on the elements; ser mo (Gaurī, as in the verse on the aggregates) for sor mo; chos for chod in "sphere of phenomena"; reg bya "the tangible" for rig bya; bar snang "intermediate space" for par snang; and minor orthographic slips such as bzhes for zhes in the title. The contraction 'gro bde is read as 'gro ba de, "those beings." In the verse on the sense objects, chal is read as cha la and reg byas as reg bya sa (Bhūcarī). "Earth" at the head of the verse on the elements is supplied; it appears to have dropped out after gsungs pa ni. The goddesses of earth, fire and wind, spos byed ma, grags 'dzin ma and sme mo in this Tibetan, are identified as Pukkasī, Caṇḍālī and Ḍombinī from the parallel verse on the elements in the Hevajra Tantra. Sa 'og gnas pa mo is translated by its meaning, "she who dwells below the earth." Here rigs drug means the six tantric families (kula), not the six classes of beings. In the closing lines of the verse on the purified afflictions, gti mug rdo rje is taken as the goddess Mohavajrā, as in the verse that follows.  Evident scribal slips are read as corrected: ma tshang "lacking" for mtshang, giving "twenty less one," that is, nineteen; yin pa for ya ni pa; bsgrub pa "what is to be established" for sgrub pa in the sentence on fire and smoke, as the parallel argument about stream-entry requires; the letter a for ya, since the moon disc arises from the vowels and the sun disc from the consonants beginning with ka; and chos kyi dbyings "sphere of phenomena" for chos kyi dbyibs. The verb sgrub is rendered "establish" throughout the passage on fruit and cause because the commentary uses it in both senses, bringing a result about and proving it, as fire and smoke illustrate in inference. In the ways desire-realm beings are bound, lta ba is gazing, one of the modes of union of the desire-realm gods, not "view." The verse beginning "Though the Buddha's own nature" corresponds to a verse of Dharmakīrti's Pramāṇavārttika whose subject is cognition (buddhi); this Tibetan reads "Buddha" and is translated as it stands.  Evident scribal slips are read as corrected: gru bzhi, "square," for grub bzhi, and yan lag drug, "six limbs," for yan lag drub. Names I cannot identify with confidence are translated by their meaning: rtag pa, "the Eternal One," linked here with mirror-like wisdom, is perhaps Śāśvata, a name of Vairocana in the Hevajra literature; skam pa mo, "the Tongs Lady"; nyi phyag ma, "the Sun-Handed Lady"; padmo, "the Lotus Lady"; and two of the musician goddesses, rdza rnga ma, "the Clay Drum Lady," and rde'u chung ma, "the Little Pebble Lady." The title rdo rje snying po mngon par byang chub pa is likewise translated literally. Drag 'dzin ma, spelled grags 'dzin ma in the first piece, is again Caṇḍālī. Rim pa, rendered "level" in the account of the maṇḍala, could instead mean the two stages of generation and completion.  Evident scribal slips are read as corrected: spos byed ma (Pukkasī) for sbos byed ma; nam mkha' for rnam mkha'; gang gi tshe "when" for gang gi tsho; de gnyis char "both of these" for de nyid char; sbas pa (Sūryagupta) for spas pa; and zhal snga "the venerable" for zhal sda. The colophon's a lba dhū tī pa is read as Avadhūtīpa, used here as a title. Skyon gyi khams, contrasted here with the form and formless realms, is taken as the desire realm, "the realm of faults." Wisdom-gnosis (prajñājñāna) is the name of the third empowerment, which follows the secret empowerment.
