In the language of India: Ārya Arśa Praśamanī Sūtra. In the Tibetan language: The Noble Sūtra That Thoroughly Pacifies Hemorrhoids.

Homage to all buddhas and bodhisattvas.

Thus did I hear. At one time the Blessed One was dwelling at Rājagṛha, in the Bamboo Grove of Kalandaka, in the Cool Grove, together with a great Saṅgha of monks numbering around five hundred.

Then the Blessed One, on that very day of the full-moon restoration ceremony of the fifteenth, took his seat upon a prepared throne before the Saṅgha. At that time the venerable Ānanda stood to one side of the Blessed One holding a fan, fanning the Blessed One.

Then the venerable Ānanda arranged his upper robe over one shoulder, placed his right knee on the ground, joined his palms toward the Blessed One, bowed in the direction where the Blessed One was, and spoke these words to the Blessed One:

"Blessed One, here in Rājagṛha there are many monks afflicted with hemorrhoids who are tormented by the pain of hemorrhoidal disease, experiencing unbearable, violent, unpleasant, and disagreeable feelings of suffering. Venerable Blessed One, how shall I treat them?"

When he had spoken thus, the Blessed One declared these words to the venerable Ānanda:

"Ānanda, learn this sūtra for clearing away hemorrhoids. Ānanda, whoever knows the name, the words, and the syllables of this sūtra for clearing away hemorrhoids — for as long as they live, they will not be struck by the disease of hemorrhoids, and for seven lifetimes they will be able to recall their past lives."

Tadyathā: Alante, amale, śvālicinī, kuśe, sambhave, svāhā.

"Ānanda, it is like this: in the northern direction, on the king of mountains called Himavat, there is a śāla tree called the Victorious One. It has three flowers. The first is called the Swelling One, the second is called the Soft One, the third is called the Dry One. Just as that one is dried, so may the wind-hemorrhoids of sentient beings be dried up. May the bile-hemorrhoids be dried up. May the phlegm-hemorrhoids be dried up. May the impure-hemorrhoids be dried up. May the blood-hemorrhoids be dried up. May the combined-hemorrhoids be dried up. Likewise, may the phlegm-hemorrhoids of myself and all sentient beings be dried up. May the impure-hemorrhoids be dried up. May the blood-hemorrhoids be dried up. May the combined-hemorrhoids be dried up. May the nasal-hemorrhoids be dried up. May the pendant-hemorrhoids be dried up.

Tadyathā: Śame śamāne, śame śamāne, svāhā.

May there be no inflammation. May there be no suppuration. May there be no discharge of sound. May there be no oozing. May there be no suffering. May there be no extreme suffering. May there be no outflow. May there be no excessive outflow. May they be exhausted right here, completely exhausted, and rendered nonexistent. May they go down into the earth.

Namo bhagavate, buddhāya, satyavātīnaṃ, siddhyantu mantrapadaḥ, svāhā."

When the Blessed One had spoken thus, the venerable Ānanda was delighted and rejoiced at what the Blessed One had said, and he praised it.

As for the ritual procedure, it is as follows: having accomplished the recitation, recite this seven times over a red thread, tie seven knots, and hang it around the neck.

The Noble Sūtra That Thoroughly Pacifies Hemorrhoids is complete.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The three "flowers" of the śāla tree named Victorious One are almost certainly therapeutic or botanical referents within an Indian medical framework correlating the three flowers (described as swelling, soft, and dry) with the drying action invoked for each type of hemorrhoid (wind, bile, phlegm, impure, blood, and combined). This is a folk-medical simile embedded in a ritual-dhāraṇī context rather than a straightforward botanical description.

The Sanskrit dhāraṇī syllables are transliterated from the Tibetan phonetic renderings and are given here as they appear; the original Sanskrit forms may have been slightly altered in transmission.

The term rendered throughout as "hemorrhoids" (Tibetan: gzhang 'brum) denotes a range of anal and rectal growths or fistular conditions in traditional Tibetan medical literature, broadly corresponding to Sanskrit arśas, which covers hemorrhoids, fistulas, and related conditions. The title Sanskrit Arśa Praśamanī confirms this reading.
