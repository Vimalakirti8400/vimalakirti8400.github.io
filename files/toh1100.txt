Verses of Auspiciousness Requested by a God (Devaparipṛcchāmaṅgalagāthā)

Verses of Auspiciousness Requested by a God, known in Sanskrit as Devaparipṛcchāmaṅgalagāthā and in Tibetan as Lhas zhus pa'i bkra shis kyi tshigs su bcad pa, is a short verse text in which a god approaches the Buddha while he dwells in a solitary place and asks what the supreme auspiciousness is, since gods and humans each speak of auspicious things in their own way. In reply, the Buddha lists the conditions he regards as truly auspicious, beginning with living in a good land, keeping excellent friends, having made merit in the past, and setting the mind well on its course. The work closely parallels the well-known Discourse on Great Blessings of the Pali tradition, and it reframes worldly ideas of good fortune in terms of virtue, wholesome circumstances, and spiritual practice. It is Toh 1100 in the Kangyur, where it appears among the short works gathered near the end of the collection of dhāraṇīs and related recitation texts, valued for their auspicious and protective use.

In the language of Tibet: Verses of Auspiciousness Requested by a God.

Homage to the Three Jewels.

When the perfect Buddha, unconquered by others,
The Protector, was dwelling in a solitary place,
A certain god bowed to the great hero
And addressed him with these words:

"When they are seeking what is virtuous,
Gods and humans, each in their own way,
Pronounce various words of auspiciousness.
What is the supreme auspiciousness?"

The Blessed One said:

"When they are seeking what is virtuous,
Gods and humans, each in their own way,
Speak of things that are auspicious.
Those auspicious things I shall declare.

To dwell in a good land, to have excellent friends,
To have made merit in the past,
And to have set the mind well on its course:
This is the supreme auspiciousness.

Generosity, and speech that is most pleasing,
Certainty about the true meaning,
And keeping company with those who have moral discipline:
This is the supreme auspiciousness.

Not relying on the childish,
Relying on the wise,
And honoring those worthy of honor:
This is the supreme auspiciousness.

Serving and honoring one's father and mother,
Peace, and the practice of celibate conduct,
And compassion for all living beings:
This is the supreme auspiciousness.

Turning away from deeds of wrongdoing,
Relying on deeds of virtue,
Conscientiousness, and constant skill:
This is the supreme auspiciousness.

To be without pride and without conceit,
To live in harmony with contentment,
And always to repay the kindness one has received:
This is the supreme auspiciousness.

Not reviling others, and likewise patience,
Associating in harmony with the noble ones,
And listening to the Dharma at the proper times:
This is the supreme auspiciousness.

Freedom from laziness, great learning,
Thorough certainty about the Dharma,
And likewise a mind free of turbidity:
This is the supreme auspiciousness.

When one deeply cultivates a mind of loving-kindness
Toward all sentient beings,
Immeasurable and unshaken:
This is the supreme auspiciousness.

When one has fully purified the eye of wisdom
With regard to the truths of the noble ones,
And one's moral discipline has not been impaired:
This is the supreme auspiciousness.

Seeing the truths of the noble ones,
Cultivating celibate conduct,
And coming to attain nirvāṇa:
This is the supreme auspiciousness.

When one has completely abandoned attachment, aversion,
And ignorance as well,
And unshakable knowledge comes to arise:
This is the supreme auspiciousness.

When, though stirred by the worldly concerns,
One's mind does not waver,
Stainless, peaceful, and free of sorrow:
This is the supreme auspiciousness.

That and this, each as is fitting:
Whatever wise person praises
These auspicious things here,
For that one there will always be auspiciousness."

The Verses of Auspiciousness Requested by a God are complete.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

This text is the Tibetan counterpart of the Maṅgala Sutta. The "worldly concerns" are the eight worldly dharmas: gain and loss, fame and disrepute, praise and blame, pleasure and pain. In the final verse, the Tibetan reads stod, which is taken as bstod, "to praise." The first line of that verse, "That and this, each as is fitting," is terse in the Tibetan and is translated literally.
