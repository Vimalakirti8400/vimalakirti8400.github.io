The Dhāraṇī Called "Cloud of Offerings" (Pūjameghanāmadhāraṇī)

The Dhāraṇī Called "Cloud of Offerings," known in Sanskrit as the Pūjameghanāmadhāraṇī and in Tibetan by a title beginning Mchod pa'i sprin, is a short scripture centered on a single dhāraṇī, a sacred incantation addressed to the buddhas and invoking the indestructible vajra in its many forms, culminating in the purification of all karmic obscurations. After presenting the mantra, the text explains its rite and praises its power: merely calling it to mind is said to equal making offerings and bowing to as many buddhas throughout the ten directions as there are fine particles in the sands of the Ganges, honoring them with flowers, incense, perfumes, garlands, robes, parasols, banners, and every other kind of offering. In this way it offers practitioners a simple yet vast means of accumulating merit through recollection and recitation. It is Toh 1068 in the Kangyur, where it belongs among the dhāraṇī texts, the class of works devoted to potent spells and their accompanying practices.

In the language of India: pūja megha nāma dhāraṇī. In the language of Tibet: The Dhāraṇī Called "Cloud of Offerings."

Homage to the Three Jewels.

namo ratnatrayāya namo bhagavate vajrasārapramardani tathāgatāya arhate saṃmyaksaṃbuddhāya tadyathā oṃ vajre vajre mahāvajre mahātejavajre mahāvidyāvajre mahābodhicittavajre mahāpodhimaṇḍovasaṃkramaṇavajra sarvakarma āvarṇaviśodhanavajre svāhā

This is the rite for this dhāraṇī-mantra. The very moment one merely calls it to mind, one will have made offerings to as many buddhas, blessed ones, dwelling in the ten directions, as there are finest particles in the sands of the river Ganges, and will have bowed at their feet. One will have honored them with flowers, incense, perfumes, garlands, ointments, powders, robes, parasols, victory banners, banners of every kind, pennants, cushions, garments, gifts, pure food and the like, ornaments, and all kinds of streamers.

One will be freed from all wrongdoing and will come to possess all the roots of virtue. Seeing all the buddhas and bodhisattvas, one will be truly consoled. All the gods, nāgas, yakṣas, gandharvas, asuras, garuḍas, kiṃnaras, and mahoragas, Vajrapāṇi and the others together with the Lord of Secrets, and the Four Great Kings will follow close behind, and will guard, protect, and shelter one.

And when one passes away from this life, one will be reborn in the world-realm of Sukhāvatī. These special qualities are obtained without any practice, merely by hearing it once.

Here ends the dhāraṇī called "Cloud of Offerings."

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The final sentence reads "merely by ngan pa once." As written, ngan pa means "bad," which makes no sense here. It has been taken as a copyist's slip for nyan pa, "hearing," which differs by one similar-looking letter. Another verb, such as "reciting," may have been intended.
