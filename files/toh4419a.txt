A Distant Song Summoning the Dear Friend of the Virtue and Excellence of Ground, Path and Fruit: Dedication Verses and Printing Colophon of the Derge Tengyur

This text, catalogued as Toh 4419a in the Tengyur, is the dedication and printing colophon of the Derge edition of the Tengyur, the Tibetan collection of Indian treatises that explain the Buddha's words. Its verses of dedication and aspiration are titled A Distant Song Summoning the Dear Friend of the Virtue and Excellence of Ground, Path and Fruit, in Tibetan Gzhi lam 'bras bu'i dge legs yid gcugs mdza' bo 'gugs pa'i rgyang glu. Opening with Sanskrit verses of blessing and continuing in ornate Tibetan poetry, it praises the Buddha and his teaching, dedicates the merit of the great printing project toward the awakening and happiness of all beings, and then records in prose how the blocks were carved: at the command of the great abbot Tashi Lhündrub of Ngor, under the patronage of the Derge rulers Tenpa Tsering and his son Kunga Trinlé Gyatso, beginning in the fire snake year of 1737 and completed at Derge Lhündrubteng in the water dog year of 1742. It was composed by the edition's chief proofreader, the monk Tsultrim Rinchen, and it stands among the catalogue and colophon materials that close the Tengyur, rather than among its translated Indian treatises.

It came about in this way. There is the great being, the regent who received the nectar of prophecy from the excellent vase of the throat of the Blessed One, the Lord of Sages; the great one who in wisdom is indistinguishable from the Victor himself, renowned in every direction as the glorious Vajradhara Kunga Zangpo. Upon the high throne of that very one, its footstool upheld by fearless five-faced lords stretching forth, he placed his pair of feet marked with wheels; and with the wheel of his pure white activity he seems to measure the limits of the path of the immortals. If one speaks his name, he is the Great Abbot Mañjughoṣa Tashi Lhündrub Drakpé Gyaltsen Palzangpo. From him came a command, prefaced by its immeasurable benefits, such as that if these great commentaries on the intent of the complete teaching were carved into printing blocks, it would become a cause for the precious teaching of the Victor to remain long in the world.

In reliance on that command, there is one whose secret drum of fame pervades the whole circle of the horizons: the great lord of the land of Derge, known as Tenpa Tsering. Among the lands most highly praised as the great region where the stainless tradition of the Omniscient Ikṣvāku has fully spread, he holds sway over most of the earthly sphere known as Great Tibet, with a wheel of activity and power in accord with the Dharma system of the ten virtues; and in particular, by the decree of the great Emperor of the East, appointed by Heaven, he was enthroned as the general lord of the beings of the land of Great Tibet. Among all the ways of conduct of the noble and excellent that are worthy of praise, he held in his heart, as the excellent jewel of scripture and reasoning, that to accomplish the guru's command just as it is given is the very highest. He promised to accomplish it exactly as the excellent Great Abbot had commanded, and he began the work on an auspicious day of a month of the fire snake year called Piṅgala.

From then on, the dominant condition that brought this work to completion, in its beginning, middle and end, through a burden borne with marvelous courage, is his stainless son. When he was enthroned, with inconceivable varieties of offerings from gods and humans, as victorious over all directions, upon the high throne that guards as one the jewel heap of the merit and the fortune of all the beings of the land of Great Tibet, he became widely renowned as the Derge Lama Kunga Trinlé Gyatsö Dé, whose thousand-spoked wheel of activity in Dharma and in government travels to the ends of the directions. In the broad womb of the Gold-Bearing Earth, the beloved one of his karmic power and immeasurable merit, he climbed to the peak of supreme delight in taking up the whole burden of this great ocean of good deeds that no one before had been able to create, and he steered the activity of accomplishing his wishes just as they were intended.

The two romanized lines are a Sanskrit version, irregularly spelled, of the two Tibetan verses that follow each of them. In the second Tibetan verse, pos ba is read as mos pa, "aspiration," which matches adhimukti in the Sanskrit line. Ma mo in the stanza on the king is read as master copies, that is, carved printing blocks, not as mātṛkā goddesses; the passage praises a ruler who had the translated commentaries carved for printing. The "maker of all the world" is the creator god of Indian myth, Brahmā, said to have fashioned the Sahā world. The passage ends in mid-sentence; it continues in the next piece.  The syllables ཏསྲུ་རས in the stanza on Kunga Zangpo make no sense as written; the line is rendered by its evident sense, "in the speech of the Omniscient One." Several stanzas praise a ruler and a hierarch of the Sakya tradition in ornate kāvya style, and some phrases, such as "ocean of the activity of the Victor" and "ocean of good qualities," may conceal personal names; they are translated by their meaning. Kunga Zangpo is probably Ngorchen Kunga Zangpo, founder of the Ngor tradition. "Sapphire drops" reads ཨནྡྷ་རྙིལ as a form of indranīla, sapphire.  In the first line, འཛུམ ("smile") is read as ཟུམ ("close"), since "may his eyes never smile" makes no sense here. In the stanza on the Buddha's descent from Tuṣita, ཕྱི་བའི་བེེད is unclear as written; it is rendered by its likely sense, "to make rich." In the following stanza a syllable is missing before རྩི; the evident reading is bdud rtsi, "nectar." The verses on generosity through wisdom follow the six perfections, and the four that follow the four means of attraction.  This piece is the printing colophon of the Derge Tengyur. The two hundred volumes "exceeding that by the auspicious signs" is taken as two hundred plus eight (the eight auspicious signs). Piṅgala (fire snake) corresponds to 1737 and Dundubhi (male water dog) to 1742. "Kinsman of the Sun" (Ādityabandhu) and "the Omniscient Ikṣvāku" (literally "Sugarcane One") are epithets of the Buddha.
