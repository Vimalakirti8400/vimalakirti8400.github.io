The Fivefold Pledge (Samayapañca)

The Fivefold Pledge, known in Sanskrit as the Samayapañca and in Tibetan as Damtsik Ngapa, is a short verse manual on the feast gathering (gaṇacakra), the communal tantric rite in which initiated practitioners assemble to make offerings, share consecrated food and drink, and sing and dance as deities. Opening with homage to Vajrasattva, it sets out when and where the feast should be held, the qualities required of its master and its participants, the arrangement of the seats and consorts, the offering of torma and the inner fire offering, the code words and the five nectars and meats, the etiquette of receiving the skull cup, and the conduct expected of everyone present, closing with aspiration prayers. Its colophon attributes it to the master Padmākara and records that it was translated by the Khampa translator Bari Dharmakīrti at Vikramaśīla monastery. It is Toh 1224 in the Tengyur, where it belongs among the commentaries and ritual works on the tantras.

In the language of India: samayapañca. In the language of Tibet: The Fivefold Pledge.

Homage to glorious Vajrasattva.

Having paid homage to glorious Vajrasattva, the loving one,
who bestows in full every accomplishment of perfect bliss without exception,
I shall explain, in brief, the beautiful rite of the feast gathering,
most secret, taught in the glorious secret tantras and others.

On special nights the mantra practitioner should hold it, day after day, as often as possible;
if even a single month passes without the circle, one falls into a lapse.
In a charnel ground free of harm, in a hermitage, on the bank of a pond, or in a pleasure grove,
on a lake shore, by a river, in a mountain hollow, in one's own home and so forth, one should perform the circle that grants results.

One who has received an authentic lineage,
glorious, content, endowed with compassion,
unattached to worldly concerns,
who has wholly abandoned craving, deceit and the like,
who speaks pleasantly, with a smiling face,
patient, steadfast and endowed with diligence:
such a one, glorious Vajrasattva has said,
is to be the master of the feast gathering.

For that very authentic circle,
the disciples have wholly abandoned craving and the like,
have received empowerment, are intelligent and patient,
and are endowed with diligence, mindfulness and so forth.
Those who carry out the pledges that are to be carried out
are to be placed in order of seniority.
Unbeknown to those who have not received empowerment,
the practitioner should perform that circle.

With flowers, incense, ointments and lamps,
richly adorned with feasts of many kinds and with drinks,
with couches and cushions and all good things,
it is held for many noble persons, or for an assembly.

Then the seat of the master of the gathering
should be made of a corpse, or of a tiger skin, or of a corpse's skin.
The ornaments are to be made of clay.
Embraced by a consort (mudrā) who is no outsider,
the master is surrounded by their own disciples,
and each of them too embraces their own consort (vidyā).
Those who are unable should do this through meditative concentration.

A disciple who knows the rite and keeps it in mind,
who is endowed with diligence and patience,
who is devoted to the guru and intelligent,
should be appointed the action vajra (karmavajra).

Even to those who have received empowerment, if they are malicious,
to those who seek gain and those who merely follow along,
and to those who have not received empowerment, the circle
should not be shown, not even from afar.

The guru, having meditated on great bliss,
gives torma to all the bhūtas.
Having purified the "strength" and the rest,
one worships one's own body with flowers.
One vessel is given to each and every one;
to the guru two vessels are offered.
Likewise the guru's portions of food, drink and the rest are doubled.

Then, with one's own mouth as the hearth,
one makes the oblation to consciousness, the sāla.
With the hands as the ladle,
one burns the "strength" and the rest in the fire with the mind.
By this inner fire offering of oneself,
one satisfies one's chosen deity.

One should give delight with songs and the like,
and again and again speak the code words.
The five nectars and the five lamps,
and the eight consorts of excellent family,
one should know how to rely on, according to the rite.
One should perform the kolāla and whatever else one knows,
and dance in the form of Heruka;
one should practice yoga with mindfulness, without forgetting.

With a mind devoted to meditation,
one should continually train the mind.
The yoginīs of the vajra, of the Dharma and of the buddhas,
and the hosts of mothers as well, should train in song and dance,
and should perform song and dance supremely.
This itself is the protection of the gathering,
and it protects oneself as well.
By this the world is brought under one's power;
it is the very essence of mantra recitation.

Where songs are sung continually
and dances are always performed,
with the master of the gathering placed at the fore,
then the scents should be signified.
The scent of garlic comes first,
then the scent of the vulture as well,
then the scent of camphor and sandalwood:
these are to be signified within the song.
As for sounds, the sound of geese and of bees,
heard at the close of the sounds,
and the sounds of jackals as well,
are signified as the outer pleasure grove.

If the genuine substances cannot be found,
or to avoid danger from those who are not fit vessels,
the five nectars, the five meats and the rest
should be fully imagined through meditative concentration.

The vessel blessed by the master of the circle,
in order to make good any fault in the vessel,
since it is beset by māras,
should be taken neither too fast nor too slow;
to avoid the contempt of obstructors and the rest,
it should be received with the lotus mudrā.
Without any slurping sound and without distraction,
one drinks a little, fills it well again,
and, with a special act of homage,
offers it with the appropriate mudrā.
The practitioner should pay homage
to all of them again and again.

If the vessel is set down on the ground
or handed to another,
the seed in the hand is cut off as a penalty.
One should not drink all of it oneself.
Food and drink one should not take for oneself,
even when very hungry.

Whoever engages in quarrels and the like
should then be expelled.
Or, if one is unable to expel them,
then, regarding it as wrongdoing,
one should oneself go elsewhere,
or meet it with silence.

For the action vajra, there is no fault
even in taking drink and the rest for oneself.
If someone goes out under the press of bodily needs,
that should not be prevented.

Even if one is reviled in ignoble words
by those who, from conceit and aversion, revile others,
quarrelsome talk
and displeasure should still be avoided.

If one has engaged in what is forbidden,
then for root downfalls and gross offenses
one should swiftly perform the rite of restoration;
this is known as the rite of rising again.

When the vajra master is satisfied,
the intermediate torma should be given.
The continuing of song, dance and the like
should be granted by the master's command.

What is to be done in the assembled gathering:
one should keep hands, feet and the rest with care.
Because it is a cause of great heedlessness,
one should not partake of much drink.
Through much, one becomes distracted;
through distraction, yoga is disturbed;
when yoga lapses, there is no accomplishment;
and the works of Māra are hard to turn back.

Whoever is able to guard the mind
should worship both oneself and others.
Through the purity of form and all the various objects,
by that, this becomes accomplishment.
Not even for an instant should the wise,
who wish for success and seek accomplishment with effort,
dwell in another form,
with a mind ignorant and malicious.

However many the movements of the body
and however many the utterances of speech,
that many are mantras and mudrās:
one abides in the state of glorious Heruka.

That by which a piece of poison
brings people to their death:
one who knows the true nature of poison
destroys poison with that very poison.

If some, having other business,
are unable to stay long,
they should bow, inform the master,
ask forgiveness, and then depart.

The activities taught in this
and in other tantras should be done at leisure,
because doing them in haste
spoils the joy of the yogins.

Thus, in the great feast gathering,
because one's own disciples are empowered,
through the very power of meditation and the rest,
accomplishment comes in this very life.
Likewise here, through meditation and the rest,
and also by making the command clear,
if there is no downfall,
one attains it within sixteen lives.

The guru does not give the final torma,
nor likewise the outer leftover torma.
The practitioner pays homage
and makes aspirations in this way:

Beyond all conceptual thought, in union where existence and peace are equal, forever unchanging,
delighting in the taste of incomparable bliss: to the supreme yoginī I pay homage.

United with the vajra woman,
the heap of conceptions is utterly destroyed:
by this virtue of mine,
may the means to that be swiftly shown.

Having paid homage with all things,
one concludes the glorious feast gathering
and should then remain at ease.

This, received through the succession of gurus,
I have set down, a little of the rite,
in order to remind myself;
may the learned be patient with it.

By whatever merit I have gained by clearly writing down the rite of the feast gathering,
basis of all accomplishments and preceded by good qualities,
may the fortunate realm of glorious Vajradhara swiftly be embraced,
and, freed from existence, may the peaceful Great Seal (mahāmudrā) be attained.

The Fivefold Pledge, composed by the master, glorious Padmākarapāda, is complete.

It was translated, edited and finalized by the Khampa translator Bari Dharmakīrti at the temple of glorious Vikramaśīla, the source of reasoning.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The title is taken as "fivefold" (lnga pa after a noun); it could also be read "The Fifth Pledge." In the line on abandoning "craving and the like," the text's rgyu ("cause") makes little sense in a list of faults; it is read as sgyu, "deceit." "Strength" (stobs, Skt. bala) is here a code word of the feast for meat, as in the Hevajra Tantra's coded language. The ornaments of 'dam bag are taken as clay; the exact sense is uncertain. In "those who seek gain and those who merely follow along" (thob dang rgyun can), and in "the oblation to consciousness, the sāla" (rnam rig sā la'i mchod yon), the wording is obscure and the renderings are tentative. In the doubling of the guru's portions, nas mtshan is not clearly construable and is left implicit. Kolāla (ko la la) is left untranslated as a term of the rite whose meaning is uncertain. Chad pa is rendered "penalty," its sense in this context of conduct at the feast, rather than "annihilation," and the line on "the seed in the hand" is obscure.  In "the guru does not give the final torma, nor likewise the outer leftover torma," the negative is kept as written, but the line may well have read gtor ma sbyin, "the guru gives the final torma," with mi as a slip for ma; the feast rite normally includes both the final and the leftover torma. "Oneself and others" renders rang dam gzhan, taking dam as a slip for ngam or dang. The monastery name is written kra ma shī la, evidently Vikramaśīla with its first syllable lost.
