The Great Sūtra on What Accords with Wandering Everywhere and Not Wandering Everywhere (Āṭānāṭīya-mahāsūtra)

The Āṭānāṭīya Mahāsūtra, known in Tibetan as the Great Sūtra on What Accords with Wandering Everywhere and Not Wandering Everywhere, is Toh 656 in the Kangyur, classified among the collection's sūtras. It belongs to a well-known Buddhist textual tradition associated with the protection dhāraṇī called the Āṭānāṭīya, which is recited for warding off malevolent spirits and safeguarding practitioners. The sūtra is set at Śrāvastī, where the Great King Vaiśravaṇa — one of the four guardian kings and lord of the yakṣas — visits the Buddha by night with a vast retinue of yakṣas, introducing what becomes a discourse on the powerful protective formula associated with his name and domain. This text is part of a broader canonical tradition found across various Buddhist schools, and its inclusion in the Kangyur reflects the importance placed on protective ritual literature within Tibetan Buddhist practice.

In the language of India: Āṭānāṭīya-mahāsūtra nāma. In the language of Tibet: The Great Sūtra on What Accords with Wandering Everywhere and Not Wandering Everywhere.

Homage to the Three Jewels.

Thus did I hear. At one time the Blessed One was dwelling at Śrāvastī in the Jeta Grove, the garden of Anāthapiṇḍada. Then the Great King Vaiśravaṇa, of brilliant complexion, accompanied by many yakṣa retainers, by many hundreds of yakṣa retainers, by many thousands of yakṣa retainers, and by many hundreds of thousands of yakṣa retainers, when the night had grown still, went to where the Blessed One was. Having arrived, he bowed his head at the feet of the Blessed One and sat down to one side. At that time, by the power of the brilliant complexion of the Great King Vaiśravaṇa, the entire Jeta Grove was illuminated with a great light.

Seated to one side, the Great King Vaiśravaṇa then offered these verses:

O perfect buddha, best among two-footed beings,
what you, O wise one, have come to know
was not sought out even by the gods.
To you, O great hero, I pay homage.

To all the buddhas who have passed away,
those who have appeared now, and those yet to come —
to all of them I pay homage,
and to you as well I come for refuge.

Venerable one, among the śrāvaka disciples of the Blessed One — the monks, the nuns, the laymen, and the laywomen — there are those who dwell in hermitages, forest edges, and border regions. Among them there are yakṣas and non-humans who are innately malevolent, some of whom are devoted to the sacred words of the Blessed One and some of whom are not. Those yakṣas and innately malevolent non-humans who are devoted to the sacred words of the Blessed One are exceedingly few, while those yakṣas and innately malevolent non-humans who are not devoted to the sacred words of the Blessed One are very many. Therefore, venerable one, in order to guard, protect, and establish in comfort the monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen among the śrāvaka disciples of the Blessed One; to bring those yakṣas and innately malevolent non-humans who are not devoted to faith; and to further increase and multiply those who are already devoted — it would be excellent if this very sūtra that accords with wandering everywhere and not wandering everywhere, together with its vidyā-mantras, protective spells, and foot-treading salutations, were to be held in its fullness and brought to complete fulfillment.

When he had said "Excellent! Excellent!" the Blessed One signified his approval of the Great King Vaiśravaṇa by remaining silent.

Then the Great King Vaiśravaṇa, understanding that the Blessed One had granted approval through his silence, bowed his head at the feet of the Blessed One, and at that time, in the presence of the Blessed One, he recited at length this very sūtra that accords with wandering everywhere and not wandering everywhere, together with its vidyā-mantras, protective spells, and foot-treading salutations:

The yakṣas who come to fulfill all desires, possessed of great miraculous power and great strength —

Reverend one, beyond the hollow city of Uśira: there are places of bodily abiding, and spacious abiding-places, and beyond those, places with streams flowing in their courses — many with flowing streams, and beyond those still more — appearing in the intermediate sky and the heavens like birds.

Reverend one, to the north of the hollow cities there is an assembly ground called the place of dancers (Nṛtyabhūmi). There the yakṣas gather and dwell. Among them are those with ugly feet and drooping lips, and those with fearsome bristling bellies. There the trees bear fruit without cease. Peacocks, cranes with melodious calls, cuckoos with their great resounding cries, and the śaṅkhaṭeyu birds all give voice. The sounds of the peacocks, too — the gaukomṛdha and the cañcalā birds and various other birds — raise their calls, delighting in the taste of flowers and honey. Those of unpleasant bodies sport with singing and dancing, playing together with the lotus-born ones.

Reverend one, beyond the willow city (Vañjulavatī) lies the country of the king called Vīra, in the border regions of his realm. The assembly ground where I dwell is called the excellent herd (Uccaihśravas). My name is the Son of Vaiśravaṇa. This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The title phrase "wandering everywhere and not wandering everywhere" renders the Tibetan kun tu rgyu ba dang kun tu rgyu ba ma yin pa, which corresponds to the Sanskrit concept distinguishing roaming or pervasive spirits from those that are not roaming.
