The Noble Sūtra of the Hurled Stone (Ārya-śilākṣipta-sūtra)

Toh 295 in the Kangyur is a sūtra known in Sanskrit as the Ārya-śilākṣipta-sūtra and in Tibetan as the Phags pa rdo 'phangs pa'i mdo, rendered into English as The Noble Sūtra of the Hurled Stone. It belongs to the general class of canonical sūtra literature and recounts an episode in which the Buddha, wandering through the territory of the Mallas and resting in a dense grove of sal trees, instructs the Venerable Ānanda to accompany him toward the city of Kuśa. The narrative unfolds against the backdrop of a large assembly of Malla warriors gathered in council, suggesting the text is concerned with teachings or events arising from this dramatic social and political setting. As a canonical sūtra attributed to the direct speech of the Buddha, it is preserved among the sūtra collections of the Kangyur.

In the language of India: Ārya-śilākṣipta-sūtra. In the Tibetan language: The Noble Sūtra of the Hurled Stone. First fascicle.

Homage to the noble Mañjuśrī, ever youthful.

Thus did I hear. At one time, the Blessed One had been wandering through the territory of the Mallas and came to rest in a dense thicket of sal trees — the dark thicket of sal trees.

Then the Blessed One said to the Venerable Ānanda: "Ānanda, come here toward the city of Kuśa." "Venerable One, so be it," replied the Venerable Ānanda, and he followed the Blessed One's instruction.

Now at that time, the Mallas of the city of Kuśa numbered some five hundred warriors, and each of those warriors had a retinue of a hundred warriors each. Gathered and assembled in a certain meeting place, they spoke together in true words to this effect: "Friends, the Blessed One has been wandering through the territory of the Mallas and has come to rest in the dark thicket of sal trees. There, the Mallas born in that dark place paid homage to him with the finest of honors, offered the finest of offerings, and attended upon him with the utmost reverence fit for a teacher. And the Blessed One, for his part, bestowed upon them the supreme and sublime gift of Dharma, most excellent and unsurpassed, for their benefit. Now, as the Blessed One dwells there as he dwells, before long, surrounded by the assembly of monks, with the monastic Saṅgha leading the way, he will depart for the city of Kuśa — the realm of our own village. So then, ought we not, for our own benefit, pay homage and offer offerings to the Blessed One? Let us therefore prepare the entire city of Kuśa in proper ceremonial fashion: let banners and pennants be hung, let the streets be well-swept and well-cleaned, and let everything be adorned with every manner of ornament and bunting, with incense, garlands, flowers, perfume, and the most beautiful sounds of music. Let parasols, victory banners, and flags be raised. Let festival days be observed. And the path along which the Blessed One is expected to travel — that road of passage too shall be prepared in proper ceremonial fashion with banners and pennants. From then on, all of us will do nothing but go forth to receive him." So thinking, they all assembled together with genuine joy, free from dispute, and with one purpose and one mind, they prepared the entire city of Kuśa in proper ceremonial fashion with banners and pennants. The streets were well-swept and well-cleaned, and everything was adorned with every manner of ornament and bunting, with incense, garlands, flowers, perfume, and the most beautiful sounds of music. Parasols, victory banners, and flags were raised. Festivals were observed. And the path along which the Blessed One had set out — that road of passage too was prepared in proper ceremonial fashion with banners and pennants.

Now at that time, between the city of Kuśa and the river Hiraṇyavatī, in a place where it had no business being, there stood a large broad stone lying right in the middle of the great road, rising visibly some fifteen fathoms high and eight and a half fathoms wide. Then they thought: "What if we were to lift this great broad stone from its present location and set it to one side? That way the road will be most beautifully prepared, the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly and fully awakened Buddha will be honored, and the report, renown, and verse-praise of our great virtue will spread in all directions and quarters. Those who lift this great broad stone from its location and set it aside in whatever suitable direction —

"How wonderful! The champions of the city of Kuśa have shown courage! How wonderful! The champions of the city of Kuśa have shown themselves to be bold, possessed of bodies with the finest limbs, and endowed with the greatest strength!" — thinking thus, they reflected on it in this way and took their vows.

"O you who are wise, listen, you champions of the city of Kuśa, or you young sons of champions! I am resolved to strive to prepare this road. This great broad stone is lying where it should not be. It is obstructing the royal road. Therefore I shall lift this great broad stone from this side and set it to one side. By doing so, we will have the road most beautifully prepared, the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly and fully awakened Buddha will be honored, and the report, renown, and verse-praise of our great virtue will spread in all directions and quarters. Of those who lift this great broad stone from this side and set it to one side, it will be said: 'How wonderful! The champions of the city of Kuśa have shown courage! How wonderful! The champions of the city of Kuśa have shown themselves to be bold, possessed of bodies with the finest limbs, and endowed with the greatest strength!'"

Therefore, O wise ones, they all said "So be it — let us go to the great broad stone this very day!" and the champions of the city of Kuśa listened to one another. Then, each one mounted his finest steed, and thinking with pride "We are the most powerful on this earth," they came filled with arrogance over their lineage, their strength, their physique, and their lordly status, making loud and great noise, clamoring and shouting with excitement and glee, and with joy, happiness, and contentment arising in them. Setting out from the city of Kuśa with the greatest of grand retinues — horses, mares, the finest blue bulls, the best of herds, elephants, and many hundreds of fragrant elephants — they went to where the great broad stone lay. Arriving there, they wound it many times with strong, tough ropes of hide. Some rigged levers and pulled it this way and that with ropes of muñja grass, of balbaja grass, and with horse-traction ropes. Some wished to pry it from the ground with a hundred iron bars. Some wished to cut it with axes. Some wished to smash it to powder with vajra-hammers. Some wished to grip the edges with their own bodily strength and dislodge it entirely. Some wished to overturn it with the finest stallions and the best bulls. Some wished to break it apart by the force of a whole herd of cattle. Some wished to destroy it with the power of medicine. And some wished to make it vanish by the power of mantra. Yet with all that effort, exertion, and striving of theirs, as they set about lifting it from this side and setting it to one side, their bodies grew weary, their bodies grew exhausted, and their bodies bent low with supreme effort and became dispirited — they could not even budge the great broad stone, let alone raise or throw it.

Then the Blessed One, surrounded by the assembly of monks and with the Saṅgha of monks before him, set out toward the place where the great broad stone lay. The champions born in the city of Kuśa saw the Blessed One approaching from a great distance. And upon seeing him, they were delighted and glad, and joy, happiness, and contentment arose in them.

Great reverence for their teacher arose in them all, and they went forward to receive the Blessed One. They bowed their heads to the Blessed One's two feet. They circumambulated the Blessed One three times, and having done so, they stood to one side in silence.

The Blessed One then spoke these words to the champions of the city of Kuśa standing to one side: "Young men, have you undertaken this with supreme great effort? Are you straining yourselves?"

When he had said this, the champions of the city of Kuśa replied to the Blessed One: "Reverend Sir, we champions of the city of Kuśa — we heard that the Blessed One had traveled through the domain of champions and gone to the evil one, that he had entered into the lotus grove where the evil insects dwell. There, the champions born in that evil place paid him the highest honor in the form of painted images, the highest offering, and the highest reverence toward their teacher. And the Blessed One in turn benefited them with the supreme, exquisite, unsurpassed gift of the Dharma. Now we have heard that the Blessed One, surrounded by the assembly of monks and with the Saṅgha of monks before him, is journeying to the city of Kuśa, our own home territory. And upon hearing this, all of us thought: how fitting it would be if we too were to honor the Blessed One with painted images and make offerings to him for our own benefit, and were to sweep and clean the entire city of Kuśa according to proper ceremony, setting everything in good order, and were to adorn it with every ornament and pennant, and then to deck it further with incense, garlands, flowers, perfumes, and the sweet sounds of music; to erect parasols, victory banners, and flags; to establish a festival day; and in whatever spot the Blessed One had walked with effort, to sweep and clean that very path according to proper ceremony. We thought: it will be best if all of us together simply go to receive him. And so we all gathered together, rejoiced in proper harmony, free from discord, single-minded and of one heart, and swept and cleaned the entire city of Kuśa according to proper ceremony, set everything in good order, adorned it with every ornament and pennant, with incense, garlands, flowers, perfumes, and the sweet sounds of music, erected parasols, victory banners, and flags, established a festival day, and swept and cleaned according to proper ceremony the very path along which the Blessed One had walked. And it was then that we saw the great broad stone lying where it had not been before, resting in the great open space. We then thought: let us lift this great broad stone from this side and set it to one side, whichever side may be convenient — and then the road too will be very well swept, the Tathāgata, the arhat, the fully and perfectly awakened Buddha will be honored, and in all the cardinal and intermediate directions our great virtue will become widely renowned and celebrated in verse and song, and people will say, 'How brave are the champions of the city of Kuśa, who lifted that great broad stone from this side and cast it to the other.'"

"How wonderful — champions of the city of Kuśa have arisen, daring and bold, possessed of the finest bodily form, endowed with the greatest strength!" Thinking thus, they deliberated on the matter and resolved upon it. Then they said: "Wise ones, champions of the city of Kuśa, and you young sons of champions, hear us! We are striving to sweep this road clean. This great broad stone rests where it should not be and blocks the royal road. Therefore we shall lift this great broad stone from this side and set it to the other side. By doing so the road will be very well swept, the Tathāgata, the arhat, the fully and perfectly awakened Buddha will be honored, and in all the cardinal and intermediate directions our great virtue will become widely renowned and celebrated in verse and song, and people will say, 'How brave are the champions of the city of Kuśa — how daring and bold, possessed of the finest bodily form, endowed with the greatest strength, who lifted that great broad stone from this side and cast it to the other!' Therefore, O wise ones, let everyone come out now to where that great broad stone lies. So be it!" When they had spoken thus, each listened to the others in turn, and then each mounted his own fine horse. Filled with the conceit of thinking themselves the mightiest upon the earth, swollen with pride in their lineage, their strength, their physique, and their lordship, they came with loud voices and great clamor, shouting and making a din, pleased and delighted, joyful and happy and glad at heart. Setting out from the city of Kuśa in the greatest splendor, driving before them horses, she-camels, and the finest blue bulls, the finest of their herds, and many hundreds of elephants and perfume-bearing elephants, they went to where the great broad stone lay. They bound the great broad stone in every direction with strong ropes of twisted bark. Then some among them set up levers and tried to drag it hither and thither with ropes of muñja grass, of balvaja grass, and of horse-halter cord. Some wished to pry it up from the base with a hundred iron hooks. Some wished to cut it apart with battle-axes. Some wished to shatter it to powder by striking it with vajras. Some wished to press their lips together with the strength of their bodies and try to heave it by bodily force alone. Some wished to topple it with the finest horses and the finest bulls. Some wished to crush it utterly with a host of elephants. Some wished to dissolve it by the power of medicine. And some wished to make it vanish by the power of secret mantra. Yet for all their striving, their pulling, and their effort, they could not in the slightest move that great broad stone from its place — let alone lift it up or hurl it to one side. After twenty-nine days of effort, with their bodies exhausted, their bodies worn out, their bodies bent and drooping, and their minds dispirited, they had been unable to move that great broad stone from its place entirely. How then could they lift it or hurl it away?

The Blessed One declared: "Young ones, does the Tathāgata wish to lift that great broad stone from its place and hurl it to the other side?"

"Most excellent Blessed One, we implore you! Most excellent Sugata, we implore you!"

Then the Blessed One drew his robe over one shoulder and —

approached the place where the great broad stone lay, and going to it, he moved the great broad stone with the big toe of his right foot, stirred it completely, and drew it out from its foundation without remainder. Taking it up with his left hand, he set it in the palm of his right hand, paused for a moment in contemplation, and then hurled it upward to the very world of Brahmā, where it came to rest, vanishing entirely from sight. At the sound of this, the assembly of wrestlers was struck with terror.

The Blessed One declared: "Young ones of stable lineage, do not be afraid. Simply by being hurled upward, all formations are impermanent, all phenomena are without self, and nirvāṇa is peace" — and he caused just such a sound to ring out. Then it came back, and that same stone came to rest in the very palm of the Tathāgata's hand. The Blessed One then reduced it to the finest dust with the breath of his mouth. And having made it into powder, he caused a rain of finest dust-particles to fall in every direction, in all the quarters and intermediate directions.

Then the assembly of wrestlers, seeing that the great broad stone was no more, became exceedingly distressed. Thereupon the Blessed One, with his mind knowing all, perceived the thoughts of that entire assembly of wrestlers, and understanding that they were exceedingly distressed at the absence of the great broad stone, he gathered and assembled from without every last particle of that stone-dust, without any loss or deficiency, and fashioned it once more into exactly that same great broad stone, and caused it to rest in a solitary place to one side.

Then those five hundred or so wrestlers, drawn in by the Blessed One through these great and wondrous qualities, became filled with delight, joy, happiness, and contentment of mind, and addressed the Blessed One with these words: "Blessed One, what was that? Was it the power born of the Tathāgata's father and mother? Or was it the power of miraculous ability? Or was it the power of meditation? Young ones of stable lineage, the Tathāgata's moving the great broad stone with the big toe of his right foot, drawing it out from its foundation without remainder, taking it up with his left hand, setting it in his right palm, pausing for a moment in contemplation, and then hurling it upward to the very world of Brahmā — that is the power born of the Tathāgata's father and mother. Young ones of stable lineage, the Tathāgata's causing the great broad stone to resound with the words 'All formations are impermanent, all phenomena are without self, and nirvāṇa is peace' — that is the power of the Tathāgata's miraculous ability. Young ones of stable lineage, the Tathāgata's reducing the great broad stone to finest dust with the breath of his mouth, and then gathering and assembling that dust, every particle without loss or deficiency, and fashioning it into exactly that same great broad stone and causing it to rest in a solitary place to one side — that is the power of the Tathāgata's meditation."

"Venerable one, what manner of power born of father and mother do the Tathāgatas, those arhats, those perfectly and fully awakened buddhas, possess?"
