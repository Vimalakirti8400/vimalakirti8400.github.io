The Secret Tantra of the Ritual Manual of Krodhavijaya (Krodhavijayakalpaguhyatantra)

The Secret Tantra of the Ritual Manual of Krodhavijaya, known in Sanskrit as the Krodhavijayakalpaguhyatantra and in Tibetan as Khro bo rnam par rgyal ba'i rtog pa gsang ba'i rgyud, is a tantric scripture centered on Yamāntaka, the wrathful form of the bodhisattva Mañjuśrī, here called Krodhavijaya, the Victorious Wrathful One. It opens with the terrifying appearance of this wrathful deity, before whom the other wrathful ones drop their weapons and the worldly gods bow down in fear and offer praise, and it goes on to teach his mantras, mudrās and ritual procedures. Much of the text consists of ritual instructions in verse: rites of protection, healing and pacification, rites for bringing rain or stopping hail, rites of subjugation and destruction directed at enemies, and the construction of the secret wheel diagram called the Hela wheel together with the twelve postures of the wrathful practitioner. Its chapters are presented as sections of a secret ritual manual belonging to the root rite of Mañjuśrī. It is Toh 604 in the Kangyur, where it belongs to the Tantra section, among the action-oriented ritual tantras associated with Mañjuśrī and Yamāntaka.

In Sanskrit: krodhabijayakalpaguhyatantra. In Tibetan: The Secret Tantra of the Ritual Manual of Krodhavijaya.

Homage to the Blessed One Mañjughoṣa.

Then the wrathful one of Mañjughoṣa,
this wrathful one named Yamāntaka,
turbulent with weapons and blazing tongues of flame,
fierce and endowed with fierce power and might:
when they saw him, all the other
wrathful ones let their weapons fall.
Likewise the worldly
gods bowed down with their bodies,
and moreover, joining their palms,
in terror they offered many praises.
All the many gods, however many there were,
as though tormented by the splendor
of Mañjuśrī's wrathful one, declared:
"Blessed One, we have the Buddha,
the Dharma and the Saṅgha as our refuge,
and we have seen your utterly unbearable, terrifying form,
pervading the directions with blazing splendor,
throwing the three realms into turmoil."
When the gods and the asuras,
terrified, had gone for refuge,
then the wrathful one of Mañjughoṣa
became peaceful once again.
When the great wrathful one
called Yamāntaka was at peace,
all the gods who had gone for refuge
then asked him:
"We who seek your supreme diligence,
how are we to please you?
In what way will you be gracious?
We have great doubt."
The wrathful one heard the words of the gods,
and Yamāntaka declared:
"All you gods, listen!
I arise before the three times;
I arose before all the buddhas.
I arise after the three times as well;
I arise in the present of the three times.
At the time of the last five hundred years,
I cause my teaching to arise.
When all the teachings have declined,
I cause my teaching to arise.
When evil deeds of immediate retribution are done,
then my teaching arises.
When people eat foul and heavy food,
then my teaching arises.
When they wear poor clothes and tattered rags,
then my teaching arises.
I am the body that arises in the three times;
when all the teachings have declined,
then my teaching arises.
In whatever way things become peaceful,
I am the body of all peace;
in whatever way they become wrathful,
all wrath is my body.
All of you of great splendor,
since you have gone for refuge to the Three,
keep my unbearable
wrathful one abiding in your minds.
Because the Three Jewels abide,
and because their command is upheld,
and out of loving compassion for all sentient beings,
my mind has become peaceful.
You who have gone for refuge,
endowed with the mind of awakening:
the supreme accomplisher of every aim,
these seventeen syllables,
my heart essence, arisen from my mind,
all you gods must hold!"
When Yamāntaka himself
had proclaimed the heart essence,
the earth then quaked,
the ocean too was violently churned,
and the yakṣas, the rākṣasas,
the piśācas and the rest were destroyed.
All the gods bowed down,
the hair on all their limbs standing on end,
and took up that wrathful heart essence
of the wrathful one Yamāntaka.
Then, to protect the sacred Dharma,
Yamāntaka taught them at length
this ritual manual
of Krodhavijaya.
Then the ritual manual of Krodhavijaya
was spoken by the wrathful one himself.
The gods endowed with compassion and the mind of awakening
heard it and rejoiced,
and to protect the Three Jewels
each explained a ritual of his own.
As for this root tantra
proclaimed by the wrathful one himself,
called Krodhavijaya:
the transmundane and the worldly ones
who had bowed to him before
heard it just as it came from the wrathful one's mouth;
and this tantra spoken by the wrathful one himself
the bodhisattvas,
with compassion grown vast in the same way,
blessed in many ways.

This was the first chapter, called "The Emergence of the Tantra from Krodhavijaya, the Root Ritual Manual of Mañjuśrī."

Then I shall now concisely explain
the supreme and marvelous ritual
of Krodhavijaya,
taught by Mañjuśrī's wrathful one.
Here, its maṇḍala,
which bestows all accomplishments
and throws the three worlds into turmoil,
is called Many Pretas.
In what place is this maṇḍala
accomplished in every respect?
The mantra practitioner, first of all,
should examine its site:
a solitary tree, or a charnel ground,
an empty house, a crossroads,
a stūpa, a place where two borders meet,
an empty thicket, an old road,
a pleasure garden, a mountain peak,
a riverbank, a forest,
or else the shore of a lake;
a shrine of the Great God (Śiva) is suitable as well,
or a cattle pen not trampled down.
Above all, in a clean place,
wherever the mind is also glad,
there one should make the maṇḍala.
Then the vidyādhara should diligently
purify the ground properly:
free of salt, uncracked,
level and free of thorns,
high toward the east and north,
its color rich and especially good.
Smear it with cow dung
mixed with the five products of the cow.
Having smeared it as is fitting,
the master, having bathed,
having kept a fast and settled in meditative equipoise,
should bless it with the wrathful one's protection.
At night, draw the supreme secret maṇḍala
called the Great Host of Pretas.
With a beautiful line of three twisted strands
made of linen or cotton cloth,
the wise one should lay out the lines of the maṇḍala,
with four corners and four doors:
first from the east and the north
to the south and the west.
That line, from the intermediate directions,
should then be drawn straight.
At the southeast corner
and at the northwest corner,
measure it well with the line,
and lay out the lines as a square.

Beautiful with a single tier,
it should be adorned with four doors.
The southern door is left open,
and the rest should be closed.
In the center, draw a lotus
proportioned to the maṇḍala.
The lotus and the doors of the four directions
should, without doubt, be made equal.
The ornament should be made half the door;
its sides are equal in measure to the petals.
The secondary ornament as well
should be made like the ornament.
Having laid out the lines of the maṇḍala
all around, according to the rite,
afterwards the vidyādhara, in the proper way,
draws with ash from the charnel ground.
Always within the corners,
the northeast and the rest,
the wise one draws the cudgel mudrā.
At the doors of the directions, place pretas,
and a fifth preta besides
should be visualized upon the lotus.
Likewise, inside the outer doors
and in all of those directions,
draw headless human corpses, pretas,
starkly, with ash from the charnel ground.
That is why it is the Great Host of Pretas.
Its size runs in steps from one cubit
up to eight cubits;
never draw it larger than that.
For one's own constant worship,
one of a single cubit should be drawn.
For pacifying and for increase,
and for rites concerning kings, two cubits are taught.
For driving away all beings,
and likewise for destroying, three cubits.
Four cubits is declared the best
for the acts of subjugation and summoning.
Five cubits is best for striving to eliminate
disease, pestilence among people, and enemies.
Six cubits surely turns back
all obstructors and grahas.
Seven cubits performs
the acts of subjugating and summoning the gods.
Eight cubits should be known as supreme
for all accomplishments and for fire offerings.
Having drawn it in whatever way is fitting,
one who is pure should then touch it.
For the water, a water vessel
should be filled to the brim with water.
Likewise, for the flowers,
another fine vessel should be filled well
with lovely, fragrant flowers and garlands of flowers,
and made beautiful.
For the scent,
take white and red sandalwood,
and likewise guggulu incense
mixed with aloeswood.
For lamps, butter lamps,
and the wise one uses a soft wick;
for fierce rites,
it is taught that human fat is used.
As torma, offer fish, meat and so on,
and especially mixed porridge and the like.
Having gathered the requisites there in this way,
the holder of the vidyā-mantra
bows to all the buddhas
and focuses on the mind of awakening.
If one is unable
to carry out the work of drawing,
then upon the lotus drawn earlier
in the center of the Great Host of Pretas,
the wise one should imagine
the preta with the mind.
Having made this visualization
with the supreme mantra of the preta,
the four cudgels of the corners as well
should be visualized with their own mantras.
If one is able to draw them,
the mantra itself should be placed upon them.
If one is unable to draw
the four pretas of the doors as well,
it is taught that they are likewise to be blessed
with the mantra that seizes pretas.
In order to bless them within
with their own mantra,
imagine legs, arms, heads and shoulders,
and likewise further shoulders,
and likewise garlands of hands,
skulls, skeletons, fresh human skins,
and garlands of corpses.
Within it, cause them to be blessed
with their own mantra.
Having visualized in this way according to the rite,
at the end, invite the wrathful Yamāntaka
by reciting his mantra,
and request him to be seated with his own heart mantra.
The wrathful one, turbulent and terrifying,
his body like blazing flames,
noble in splendor, is to be visualized,
his complexion radiant as if crushing all pride.
He has six faces, his hair is tawny,
his brows are tawny and his eyes are yellow;
he has three eyes, and his eyes are round;
his form has twelve arms.
He sits likewise upon a preta,
beautified by six legs.
A trident, a sword, a staff, an axe,
a hand outstretched in giving,
and the weapon of the hook:
these he holds likewise in his right hands.
Likewise the six on the left:
a skull cup, a short spear, a great arrow,
the forefinger raised in threat, a noose held,
and a club in hand; so he is to be visualized.
He is adorned with fresh and dried human heads
and with garlands of pretas,
and wears a garment of tiger skin.
His body is huge and his belly is huge;
his faces have many shapes,
his hair is like a turbulent ocean.
One face laughs, one face is peaceful,
one face glares terrifyingly with wide eyes,
one face has its mouth gaping open
and moves its tongue about,
and one face bares its fangs.
Thus, having invited him well with the heart mantra,
one should offer him the welcoming water.
Then, having offered the welcoming water according to the rite
with water mixed with scent,
place a red flower
on the crown of the preta as well.
Then the one of great intelligence
should offer red sandalwood to the wrathful one.
After the scent come the flowers;
offer a torma of sandalwood.
Flowers and garlands of flowers
should be offered with the flower mantra.
Having offered the flowers in due order,
according to the rite, with the flower mantra,
then, with his own heart mantra as well,
on the crown of the wrathful one
and at the heart of the chief wrathful one
the heart mantra should be set in place.
At his fontanelle place the fontanelle mantra,
and on his topknot the crest mantra;
on his body the armor,
and on the array of weapons the weapon mantra.
These, as here taught,
should be set in place with flowers.
Having worshiped in whatever way is fitting,
offer incense with its own mantra.
Then blazing lamps
should be set out in the eight directions as well.
Having offered the bhūta torma to the wrathful one,
outside the Great Host of Pretas
the wise one
should give the outer tormas.
Facing toward the chief wrathful one,
then once again offer incense.
Outside it, a vessel with water
filled with flowers is set down.
Having summoned the disciples,
who have bathed and fasted according to the rite,
one should kindle splendor in them with all the mantras,
and with the heart mantra of the wrathful one
give flowers into their two hands
and cover their faces with a cloth.
Having had them recollect the Three Jewels,
the master should lead them in.
With his own heart mantra, have them
cast the flower itself onto the maṇḍala.
Then the mantra practitioner shows them
the mudrā called the Cudgel.
The secret pledge of the Three Jewels
should be given to them.
Then, for the sake of accomplishment,
the mantra practitioner, having empowered them
with water and flowers, gives them
the secret mantra of the chief wrathful one.
Then, having empowered them according to the rite
with the heart mantra of the wrathful one,

with flowers, incense and garlands
the heart mantra should be given to them.
When they in turn have thus received
the supreme heart mantra of the wrathful one,
they should then bow to the guru
and honor him with gifts as best they can.
If the mantra practitioner honors the guru,
accomplishment will be gained; not otherwise.
Having given the disciples the heart mantra,
and having worshiped with garlands, sandalwood and so on,
after that, take up the vessel
placed outside earlier,
filled with scented water and the like,
and, having offered the welcoming water,
request the unbearable wrathful one of Mañjughoṣa,
fierce in his might, to depart.
Once he has departed, that offering water
should be offered to the maṇḍala as well.
In the maṇḍala on the vast ground,
the holder of vidyā-mantras
who desires unequaled accomplishment
should always perform the worship well in this way.
Then he will obtain accomplishment.

This concludes the second chapter, the presentation of the maṇḍala of the Great Host of Pretas, from the Detailed Root Ritual of Mañjuśrī, the Great Tantra of Krodhavijaya.

Next, further, I shall explain the mudrā
of Mañjughoṣa's wrathful one,
Ekavīra, the Sole Hero,
the great being of boundless splendor.
First, having displayed
the pledge of the Three Jewels,
one should bind the right hand
well and firmly, according to the rite.
The vidyādharas, always,
with all the fingers of the hand
hide the thumb well
and bind it tightly and well.
This is the cudgel mudrā, as the rite calls it.
With the heart mantra, or separately,
the mantra practitioner binds the mudrā with its mantra;
otherwise it is never to be bound.
oṃ namo buddha dharma saṃghebhyaḥ oṃ sarba tathāgate krodha bijāya agnir jvālita jihvā preta śira hūṃ phaṭ
That is the mudrā and mantra of the cudgel.
This mudrā, though only a single one,
makes the heart mantra of the wrathful one blaze;
chief of all weapons without exception,
it pacifies all the māras.
How it is drawn in the maṇḍala,
that rite I shall now explain.
The cudgel has the form of a staff;
this is what is known as the cudgel.
In particular, at its tip
the wise one draws the head of a preta.
Always draw it in the Many Pretas,
and never otherwise than that.
According to the rite of the vidyādhara,
this seal is drawn with ash from the charnel ground.
Or else, whenever one wishes to practice it,
this very seal is drawn on its own
in the maṇḍala belonging to the practitioner
who desires accomplishment.
Next, I shall explain here
the maṇḍala for accomplishing the mudrā.
As before, here too
draw the square Great Host of Pretas.
In its center, place a cudgel,
and likewise in the eight directions.
Since it has nine seals,
it is also called the Mudrā Maṇḍala.
Likewise, open the southern door
and then perform the worship.
All the mantras of the mudrā without exception
should be interspersed with the wrathful king's own mantra.
By this, worship is made here,
and in stages the secret worship comes to be performed.
oṃ namo buddha dharma saṃghebhyaḥ oṃ ā sarba tathāgata krodha bijaya agni jvālitā preta śira hūṃ phaṭ hūṃ
This is the mantra of the cudgel mudrā, with Krodhavijaya placed at its head. With it, all rites are to be accomplished.

Next, I shall explain the practice of the cudgel,
supreme among all accomplishments,
belonging to the chief wrathful one himself,
together with the secret worship.
Make a lotus of eight petals,
beautiful with its pericarp and stamens,
and at the petal tips, in the eight directions,
draw the cudgels, with the wrathful one set between them.
Having placed the ninth in the center,
the others, beginning from the east,
going around clockwise in due order,
should be set in place with flowers.
The seed syllable of all the cudgels, hūṃ,
should be placed on the crowns of the pretas.
At the beginning, furnish it with oṃ;
oṃ and namaḥ serve as worship.
This aḥ is declared to be the seed
and the pledge of Krodhavijaya.
oṃ āḥ hūṃ
oṃ namo buddha dharma saṃghebhyaḥ
It is praised by all the buddhas.
All of the cudgels, every one,
possess the urging of their own pledge;
all possess their own pledge,
arising from the family of the tathāgatas,
and are fully endowed with words of urging.
When they are examined with the wrathful gaze
and urged, each with its own sign,
they at once perform the action.
This āḥ is Krodhavijaya
looking with the wrathful gaze.
Endowed with its own pledge
and joined with hūṃ, it acts as the urging.
Pacifying, increase, subjugation and summoning,
sowing division and expelling:
these, by the application of the secret worship,
I shall explain in brief.
At its beginning is the syllable oṃ;
what follows it makes the mantra grow.
The pledge of the tathāgatas itself
is proclaimed to be the third.
The fourth, in order to bestow accomplishment,
is here the word that urges the mantra.
To say oṃ sarba tatāgata is the very essence of the mantra.
The hūṃ at the end
is said to make the wrathful one's mantra grow.
Beyond that, in particular,
one acts with one's own words of urging.
One who wishes to bring something under control
should name the thing itself:
having said, "Bring the lord of the gods under control!"
add svāhā at the end.
For pacifying, the urging itself
should be furnished with pauṣaṭ at the end.
One who desires the unequaled glory
born from the family of the tathāgatas
should always add namaḥ svāhā
at the end of the words of urging.
For increase, urge as before
and add pauṣaṭ.
For increase, also add the wrathful one's hūṃ
at the beginning and the end of the urging.
For killing, the blazing phaṭ
is the special addition here.
For destroying families,
phaṭ joined with the wrathful hūṃ in the middle.
Likewise, for expelling, place hūṃ
at the head and the end of the urging,
and place the truly blazing phaṭ as well.
This is known as the rite of the secret worship.
In that sequence of worship, thus,
the nine cudgels are completed.
By the application of the secret worship,
with incense, flowers and likewise scent,
and likewise tormas and lamps,
success comes even without recitation.
For subjugation, sowing division,
expelling, killing and the rest,
in the ways that have been taught
and by such applications as these,
there is no need for lunar dates,
for constellations, or even for fasting.
One who knows the rite of the secret worship,
having made the mudrā maṇḍala well,
whether bathed or not bathed,
may act freely, as he wishes.
Those who worship the heart mudrā
of Yamāntaka
with a torma of any kind at all,
or even with leaves, flowers and fruit,
if they possess the mind of awakening
and are deeply devoted, within six months
will find whatever they want and need,
and will perform whatever rites they wish.

This concludes the fourth chapter, the presentation of the secret worship of the cudgel mudrā maṇḍala, from the Detailed Root Ritual of Mañjuśrī, Krodhavijaya.

Next, accomplish all that has been taught
concerning the cudgel mudrā.
Its second maṇḍala is a different one,
called Preta in the Center.
The practitioner, at night in a charnel ground,
with ash from charnel grounds,
draws a square with four doors,
furnished with the lower halves of human corpses.
Adorn it with a canopy, yak-tail whisks,
mirrors, bells and garlands of flowers,
with divine substances, and many pretas starkly drawn,
and decorate it with eight skull cups.
Afterwards, draw the cudgel mudrā as well
upon a headless corpse.
In this maṇḍala that accomplishes everything,
called Preta in the Center,
perform the worship as taught
with the mudrā of the chief wrathful one.
Having given all the outer tormas,
being pure and having donned the armor,
and having first performed the approach
of a hundred thousand recitations, one should begin this.
Having filled an auspicious skull cup
with the blood of great beasts,
and likewise flesh that comes from a human,
offer these to the mudrā.
Blazing lamps
made from the fat of great beasts
should be offered outside it, in the eight directions,
to that which is guarded by mantra.
Having done this in such a way,
the one who knows the rite should then practice.
Glorious, wearing red clothing,
anointed with red sandalwood,
not doing anything else whatsoever,
he should do it only on the eighth day of the waning moon.
Together with three companions
who are brave, strong-minded and powerful,
the intelligent practitioner, of great fortitude,
with senses well restrained,
enters into the practice
in the maṇḍala of the preta.
Then at night, binding the mudrā,
he sits firmly and recites well.
Various obstructors
appear in a single night, and he must conquer them.
Having thus conquered
the great hosts of rākṣasas, he will accomplish the mudrā.
Then even rites that had not succeeded
he performs without hindrance.
Urged by Mañjughoṣa's wrathful one,
there is no doubt of it here.
Urging with hana bisphoṭa bañja bañja
and saying hūṃ at the end of the urging,
standing in the posture with the left leg extended,
in wrath he topples in an instant
inaccessible mountain peaks,
walls, upper stories, gatehouses,
parapets and towering houses.
If, urging with dhara bisphoṭa bañja,
one says hūṃ at the end of the urging,
mountains and many other things,
likewise the gates of the asuras,
likewise the earth and other things,
whatever one wishes, are split apart.
If, urging with paca bidhvanasaṃ śo,
one says hūṃ at the end,
by this mudrā the waters
are dried up without hindrance;
great lakes and rivers,
and even oceans, are dried up.
Say stambhaya twice,
at the end hūṃ and likewise mu,
and then at its end hūṃ;
if one adds sena stambha,

paca bidhvanasya śoṣaya śoṣaya da hūṃ phaṭ
like figures standing in a painting,
they will surely and swiftly be held down.

A king's realm or regions,
villages or cities,
by performing that rite of pacifying,
will be pacified without doubt.
Then I shall also properly explain
the rites of increasing.
Having made various offerings to a stūpa,
sit before it
and recite the great vetāla mudrā,
worshiped by the perfect buddhas.
If the stūpa contains relics,
it happens at once.
If one recites speaking the name of the target,
man or woman will prosper.
If, before a stūpa of the holy Dharma,
one recites ten thousand times according to the rite,
then, until there is increase,
recite step by step for the sake of increase.
The wise should make the offerings of the Tathāgatas
to all stūpas.
For one containing relics, according to the rite,
I shall explain in particular.
First, near the stūpa,
the wise should make a maṇḍala.
Then, there, one should spread
exceedingly pleasing flowers.
The wise should offer to it
eight brightly blazing lamps.
Having offered various food offerings
together with exceedingly pleasing water,
and having offered guggulu incense,
one endowed with the mind of awakening
bows with reverence
to all the Tathāgatas,
and, facing the stūpa,
should recite for pacifying and increasing.
One should also read the holy Dharma,
and from time to time make stūpas.
If one does this for half of each day and night,
there will be pacifying and increase.
The rite of increasing has been explained.

Further, I shall explain how,
toward the practitioner, the whole world
will certainly become delighted:
just as a mother cherishes her child,
so it is here, not otherwise.
The wise one should mix gorocana
without fail with his own blood.
Having mixed it many times over,
the wise one places it in a skull.
Then, when the moon is seized by Rāhu,
gazing with the eyes at the moon,
recite well until it is released.
In the morning, having made
a single dot on one's own forehead,
one then goes to the king's court.
This application, which brings beings under one's power,
has been explained in brief:
the moving and unmoving worlds
are delighted on seeing one.
This is the teaching on delighting
all the people of the world.
Then I shall explain in brief
the rite of summoning as well,
which the sole protector Mañjughoṣa
spoke through the mouth of the Wrathful One.
From the hair of a dead woman or girl,
on the eighth day of the waning fortnight,
diligently spin a noose well,
and then place it inside a skull.
Then, until the moon
comes to be seized by Rāhu, recite each day.
When the moon has been seized by Rāhu,
on a clean riverbank,
there draw the maṇḍala
of the Great Host of Pretas and make offerings.
As taught by the Lord of Wrathful Ones,
make a vast torma
to the vetāla accompanied by the Wrathful One,
and then to the outer bhūtas.
Then press down upon it with the hand,
and, until the noose that lies
within the human skull moves,
gaze at the moon and recite.
Then, if it springs upward,
the practice is said to be accomplished.
At that time, taking the noose,
in an instant the gods of the world
are drawn by it through one's mere thought,
and then the vidyādhara
does well whatever he wishes,
whether by thought in the mind or by word.
Even Śakra and the other gods
certainly act according to his command.
In an instant, from the sky,
endowed with great miraculous power,
drawn by that noose,
they present the supreme objects of desire.
Daughters of yakṣas, gandharva women,
or else daughters of the gods,
are drawn by that noose
into one's presence in an instant,
and then the supreme vidyādhara
does the deeds that please him.
If those who are lesser in practice
come to be tormented by desire,
then, with that very noose,
summon beings of divine nature
and resort to them without fear.
Even when tormented by desire, not women of the human kind:
afflicted by joy and sorrow,
brought low by sickness and by masters,
with urine, dust, and impurities,
human women are always to be shunned.
If one comes to be tormented by desire,
one should resort to divine women.
But if that great being
is at that time without attachment,
surrounded by virtuous lords of the gods
and by fierce yakṣas,
he dwells in his own fine celestial mansion
and goes wherever he wishes.
The gods present to him
fine immeasurable palaces,
many elixirs,
and the fruits of the wish-fulfilling tree as well.
Equal to the lords of the gods,
he obtains these without doubt.
He is certainly freed from hunger, thirst, and illness,
and is honored by all vidyādharas.
If the noose stirs or moves,
accomplishment such as this will be obtained.
If it stirs or moves, or otherwise,
but accomplishment has not come about,
then even with the unaccomplished noose
one should perform worldly rites.
Whether a man or a woman,
having thought in the mind of what one desires,
stretch out that noose at night
and recite there toward their direction.
The name of the target, together with the words,
should be joined at the end with the words.
To the place where the noose lies,
like a bird out of the sky,
they will come into one's presence in an instant,
without any doubt.
A ruler of men together with his household,
or else an ordinary person,
cattle, horses, and chariots,
any fruit or flower whatever,
and likewise desired goods as well,
whether seen or merely heard of:
when urged with the words of their name,
at that time it will summon them.
Those who dwell in other lands,
even those a hundred yojanas away,
if urged by the samaya,
are certainly summoned by that noose.
In this rite of the turbulent wrathful vetāla,
or of Krodhavijaya,
the rites accomplished by the noose,
and whatever is accomplished, unaccomplished or accomplished,
and both gods and asuras,
are taught in the rite through summoning.
This rite of the vetālī
has been explained properly and in sequence.

This is the supreme rite of the vetāla,
which grants great and supreme accomplishment
to vidyādharas,
explained properly and in brief.
Then I shall correctly and extensively explain
the rite of the Wrathful One,
detailed, wondrous, and supreme,
which came forth from the lotus mouth of the Wrathful One.
The mantra of seventeen syllables
that the Wrathful One himself spoke:
the very moment it is merely heard,
the gods are all bewildered.
Urged on by Mañjughoṣa,
the fixed number of recitations
of that blazing Wrathful One
I shall explain here in this detailed rite.
First, by reciting six hundred thousand times,
the preliminary approach will have been done.
Then, for the purpose of the rite,
one should afterward recite another six hundred thousand.
Likewise, if one recites one hundred thousand on a mountain peak,
one will succeed, not otherwise.
Afterward the wise may engage
in practice as they wish.
Then the sequence of the preliminary approach and so forth
of Krodhavijaya,
for the benefit of the practitioner,
I shall explain here in this rite.
The Wrathful One, solely heroic,
destroys all the māras,
bestows all accomplishments,
and destroys all harms.
First, each day one should perform
the bathing rite of the King of Wrathful Ones.
Then, impelled by the Wrathful One,
one should perform protection on one's own body.
In the mind, sit on a lotus seat
arisen from the seed bhaṃ.
Upon it, one should meditate on oneself
as the body transformed from the seed hūṃ,
and having meditated,
at one's own crown the syllable oṃ,
and at the two eyes the syllable āḥ:
one should protect oneself thus. Then, further,
reciting the mantra of seventeen syllables
and recollecting the Tathāgatas,
one should place at the heart the heart mantra
previously set upon one's own body.
Then, with the fontanelle of the Wrathful One,
one should touch one's own fontanelle.
The crown of the Lord of Wrathful Ones
should afterward be placed at one's crown as well.
Then, with the hands, one should also put armor
on the body according to the rite.
Then, upon one's two hands,
the intelligent one should set out the weapons.
Having bound, according to the rite,
the seal possessing one's own samaya,
the wise one should place at one's heart
the vetālī of the Lord of Wrathful Ones.
Likewise, having bound with the right hand
the club mudrā as well,
the abode of exceedingly fierce weapons,
one should enter upon the worship of the Wrathful One.
By performing the worship of the Wrathful One as explained,
he bestows all that one desires.
It is proclaimed that one should make mental offerings
to the maṇḍala of the Great Host of Pretas,
and also always to images,
and to drawings, as many as have been taught.
With fragrant divine flowers,
fine bilva leaves,
and scented water, make offerings on the ground,
or else upon images.
I shall explain the characteristics of images,
which accomplish the aims of every need.
Having done the preliminary approach according to the rite,
then have an image made.
It may be of gold or else of silver;
likewise it may be made of bell metal or copper,
brass, sapphire,
lapis, crystal, or precious gems.
One who desires kingship should make it in gold;
one who desires wealth should make it in silver;
to destroy illnesses, make it of bell metal;
copper bestows desires abundantly.
To destroy enemies, it is brass.
For the increase of one's own wealth,
make it from sapphire:
it bestows the supreme and all desires.
Lapis of various kinds
is proclaimed to bestow riches.
If made from stainless crystal,
it bestows the supreme pacifying and increasing.
One made of precious gems bestows the sword
and the accomplishment of the gods of the underworld.
In order to accomplish everything,
I shall now explain the measures.
If one makes one of gold about eight finger-widths
and worships it, one obtains kingship and splendor too.
One who always desires wealth
should make one of silver ten finger-widths in measure.
Likewise bell metal, which dispels illness,
should be made sixteen finger-widths in measure.
At twenty finger-widths in measure,
make it of copper and worship it.
Brass, too, as with copper,
is twenty-two finger-widths in measure.
One made of sapphire
should also be made sixteen finger-widths in measure.
Of lapis, eight finger-widths is auspicious;
of crystal, twelve finger-widths;
of precious gems, it should be eight finger-widths.
Thus one who desires accomplishment
should worship well the images, as described,
which show all that one desires.
With limbs unimpaired, clear and fine,
it should always be made by a skilled artisan,
endowed with the characteristics given before,
richly adorned with fine weapons:
riding on a preta, with six faces,
three eyes gazing in a terrifying manner,
radiant, with twelve fine arms,
and clearly endowed with six legs,
with garlands of human heads and pretas,
holding the se ral kha as his means,
he is seated in the posture with the left leg extended,
delighting in wearing a tiger skin as his garment.
Such an image,
complete in the manner given before,
this Wrathful One as described,
the mantra practitioner should have an artisan make.
Or else, from the heartwood of a tree,
one should make images of the Lord of Wrathful Ones
according to the characteristics described,
and always worship them.
Then I shall properly explain
images made of wood.
Pure and bestowing all desires,
it too should be fashioned in the same way.
If one desires to be supreme among all vidyādharas,
the mantra practitioner should have made
an image of sandalwood,
twenty finger-widths in measure.
From the wood of red sandalwood,
sixteen finger-widths in measure,
have a fine image made,
which bestows all that one desires.
One made from the root of rāja-arka
bestows the aims one desires;
fourteen finger-widths in measure is auspicious,
and all bliss and abundance arise from it.
From the heartwood of aśoka,
made well by a skilled artisan,
a fresh one five finger-widths in measure is taught;
this is explained for subjugating.
Likewise, if one is made from tāla wood
six finger-widths in size,
it summons the lords of the gods and asuras,
and likewise the nāgas.
From nāgakesara wood,
likewise make one seven finger-widths in measure;
it is taught for destroying illnesses
and for bewildering enemies.
One made from a human skull
is always auspicious at twelve finger-widths.
Without delay it bestows the supreme,
and it abundantly bestows the accomplishments one desires.
Those made from a human skull,
even without performing the preliminary approach and so forth,
merely by worshiping them,
accomplish the aims one needs.
This is the extensive explanation
of the characteristics of fine images.

Just as the Wrathful One has taught,
I shall explain the characteristics of the painting.
On a fine burial cloth taken from a corpse and washed,
measuring one cubit,
the wise one should paint well
with a brush made of that very corpse's hair.
Without any colored pigments at all,
one should paint with the blood of a hare.
Or else his image
may be painted with red ochre, as before.
Wrathful and blazing, terrifying to behold,
he stands with the left leg outstretched and the other bent.
He has six faces, his hair bristling upward,
his eyebrows yellow and his eyes yellow too;
he has three round eyes,
and a body endowed with twelve arms.
Likewise he is set upon a hungry ghost,
and is made splendid with six legs.
One right hand grants the supreme boon,
and the others hold a trident, a sword, an axe,
a club, and a hook.
A wrathful left hand makes the threatening gesture,
and the hands hold a spear and a javelin;
a left hand holds a staff,
and the hands hold a skull cup and a noose.
Thus should Yamāntaka be painted,
wearing a tiger skin as his garment,
his face overpowering others with terror;
the wise one should paint the image
with his fangs bared.
Then, on both sides,
the figures of the mudrās are to be painted.
On the right, a male in wrathful form,
his mouth gaping, terrifying to behold,
his hair bristling upward, deep yellow:
the club mudrā, holding a weapon,
standing with the right leg outstretched and the other bent,
threatening, his face creased in a wrathful frown,
his face and eyes in a wrathful manner.
On the left, paint a female vetāla,
with blazing mouth and great splendor,
three-eyed, her face creased in a wrathful frown,
her most terrifying mouth gaping wide,
her hair bristling upward, her belly huge,
fierce, clothed in tattered rags,
likewise holding a trident in her hand,
of great splendor, fiercely threatening,
her body ablaze with flames,
her face and eyes in a wrathful manner.
Receiving the command of the Sugata's son,
this painting of the secret mudrās
should be placed in a secluded spot.
One who knows the rite should recite
the club mudrā before this image.
Or else, if one recites
the vetāla mudrā, one gains the accomplishment of the mudrā.
Having set it in a secluded place free of bustle,
one should worship it every day.
Or else, always spreading out this painting
in the maṇḍala of the mudrās,
one who desires accomplishment, in meditative equipoise,
should make offerings and recite.
I have properly explained the painting
for accomplishing the mudrās.
Next I shall explain
another painting of Yamāntaka.
After the preliminary approach has been done,
on a fine burial cloth from a corpse, washed,
made one cubit in measure,
with good pigments containing no glue,
the Wrathful One, the solitary hero,
dark like a black cloud,
should be painted as before.
After the preliminary approach has been done,
and the painting made as is fitting,
having placed it somewhere utterly secluded,
then, following the rite of worship,
having worshiped it constantly, every day,

after that one should begin the recitation.
Next, further, the rite of recitation
is explained just as it has been taught.
Having spread out the painting and made offerings,
and having worshiped all the tathāgatas,
seated on a cushion of kuśa grass,
in meditative equipoise and keeping silent,
gazing at the painting alone,
with the head bent like a peacock's,
with a rosary of rudrākṣa beads,

one should recite with a one-pointed mind;
when weary, one should leave off the recitation.
Having recited thus three hundred thousand times,
after the approach has been completed,
one should then take up residence on a mountaintop
and, living on air, perform recitation.
One who delights in and desires the supreme accomplishment
should then practice its observance.
If one does not wish for the threefold rite,

the middling and lesser accomplishments,

will be obtained;
but through that observance even the supreme is accomplished.
Further, here

Not straying from the road and keeping silent,
eating alms food, with the senses restrained,
carrying a fine, hard staff
of khadira wood, a fathom long,
taking on the guise of Yamāntaka,
one travels by paths that are hard to tread.
The practitioner who properly practices
the observance of the wrathful hero
in the form of a hero for six months
then attains even the supreme accomplishment.
Having practiced the glorious observance,
and then having entered upon the practice of accomplishment,
without doubt the sublime itself
is thus swiftly accomplished.
This is the taking up of the formidable observance.
In the practice, those of the lineage of Māra,
in the forms of goddesses of desire,

with forms of many different kinds,
torment the one whose senses are restrained.
One should not speak with them,

one sees terrible forms,

with one's thought fixed on the Sugata,
one should not be frightened by them.
If they oppress one and act with hostility,
those who break the command of the Sugata
the Wrathful One himself will utterly destroy.
Meditating with one's thought on the Sugata,
this observance is to be undertaken
by a courageous hero;
it is to be done by a hero, and not otherwise.
One who desires the supreme accomplishments
should earnestly undertake
this observance of the deity that has been taught,
in just the manner it has been taught.

A club of kovidāra wood,

Next, further, I shall explain
the practice of the supreme hook.
A hook made of urmo iron
should be four finger-widths;
its handle, straight,

On the twenty-ninth day of the month,
at night, having made offerings and tormas,

Having made fire offerings of incense
with ghee from a tawny cow,

Then, carrying it, without doubt,
when dawn breaks,
light shines out everywhere like fire,
and he rises upward, blazing.
The worldly gods
come under his power like servants,
and heed his commands completely,
like the supreme command of the Tathāgata.
If smoke comes forth

The mantra practitioner lives thirty million years,
is worshiped by yakṣas and vidyādharas,

If, through the might and power of the Wrathful One's mantra,
it becomes hot,
then the next morning, having performed auspicious rites,

if one recites, one will without doubt

Yakṣas and vidyādharas,
and likewise mahoragas and kinnaras,
daughters of the asuras,
and likewise daughters of the gandharvas,
the classes of pretas and vetālas,

Gold-making elixir and rejuvenating essences,
substances of accomplishment,

all of these, the hosts of gods,

All of them, without exception,

Adorned with every wonder,

fully endowed with every wonder,
houses and the quarters of queens,

With miraculous powers, fine qualities, and riches
they delight the practitioner.

by his command, without fail,
the yakṣiṇīs, each of them,
attend him with the pleasures he desires.
If, through faults in the rite,
it has not even become hot,
then, holding that same hook,
and sitting in solitude the next morning,
inserting the name of whichever girl one wishes,
and reciting for as long as one likes,
even one dwelling eight hundred leagues away
will then swiftly come.
Whether a girl or else a boy,
wherever they may be, it is swiftly accomplished.
Flowers and fruits are likewise accomplished,
and chariots and trees.
Even if it is not accomplished, they are always
certainly summoned by it.
Thus the practice of this sublime hook
has been fully explained.
Whatever accomplishment there is of the skull cup,
that too I shall explain here.
The trident, the sword, the club,
and likewise the axe and the hook,
whose practices have been explained,
are to be held in the right hand.
The others, the skull cup and the rest,
if held and practiced with the left hand,
then, just as has been explained,
one will certainly attain accomplishment.
A skull cup well made of wood,
twenty finger-widths in size,
evenly and very well made,
and entirely free of all defects:
sitting on the summit of a mountain,
one performs the sequence of offerings and tormas,
and, having made fire offerings of incense,
and with the mantra generated protection and power,
one holds the skull cup in the left hand,
and, placing the right hand on it,
one covers its mouth.
Until a blaze,
smoke, or heat arises in it,
for that long one should recite, keeping it covered.
If it blazes, one will travel through the sky,
and is declared lord of the three worlds.
If smoke arises,
then likewise one certainly becomes invisible.
If instead it becomes hot,
then whatever one thinks of at that time
will come forth from that skull cup.
About this there is no need for deliberation.
But if it is not accomplished,
then, without setting it on the ground,
one takes up that skull cup
and places it on a wooden stand.
Having placed it there, covered with cloth,
thinking of whatever one desires,
food, things to eat, and delicacies,
whatever they may be,
holding the cloth covering with the hand,
if one recites one thousand and eight times,
then, once it is accomplished, every day
food of any kind whatsoever,
together with delicacies, will certainly
fill that skull cup.
One should give this nectar food
to any sentient beings at all.
After doing this properly,
one should keep clean and eat in the proper way.
For this practice of the skull cup that has been explained,
it is to be held in the left hand.
A sharp, well-made short spear
should be practiced without delay.

An excellent one made of urmo iron,
auspicious, thirty-two finger-widths long:
then, following the earlier rite,
on the clean bank of a river,
one should practice at night, as before.
Its accomplishment is taught to have three results.
Thus, just as has been explained,
the wise one also practices the great arrow:
an excellent one made of urmo iron,
very sharp, as before.
Having made the fire offering of incense,
one should smear it with red sandalwood.
The practitioner, according to the rite,
sitting on the summit of a mountain,
practices at night, as before.
If it is accomplished, the results are as before.
Covering the index finger of the left hand
with the right hand,
and having performed the rite as before,
when the moon is seized by Rāhu,
sitting well on a single seat,
if the intelligent one recites,
then from that finger,
without doubt, there will come a blaze.
Or smoke may arise of itself,
or else it may become hot.
If then it certainly blazes,
one will attain the rank of a vidyādhara.
From the arising of smoke,
likewise, one is said to become invisible.
If at that time it becomes intensely hot,
one fully attains swiftness of foot.
If the finger of the left hand
has not been accomplished,
then, though the accomplishment has failed,
it will certainly perform various rites.
If, in wrath, one threatens with it,
that person, just as one wishes,
will fall down on the instant.
And one slain by the mantra practitioner,
who has thereby died,
he then, with the right hand,
raises up again for as long as he wishes.
Even a great and raging army
he can then threaten with it.
Elephants and horses,
trees and forests,
he destroys before long.
The summits of all the mountains,
however fine and lofty they may be,
with that finger of his
he topples before long.
The supreme accomplishment of the finger
that grants all accomplishments has been explained.
Next, further, the practice of the noose
I shall also explain here.
From the hair of a dead person
one should make a noose, thick and pleasing.
If one practices it according to the earlier rite,
one obtains those same accomplishments of it.
From very fine wood
one should make a staff sixteen finger-widths long,
well carved and well painted,
fine and pleasing.
Its ends as well
should be bound with the finest gold,
and its tip should be adorned
with a jewel, a ruby.
Then, properly, on the summit of a mountain,
in accordance with the rite explained before,
one should perform the fire offering, sitting facing it.
The practitioner practices at night.
Taking up that blazing staff,
one rises into the sky without doubt,
becomes, as before, the chief
of the accomplished ones, taking forms at will.
Through the smoke, the heat, and the rest,
one obtains through it the results as before.
The practice of the supreme weapons
has thus been explained in summary.
Next, the other accomplishments
that come from the rites are to be explained,
just as the Supreme Wrathful One
himself explained them in summary.
Having first drawn the maṇḍala
called Many Pretas,
and having obtained an unwounded corpse,
the wise one washes it and adorns it well.
Having worshiped with flowers and incense,
one gives a torma together with meat.
The rite is to be done in charnel grounds;
it is never to be done anywhere else.
On a bed strewn with flowers,
one lays it with its head toward the western door.
Taking a fine skull cup
filled to the brim with excellent food,
one should also pour human fat
into that excellent food.
At that time, the wise one,
if with a ladle he pours into the man's mouth
a full one hundred oblations
empowered with the Wrathful One's mantra,
then its tongue will come forth.
Then it is to be cut with a blade.
From the tongue cut at its root,
through the power of the Wrathful One, swiftly,
that very thing turns into the form
of thirty-two fine swords.
Then, taking up the swords,
one gives one to each of one's companions.
When the practitioner takes one up,
he is accomplished on the instant.
He becomes a king honored
in every way by all those vidyādharas,
and together with those companions
he will live for seven eons.
Or else, the wise one, in a charnel ground,
having then drawn the maṇḍala
called Many Pretas,
should make the offerings as they have been taught.
Having obtained the unwounded corpse
of a man or woman who has died,
washed it well, and adorned it with ornaments,
one places it at the door of that maṇḍala,
and, sitting on its heart,
one should recite one thousand and eight times.
The mantra of the Supreme Wrathful One,
of seventeen syllables, one thousand and eight times:
having recited it, one attains accomplishment.
The corpse then rises up.
One should offer it water for the guest well.
If one then at once touches
its head with the left hand,
realgar, like the color of fire,
will then swiftly come forth
from the mouth of that being.
The practitioner takes it,
and, having smeared all his limbs with it,
becomes the color of refined gold.
He gives it likewise to his companions,
and at once all of them
become the color of refined gold.
They take whatever forms they wish,
and they become the foremost
of all the vidyādharas.
When all the substances have been accomplished,
then, once they are accomplished,
one should offer one portion to the Three Jewels.
Then the wise one takes his own share,
and, having given to all his companions,
flies from here into the sky.
When a single substance has been accomplished,
yet another rite is to be practiced:
if a single substance has been accomplished,
first having made prostrations,
one should fully offer oneself
to the Three Jewels according to the rite.
After that, taking the substance,
and having given that substance
to the companions in due order, one will fly.
Afterward one practices these rites.
If one does not do it in this way, then even if it is accomplished,
that substance will be stolen.
Therefore, for the supreme accomplishments,
always act just as has been explained.
Next, further, the practice of eye ointment (añjana)
and of edibles, just as it is,
in order to accomplish every need:
this sublime practice I shall explain.
Having kept a fast for three days,
one should draw the maṇḍala in a charnel ground.
Then, following the earlier rite,
one should perform the torma rite there.

Then one should protect oneself
with the ashes of a cremated corpse.
Having smeared oneself with them,
that joyful one, with excellent companions,
having smeared himself with them,
should place the essence of the eye ointment
on the head of one executed on a stake.
Having covered it with a dead person's cloth,
one holds it in the hand and recites;
until the three kinds of accomplishment appear,
for that long the intelligent one recites.
Heat or else smoke arises,
or else it blazes like fire.
If one anoints one's eyes with it,
then through the blaze one will travel through the sky.
Through the smoke, the body, it is said,
without doubt becomes invisible.
If it becomes hot, then the beings
one looks at will be killed,
and one will revive them again, fully,
before long and without doubt.
Even if one has not accomplished this,
if one anoints one's eyes well with it,
the beings one looks at come under one's power;
they kneel down and bow their heads.
Following the rite of the eye ointment,
the wise one should practice with bezoar.
Its accomplishments are taught likewise;
if it is not accomplished, beings come under one's power.
Red lead, orpiment,
and realgar likewise
the wise should practice
following the procedure for the eye ointment.
The dead person's cloth is likewise practiced according to the rite,
and the other practices in the same way.
The mantra spoken by the Wrathful One himself
I shall fully explain in summary.
One should draw the maṇḍala in a charnel ground,
and make offerings and tormas.
Having put on that dead person's cloth,
the one who knows the rite recites at night.
Then, if together with that cloth
one comes to blaze brightly at night,
blazing and surrounded by blazing light,
one soars well into the sky.
One gives forth the sound of loud laughter
that terrifies the world.
Always endowed with whatever form one desires,
one moves about like the Wrathful One.
Likewise, if smoke arises,
without doubt one becomes supreme
in invisibility, it is fully taught.
If that practitioner's cloth
likewise becomes hot,
one becomes swift of foot and of great strength,
travels everywhere across the earth,
and lives a hundred years without illness.
Even if one has not been accomplished,
all beings without exception come under one's power;
whomever one sees, likewise,
those one sees become like servants.
If a man wishes to accomplish
these yakṣiṇīs,
then, at the foot of a delightful banyan tree,
having drawn the maṇḍala,
with hair unbound and without any clothing,
having filled a fine alms bowl completely
with excellent, well-cooked food,
one practices at night during the waning fortnight.
Then, taking out seven handfuls,
and having charged them seven times with the mantra,
one says, "Fair Goddess, accept this!"
and throws them behind one's back.
Then, taking another seven handfuls,
charged with the mantra, one should eat them.
The one called the Fair Goddess,
accomplished, sovereign of all the yakṣas,
surrounded on all sides by a great retinue,
then appears, her light blazing.
Then, having rinsed one's mouth,
one should offer her water for the guest
together with incense and fine flowers,
and also offer flowers of welcome.
Then, if one offers her a fine and delightful seat
furnished with flowers,
she says to him, "Now I am accomplished for you;
I will do as you command, and in just that way,
never otherwise, I will obey your command.
Give me your command!"
She becomes a mother, or else a sister,
a beloved, or else a wife.
If one addresses her as mother, then without fail
she gives him realgar and the like,
many substances of accomplishment,
without doubt.
As a sister, she gives flowers and fruits,
supreme nectar and gold-making elixir
to that practitioner always,
without doubt.
When one says, "Become my beloved!",
at night she shows him excellent pavilions,
and other pleasing things as well,
and shows herself delighted.
Swiftly she brings lovely women,
leading them from a hundred leagues away.
Moreover, to that practitioner
she, being gentle, gives what he desires and delights.
If he addresses her as wife,
taking whatever form he desires, utterly beautiful,
with every power and every enjoyment
she delights that accomplished one.
Being gentle, she gives what he desires and delights;
smiling, respectful, granting every wish,
she provides every power and every enjoyment,
which the practitioner enjoys for as long as he wishes.
If one wishes to accomplish
the womb-born piśācīs,
then that rite of theirs for the practitioner
I shall explain here.
At the foot of a śleṣmātaka tree
one should draw the maṇḍala in a clean place.
Having made a great offering,
one should perform the torma rite as before.
There, the mantra practitioner,
having kindled a fire with wood from a charnel ground,
naked, with his hair disheveled,
then, into that blazing fire,
if with the mantra he burns
one thousand and eight fire offerings of fragments,
then, at that time, without doubt,
the piśācī with a blazing face
will come. At that time,
having made one's mind courageous,
and unafraid, one offers her water for the guest before her.
After that, to him
she shows herself in a beautiful form.
Her name is Kaṅraṅgi:
one should make fire offerings with the mantra of her own name.
Whatever wealth one thinks of,
she comes and, before long,
without doubt she offers it fully.
The one called Kaṅraṅgi
is supreme among all piśācīs.
For a practitioner who is courageous
and of very great splendor,
all grahas are fully accomplished.
If it is not so, then swiftly
she kills even the practitioner.
If the glorious vidyādhara
wishes to accomplish
the daughters of the nāgas,
this is taught as their rite.

Having gone to the shore of a lake of white lotuses,
following the former rite,
one should draw there
the maṇḍala called Many Pretas.
Then there the wise one,
performing the torma rite as before,
first kindles the fire well
with kindling sticks of udumbara wood,
and the wise one burns powdered nāgakesara
one thousand and eight times.
When the fire offering is performed, swiftly
the daughters of the nāgas
come, afraid and terrified.
If one burns it at night,
whatever the practitioner wishes
they give fully, without doubt.

Next, the mantrin
goes as he pleases to the mouth of an asura cave;
all the asura women
are accomplished by all these rites.
Having drawn the maṇḍala as before,
perform the torma rite extensively.
Having kindled a fire in the hearth
with priyaṅgu wood,
recite the mantra according to the rite.
White mustard seed steeped in saffron:
recite over it one thousand and eight times,
and then one must burn it.
Then, while the fire offering is performed,
the young asura women appear.
Whatever the practitioner wishes
they give fully, without doubt.
Into the abodes of the asuras
they lead him by a pleasant path.
Endowed with every sovereign power,
there he attains accomplishment.

If here the mantrin
wishes to sell the great flesh,
first, staying in a charnel ground,
he should take the great flesh with a courageous mind.
Having taken it and made protection,
he holds a knife in his left hand
and grasps flawless great flesh in his right.
Entering the charnel ground at night,
with a courageous mind, reciting in his mind
the Wrathful One Yamāntaka,
he cries out the call of a seller of flesh.
Hearing the words of that great being,
rākṣasas without commitment
and some lesser beings
become terrified.
Others, who have superior minds,
the guardians of the fierce charnel ground,
of various forms and great power,
show their bodies.
The adept should not be afraid;
then, with a fierce mind,
he should say, "Welcome!"
Then, to accomplish the guardians,
the practitioner should say:
"In exchange for the flesh, give me
añjana and whatever substances I desire."
The practitioner says nothing else.
Knowing that his mind is courageous,
they give to him, and not to others.

Furthermore, the rites of this one
for suppressing armies,
as taught by the Supreme Wrathful One,
I shall explain here.
Make a very fine banner
made from a corpse's cloth;
then it should be made deep blue,
and made sixteen finger-widths in size.
To a slender bamboo staff
made one cubit in length
fasten it, then raise it,
and practice at night in the charnel ground.
Having drawn a vast maṇḍala,
squatting there,
holding the banner in hand,
recite all through the night.
Then, when dawn breaks,
with that held in hand,
at that time the clashing enemy army
will without doubt come to a standstill.
And other armed men too,
whom the practitioner sees,
all of them, the moment they are seen,
are suppressed and become as immovable as a mountain.
Enemy armies with maddened royal elephants,
with chariots, horses, and elephants,
and many battling foot soldiers,
by merely being seen
will certainly appear as if suppressed.

Furthermore, the auspicious and wholesome
charnel-ground application
taught by the Supreme Wrathful One
I shall now definitely explain.
Having watched at night the blazing fire
where a corpse is being burned,
with hair unbound and without clothing,
he should perform recitation while circling it.
Then there the mantrin
takes with his left hand
a single handful of ashes,
and the wise one goes home,
or else goes to an empty temple;
he must never look back.
Then there, over those ashes,
he should recite until dawn.
When dawn has come,
guarding those ashes,
he should place them in a new vessel,
and must never set them on the ground.
If, in order to suppress enemy armies,
one scatters those ashes,
then in a land with chariots and elephants,
all weapons in battle,
and many people,
by merely scattering those ashes once everywhere,
their bodies will become immobile, without doubt.
This is the wholesome rite that suppresses weapons
and also suppresses armies.
Likewise the applications for putting to sleep
and for intoxicating:
one may go to the windward side,
or else perform it through the approach practice; either will do.
When a formidable enemy is clashing in arms,
having done this, their advance becomes motionless.
Thus the suppression of armies
through charnel-ground applications has been explained.

I shall explain other kinds
of applications for bringing beings under control.
Draw a maṇḍala in a charnel ground
and make offerings as before.
Having made an extensive torma,
having made a lamp burn
with oil of human fat,
then on a fine skull cup
the wise should collect lampblack.
Having collected the lampblack of the smoke,
one should keep practicing until dawn.
Then, when morning dawns,
if one anoints the eyes with the lampblack of the smoke,
whoever one sees comes under one's power.
Thus the application for bringing beings under control
has been proclaimed.

Now I shall explain the accomplishment
of the wholesome red ochre application.
Having placed a length of cloth dyed with fine red ochre
in the mouth of a corpse,
then, when it is half burned,
one should take it at night, without companions.
Having taken it, in an empty temple
the wise should perform recitation.
Obstructors of various kinds arise.
Until dawn breaks,
remain there and recite over it.
Then, when dawn has come,
wrap it in red cotton cloth,
worship it, guard it, and put it away.
Then, with that ochre cloth,
one should write the name of anyone at all,
whatever one wishes.
Urging them by the practitioner's name,
without doubt
the one to be accomplished swiftly comes.
What was taught on accomplishment with red ochre
has thus been explained in brief.

Now, as it is in the vidyā,
the accomplishment of the ring together with its application,
as taught according to the rite,
I shall explain fully in brief.
When a human corpse is being carried off,
go there, and in its mouth
place a ring of gold.
The wise one remains there,
reciting the mantra of the Wrathful One alone.
When the corpse has been burned by the fire,
the ring that lay in the corpse's mouth
should be taken with the left hand.
One should recite until dawn.
Then the wise should place
that fine ring
on a finger of the left hand.
Whoever and whatever
his left hand touches
all become his servants,
and follow behind him as well.
One should never have doubts about this.

Furthermore, the most wondrous
application for accomplishing white mustard seed,
just as the Wrathful One himself taught it,
I shall explain.
From sita wood make a fine casket
eight finger-widths in size.
Fill it with white mustard seed;
pour the supreme accomplishment into it,
and until the moon is seized by Rāhu,
perform recitation every single day.
Having drawn the maṇḍala according to the rite,
make extensive tormas as well.
Until all the mustard seeds in the casket
make a sound or split open,
for that long recite over the mustard seeds.
Then, taking the mustard seeds that have become accomplished,
and applying them according to the rite
to anything whatsoever,
there is nothing they cannot do.
With them one splits the earth
and dries up rivers;
many kinds of high mountains
one crushes into powder.
Likewise mountain peaks,
if they are applied, one topples completely.
Rains of blood and charcoal
one brings down, without doubt.
Thunderbolts and hail
one makes fall suddenly.
When the vidyādhara applies them
to earthquakes and to the gathering of clouds,
suddenly too there is no obstruction.
Shaking lakes and oceans,
bending trees right over,
and shaking even beneath the earth;
likewise threatening the nāgas,
great lakes and ponds,
and subduing the nāgas;
and other rites one may wish to perform,
of various kinds,
whether taught or not taught:
the wise, examining their nature with intelligence,
merely by scattering accomplished mustard seeds,
will accomplish them without obstruction.
The practice of the mustard seeds
that grant all accomplishments is complete.

One who practices the hero's discipline
should accomplish these and various others,
various ones as before,
and the intermediate ones and so on.
In this rite of the Supreme Wrathful One,
for all the rites that have been explained,
if they are done
without the discipline, there is no result.
As for the rites of the two mudrās
that have been explained here,
if one is unable to keep the discipline,
then by making offerings to this Supreme Wrathful One
as before,
and by reciting as already explained,
one obtains the rites as they were taught
and the accomplishments as they were taught.
One who always keeps the Sugata in mind,
even if he cannot always keep the discipline,
by applying himself to recitation
day by day, obtains the aims he desires.
Thus accomplishment through the discipline,
and the various practices in this rite,
whatever the Supreme Wrathful One taught,
have been explained without omission.

This concludes the eighth chapter, the detailed root rite of the Wrathful One Yamāntaka, set out in sequence, from the Krodhavijaya, the detailed root rite of Mañjuśrī.

Then, in the later rite,
how it may be done even without the discipline:
for that reason I shall state that rite
as the Wrathful One himself taught it.
The mantra of seventeen syllables
is what is called "the Wrathful One."
For it, first, as shown,
draw a painting, and before it
make offerings in the proper way.
Or else the wise one
may have a statue made; after that,
the wise should recite eight hundred thousand times.
At the end of that,
make a great offering to the Three Jewels.
After that, to the Supreme Wrathful One
make offerings just as was taught.
Then it is accomplished, and at that time,
bhūta grahas and poisons,
and whatever one desires in mind:
he is able to do the rites he intends.
It should never
be applied to trivial accomplishments.
Poison and the rest, whatever is spoken of,
these rites are done by words.
Even with words spoken while distracted elsewhere,
it is just as he himself wishes.
Likewise, toward hostile beings,
he is able to subdue them or to benefit them.
For one seized by a black one,
he can also perform the rites:
by sprinkling water upon him,
or with a whip or with the foot,
just as the Wrathful One taught,
he will recover without doubt.
Thus in all cases one should urge it on
by mind or else by word.
Even without being wrathful,
minor rites are accomplished;
by mere recollection alone
the doer's rite is accomplished.

Then one should properly perform
this other rite, of bringing down into a vessel.
A youth endowed with auspicious marks,
free of faults and defects, with complete limbs,
is thoroughly washed and adorned with ornaments,
and is also given the five products of the cow to drink.
After that he keeps a fast,
and at dawn he is washed,
anointed with fragrant scents,
and adorned with clothes and ornaments.
Having honored him in this way,
facing toward the east,
seat him, according to the rite,
on a seat of kuśa grass; then
into both his hands
give sandalwood, torma, and flowers.
Offer incense of black aloeswood.
Then one swiftly recites the mantra,
and by this rite of practice
one performs the rite of bringing down into a vessel.

When it has descended into the vessel,
one should ask through him about good and bad.
Then whatever has happened, what will happen,
and what is happening, he tells truly.
"Great being, do this!" he says,
and turns one back, saying, "Not this."
Vidyā-mantras that are deficient or excessive,
and mantras too, he here makes complete.
Then to that Supreme Wrathful One
present a water offering with flowers.
Then, by the teaching of the Sugata,
the commitments should also be shown.
When the vessel has heard them, then,
being released, he falls to the ground.
The vidyā, the Wrathful One Yamāntaka,
having been accomplished, then departs.
Then take up the vessel;
afterward wash him thoroughly,
give him food and let him eat,
honor him, and send him back.
Having performed the bringing down into a vessel
with the rites as they were taught,
then perform the rites
taught in the rite of the later rite.

Then, if the mantrin
wishes to bring a king under control,
at that time, in front of the painting,
draw his form with charnel-ground ash,
sit upon its heart,
and with one-pointed mind recite ten thousand times:
"May so-and-so come under my power."
Adding his name, as before,
at the end of the wrathful mantra as taught,
say svāhā at the close.
This is the application always taught
for bringing under control through the Wrathful One.
Then the king, come under control,
forsaking his dear ones,
with his own person and his wealth
will surely serve well, like a servant.
Likewise, if one does ten thousand recitations,
brahmins too come under control;
vaiśyas and śūdras, all without exception,
surely do so through six thousand.
For women and for girls
one should recite ten thousand times.

Then, if one wishes to separate
two people who are friends with each other,
draw an effigy of one of them
in the same way, place one foot
on its heart, and squat.
Adding his name at the end,
recite the Wrathful One Yamāntaka:
"May so-and-so swiftly
be separated from this one, phaṭ!"
For this separation, thus performed,
one should recite ten thousand times.

Then, knowing that a being is very vicious
and is to be tamed properly,
toward one who harms the Three Jewels
one should act with the Wrathful One, and not otherwise.
Uttering the enemy's name,
draw his effigy with charnel-ground ash.
Then recite one hundred and eight times
over a peg of khadira wood,
drive it into the effigy's heart,
and after that recite ten thousand times.
Then, putting the name at the end
of the mantra of the Wrathful One Yamāntaka:
"Kill so-and-so!",
ending it with hūṃ and phaṭ,
if one recites in this way
a full ten thousand times,
by that rite he will die;
there is no need to examine this.
One should always keep the Sugata in mind,
and never act with a malicious mind,
except to stir suffering in his heart
and so purify him.
By the rite of pacifying,
one should then make it peaceful and wholesome.
The sorcery, just as it has been taught,
should never be practiced as such.
Out of love for beings,
it is taught to be done with the Sugata in mind.
If one who desires accomplishment wishes to obtain
the unobstructed accomplishments
just as the Tathāgata taught them,
one should not have such a wrathful mind.
One who lacks the intention of the Sugata
will then lose the accomplishments.
In short, for one without the awakening mind,
the supreme accomplishments taught
by Mañjuśrī, the Lord of Wrathful Ones,
with discipline and recitation,
according to the rite in the kalpa,
are lost.
Whoever has the intention of the Sugata,
even without good discipline,
and likewise even without images and the like,
obtains the supreme accomplishments,
and, free of afflictions, as he wishes,
finds all that he desires.
Whoever has the intention of the Sugata,
with a mind unconfused and steady,
confronts the afflictions of existence, so hard to cross;
having paid homage
to all the tathāgatas,
one with the intention of the Sugata obtains the supreme accomplishment.
Knowing this, at that time
one should teach the supreme path of the Sugata,
which destroys affliction and suffering.
One should never serve worldly gods.
Thus the rites for taming vicious beings,
as they were taught,
are for the protection of the Three Jewels;
they should never be applied anywhere else.

Furthermore, the rites of expulsion,
for one who wishes to perform them

Likewise, with charnel-ground ash
the wise one draws the enemy's effigy.

before Mañjuśrī the Wrathful One,

and saying hūṃ again, if one recites ten thousand times,
he will be expelled without doubt.
Or else, with that same ash
draw the enemy's effigy,
and trample on its heart with the left foot,
without hesitation;

if one recites Yamāntaka,
in an instant that enemy
is struck down by a burning fever.
Then, by the rite of pacifying,
one should oneself pacify it.
If one does not do so, within seven days
he will die without doubt.
Rites such as these
one who keeps the Sugata in mind never performs.
For all beings
one should perform pacifying and enriching.
One whose very nature is the awakening mind
will before long attain accomplishment.

Then, for the happiness
of all beings, the rites of pacifying
and likewise the rites of enriching,

At stūpas or in a maṇḍala

prostrate again and again.
Whatever virtue one has gathered
by day and also by night,
relying on the Three Jewels,
do it with the intention of the Sugata:
"By this, may all beings,
who suffer so greatly, become happy!"
Whatever accomplishment one obtains by it,
even small and subtle ones,
the wise should always
use for the benefit of beings.
The wise should build stūpas,
and likewise read the sublime Dharma.
Other small virtues too,
however minor they may be,
one who wishes to obtain the supreme accomplishment in this very life
should always dedicate to beings.
Thus this awakening mind
becomes the field for the sake of the Dharma,
and the characteristics of attending upon bodhisattvas
are also fully taught.
Thus, having made one's understanding steady,
one should increase virtue;
and having increased virtue,
dedicate it wholly to beings.

The mantrin who keeps the Sugata in mind,

benefiting beings,
increases pacifying and enriching.

make various offerings;
after that, the Supreme Wrathful One,
Yamāntaka, should be worshiped
with the finest white flowers.

For anyone at all,
recite in this way a thousand times.
For lands and regions
and for kings, double it.
For horses and elephants
and for cattle, recite a thousand times.
For goats and sheep and for buffalo,
it is taught to recite always a thousand times.
Or else, having performed recitation
for all embodied beings,
one who possesses the awakening mind
will certainly become supreme in pacifying.

With a thousand, one is greatly enriched.
If one wishes to perform these enrichings
that greatly delight the heart,
the name of whoever it may be
should be added at the end according to the rite.
The wise one, adding at its end

obtain an inexhaustible treasure.

By this rite the practitioner

Likewise, by burning all kinds of food,

this, likewise, is explained as Terrifying.
The gaze is fixed upon the club,

that is explained as Binding All Around.
One threatens with the forefinger of the left hand;
with the arm raised, as with the left,
the eyes are fixed on the single finger,

this is called Agitating All Around.
Spreading the left leg,

brandishing the club with outstretched arm,
with a single threat one deludes.
The one called the Swift posture

From that same one, opening the mouth,

constantly meditating on the Wrathful One.

the glorious one lowers the left,
threatens with a single finger of the left,

with the feet, the face is turned:
constantly meditating on the Wrathful One,
this is called Kīlikīla.
Great Kīlikīla
is that same one, looking backward,
the Wrathful One closing one eye,
and swiftly assuming the stance of worship.
This is the application of the postures,
and how, in brief, they are set out
from the sequence of the spokes of the Hela wheel:
this is the good explanation.
By the power of the mantra of the Hela wheel
and by the power and delight of the right posture,
rites that are utterly marvelous,
the rites just as they were taught, are to be done.
If one knows those accomplishments, the secret tantra,
and this Hela wheel,
one becomes one with the Wrathful One in reality,
and so will act in the language of the Wrathful One.
This ritual manual, together with its secrets,
is explained as the supreme secret essence.
Than this ritual manual of the King of Wrathful Ones
there is nothing else more supreme.
Having heard, from Krodhavijaya,
this latest of the later ritual manuals,
which is called the Utterly Secret,
and meditating on the secret meaning,
all those gods rejoiced,

Belonging to the secret rite, the fifth: the chapter of rejoicing in the Blessed One Mañjuśrī Yamāntaka, the twenty-second.

The secret tantra, the King of Ritual Manuals of the Wrathful One Trailokyavijaya, is complete.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The maṇḍala is named first ཡི་དགས་མང་པོ ("Many Pretas") and then ཡི་དགས་ཕལ་ཆེན ("the Great Host of Pretas"); both are translated literally, as no Sanskrit form can be confirmed. In the list of sites, the line བ་ལང་ལྷས་ས་མ་གཡུལ་གྱིས is obscure; "a cattle pen not trampled down" is a tentative reading.  "Cudgel" renders བེ་ཅོན, the club-like implement drawn in the corners and also the name of a mudrā. The adverb དངར་ཏེ describing how the corpses are drawn is obscure; "starkly" is a tentative rendering. མི་རྒྱལ་སྒྲུབ་པ is taken as rites concerning kings (mi'i rgyal po), which is uncertain. སྨེ་གཏོར is an unclear term, tentatively rendered "outer tormas" from its placement outside the maṇḍala. མཚོག་མ is taken as the fontanelle, one of the body places where parts of the mantra are installed alongside heart, crown, crest, armor and weapon.  "Cudgel" renders བེ་ཅོན, a club or staff, used both for the implement drawn in the maṇḍala and for the mudrā. སྨེ་གཏོར is rendered "outer tormas," following the previous passage, where these tormas are given outside the maṇḍala; the term is uncertain. The line ཡི་དགས་མང་པོ་རྟག་ཏུ་བྲི could also mean "always draw many pretas"; it is taken here as naming the Many Pretas maṇḍala, as in the torma mantra's label just before. དཔའ་བོ་གཅིག་པུ is rendered Ekavīra, matching ekabīra in the mantras.  The line བེ་ཅོན་ཁྲོ་བོའི་བར་བཅུག་བྲི is unclear; it is taken as the cudgels drawn with the wrathful one set between them. ཕྱུགས་ཆེན, "great beasts," may be cattle, or may echo Sanskrit mahāpaśu, a term that in some tantric contexts denotes a human victim; it is rendered literally. The adverb དངར, applied to the many pretas drawn in the maṇḍala, is obscure and is tentatively rendered "starkly," as earlier. སྨེ་གཏོར is rendered "outer tormas," as in the preceding passages.  Many of the short urging formulas here are given piecemeal within the verse; each Sanskrit element is transcribed as written. ཅེས་བདག་གིས་བྱ after the ha ha hā hā baitāli hūṃ phaṭ formula is taken as "so one should do it oneself"; the phrase is terse. འགྲོ་བདག, "the lords of beings," stands in a line one syllable short and may be defective. "The great flesh" (ཤ་ཆེན, Skt. mahāmāṃsa) conventionally denotes human flesh. The "Ha Ha" maṇḍala (ཧ་ཧར་གྱུར་པ, literally "become 'ha ha'") may allude to laughter (cf. Aṭṭahāsa), but the name is kept as written. ཡི་དགས་མང་པོ་སྦྱར་བ could also mean simply "the arrangement of many pretas."  སྨེ་བ (in "incense, all of it impure") is taken in its sense of ritual impurity; this may also bear on the term སྨེ་གཏོར rendered "outer tormas" in earlier passages. ཚིལ་ཆེན, "great fat," conventionally means human fat. The line རོ་ལངས་ཕྱག་རྒྱ་སྒྲུབ་པ་བྲི reads བྲི ("draw"), which does not fit; since the next line announces "a third practice," the evident reading is གཉིས ("two"), and that is translated. The mantra's hanamtha and bundha are transcribed as written.  The line listing "subjugating and compelling" reads literally "subjugating and doing/making"; the second term is taken as making others act, but its exact technical sense is uncertain. "Without pennants" renders 'phan med pa literally; the sense of this qualifier of the handle is unclear. In "warmth, smoke, and blazing" the text reads dud 'thul, taken as an evident slip for dud pa, "smoke," in light of the following verses. The line rendered "with the splendor of a great being" (sems can chen po de yi gzis) is obscure. Ganaka (ga na ka) and śarapuṅkhā (sha ra bu ka) are tentative identifications of ingredient names given in transliteration; mahānala and mohanī are likewise reconstructed from the transliterated forms. "Great fat" is the usual tantric code for human fat.  "The top should be closed up well" renders rtse mo rab tu sbyar bar bya, whose exact sense is uncertain. The "leaves of a tree tossed by a whirlwind, those that float, together with what remains" is an uncertain rendering of an obscure ingredient line. In "whoever is touched by this powder," the text reads rig pa, taken as an evident slip for reg pa, "touched." The line "take earth from his footprint" is a syllable short in the Tibetan, and "earth" is supplied from context. Kanaka is probably datura; mālatī is jasmine; ariṣṭa is the soapnut tree. The text frames these destructive rites as permissible only against those who harm the Three Jewels and only for one with the mind of awakening, which it states explicitly.  The plant names barahe, ricira, patra, keśinī, sukari, and balakata are given in transliteration because they cannot be identified with confidence; the line describing them as found "where perfect awakening abides" is literal and obscure. "Houses of bell metal" renders khang pa li ba, whose sense is uncertain. "The very essence of that vetāla" is taken to mean its heart mantra.  "Brought low by sickness and by masters" renders nad dang gtso bos sma phab pa; the sense of gtso bo here is uncertain. "Without attachment" renders brtse ldan ma yin, literally "not affectionate." The closing summary lines on the noose rites are elliptical in the Tibetan and are rendered closely.  "For the purpose of the rite" renders bu don cho ga, literally "the rite for the sake of a son"; the sense is uncertain. "Fontanelle" takes mtshog ma in that sense. The implement called se ral kha could not be identified with confidence and is left in Tibetan form. "A fresh one" reads sar pa as gsar pa.  "Pigments containing no glue" reads སྦྱིན་མེད as སྤྱིན་མེད (without glue), the evident sense; as written སྦྱིན would mean "giving." The "threefold rite" (ཆོ་ག་ལས་གསུམ) is not further specified in this piece; it probably refers to the three stages just described (approach recitation, mountaintop recitation, and the observance). The final line is a volume marker of the Kangyur edition rather than part of the tantra.  "Urmo iron" (ཨུར་མོའི་ལྕགས) is left as a name since the exact kind of iron or steel meant is uncertain. "Incense of śata wood" reads ཤ་ཏ་ཤིང་གི་བདག་པོ as a slip for བདུག་པ (incense); as written བདག་པོ ("lord") makes no sense here, and the identity of śata wood is itself uncertain. "Reciting eight times" (བརྒྱད་བརྗོད) translates the text as written; a reading "a hundred" is possible but not attested here.  "Eight hundred leagues" reads དཔག་ཚད་བརྒྱད་བརྒྱན as བརྒྱད་བརྒྱ, the evident sense; as written བརྒྱན ("adorned") does not fit. "Surrounded by their own mounts" takes གཞོན་པ as a spelling of བཞོན་པ (mount, vehicle); the line is obscure. In "undefeated" ཕམ་པ has its ordinary sense of defeat in battle, not the monastic downfall.  "Intensely hot" reads ཚར་གྱུར as ཚ་བར་གྱུར, the evident sense. "Human fat" renders ཚིལ་ཆེན (literally "great fat," Skt. mahāvasā), the standard tantric term for human fat. "The great arrow" renders མདའ་བོ་ཆེ, probably the iron nārāca arrow.  "During the waning fortnight" reads the obscure མར་གྱི་ང་ཡི as མར་ངོའི, the evident sense in this ritual context; this is uncertain. "The Fair Goddess" translates ལྷ་ཡི་བཟང་མོ, a yakṣiṇī whose Sanskrit name could not be identified with confidence; in the quoted address the text has བཟང་པོ, taken as the same name. "Fragments" renders གཟེགས་མ, whose precise substance here is unclear. Kaṅraṅgi is given as the Tibetan transliterates the name (ཀང་རང་གི); its Sanskrit form is uncertain. "One executed on a stake" renders གསལ་ཤིང་གིས་བསད, a person killed by impalement.  "The great flesh" (mahāmāṃsa) is the tantric term for human flesh. In "they give to him, and not to others," the Tibetan reads byad kyis, taken as a slip for byed kyi. In the red ochre rite, dge bdag ni is read as dge ba ni ("wholesome"), and 'dri bar bya ("ask") is read as 'bri bar bya ("write"), since the rite has the target's name written with the ochre cloth. The sense of the closing phrase kha ru is unclear and has been left out of the rendering.  "Sita wood" renders si ta'i shing, a wood not identified with confidence. "Earthquakes and the gathering of clouds" reads the bdag of sa g.yo bdag sprin as a slip for dang. "One seized by a black one" most likely means a victim of a black snake, in keeping with the reference to poison just before. "Bringing down into a vessel" is the rite of making a deity descend into a medium, here a youth, for divination.  "So-and-so" renders the Tibetan placeholder for the target's name, to be replaced by the actual name in the mantra. The short line zhi bdag gi cho ga yi is read as zhi ba'i cho ga yi ("by the rite of pacifying"). "Very vicious" reads rab 'dug pa as rab gdug pa. "Except to stir suffering in his heart and so purify him" is a tentative rendering of a terse line.  "Lac-tree wood" renders rgya skyegs shing, the tree that yields lac, probably the palāśa. In the passage on the finest food, the Tibetan repeats the line "from him one obtains the supreme of desires and wealth as well," which has been kept as written.  "Paints that contain no glue" reads sbyin med as spyin med. In "When he is wrathful, the earth splits," the line is a syllable short and "the earth" is supplied from the parallel passage earlier in the text. The line on the tiger-skin garment (man bas par dgyes) is obscure; it is rendered by its evident sense.  "Then on a fine patch of ground" reads the evident དེ་ཚེ་ས་གཞི for the written དེ་ཚེས་གཞི. The phrase ར་པ་བཀྲུས་པ describing the cloth is obscure; "washed along its border" is a tentative rendering. The single syllable of Yamāntaka referred to throughout is hūṃ, as the preceding piece states.  "Mantra jewel" renders སྔགས་ཀྱི་ནོར་བུ literally; in context it most likely denotes the recitation rosary or its bead. The line འདི་དག་སྦྱིན་ནི་བསྒྲུབ་བསྟན་ཡིན is terse and its rendering "these are taught as the practices that grant them" is tentative. In the closing verses of chapter thirteen, དེ་ཡི་དབང་པོ could also be read as "his lords" rather than "his senses."  In the series of syllables added after hūṃ, ཚིག is transcribed as a syllable to fit the pattern, though it could also be read as the ordinary word "word," giving "the word hūṃ." The substances listed as ba bla and btsag are taken as orpiment and red ochre. In "even cloth will swiftly split," the word རས (cloth) is kept as written although the parallel lines suggest a hard material.  In the list of the effects of the six maṇḍalas, the text reads རི་མོ་མང་པོ ("Many Images") where the list of names has མི་རོ་མང ("Many Corpses"); the transposed syllables are taken as a slip and rendered "Many Corpses." The ornament སེ་རལ་ཁ is rendered as an ornament worn across the body; its exact form is uncertain. The title of the rite, དོན་གྲུབ, is rendered "the Accomplishment of Aims."  "Placing his mind upon the meaning" reads the written བུ་དོན as the evident དེ་དོན or a similar phrase; the written form makes no clear sense. "The lesser ones he obtains with a hundred" reads རྒྱ as བརྒྱ ("a hundred"). The couplet beginning "even what is held fast by the dark one" is obscure, and its rendering is tentative.  "Grateful" renders གཟོ་བ as written; since an artisan (བཟོ་བོ) is paid later in the rite, it may be a slip for བཟོ་བ, "an artisan."
