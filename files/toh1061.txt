The Great Sūtra Called the Sūtra in Accord with Those Who Roam and Those Who Do Not Roam (Āṭānāṭīyasūtra nāma Mahāsūtra)

The Great Sūtra Called the Sūtra in Accord with Those Who Roam and Those Who Do Not Roam, known in Sanskrit as the Āṭānāṭīya Sūtra, is a canonical discourse set at Śrāvastī, in the Jeta Grove, the park of Anāthapiṇḍada. It opens as the Great King Vaiśravaṇa, guardian of the north, arrives with a vast retinue of yakṣas whose radiance lights up the whole grove, and comes before the Buddha. The text belongs to the tradition of protective scriptures, in which powerful spirits pledge to guard the Buddha's followers, and it offers safeguards against harmful beings for those who recite it. It is Toh 1061 in the Kangyur, where it is counted among the great sūtras, a class of texts valued for their protective power and closely associated with the collection of dhāraṇīs.

In the language of India: āṭvanāṭīyasūtraṃ nāma mahāsūtra
In the language of Tibet: The Great Sūtra Called the Sūtra in Accord with Those Who Roam and Those Who Do Not Roam.

Homage to the Three Jewels.

Thus did I hear at one time. The Blessed One was dwelling at Śrāvastī, in the Jeta Grove, the park of Anāthapiṇḍada. Then the Great King Vaiśravaṇa, of surpassing complexion, came to where the Blessed One was. With him came his retinue of many yakṣas, many hundreds of yakṣas, many thousands of yakṣas, and many hundreds of thousands of yakṣas. He bowed his head at the Blessed One's feet and sat down to one side. At that time, through the power of the Great King Vaiśravaṇa's radiance, the whole of the Jeta Grove was lit up with a great light. Seated to one side, the Great King Vaiśravaṇa then offered these verses:

Perfect Buddha, supreme among those with two feet,
O wise one, what you know
the gods do not know.
Great hero, I pay homage to you.
To all the buddhas who have passed,
who are present now, and who have not yet come,
I pay homage,
and in you too I take refuge.

"Venerable One, there are monks, nuns, laymen and laywomen, śrāvakas of the Blessed One, who dwell in lodgings in the wilderness, at the edges of forests, and in remote borderlands. There too dwell yakṣas and malevolent nonhumans who are native to those places. Some of them have faith in the Blessed One's teaching, and some have no faith in it. Those yakṣas and malevolent native nonhumans who have faith in the Blessed One's teaching are very few, while those yakṣas and malevolent native nonhumans who have no faith in the Blessed One's teaching are far more numerous. Venerable One, so that the Blessed One's śrāvakas, the monks, nuns, laymen and laywomen, may in this way guard and protect themselves and dwell in ease, and so that the yakṣas and malevolent native nonhumans who lack faith may come to have faith, and those who have faith may grow in it still further, it would be excellent, excellent, if this very Sūtra in Accord with Those Who Roam and Those Who Do Not Roam, this vidyā-mantra and protection, through which homage comes to be paid with hands and feet, were taken up in full and thoroughly mastered."

The Blessed One consented to the Great King Vaiśravaṇa by remaining silent. Then, in the presence of the Blessed One, the great king Vaiśravaṇa recited at length this very Āṭānāṭīya Sūtra, the vidyā-mantra and protection that brings beings to bow down at hands and feet. He then bowed his head at the feet of the Blessed One and vanished from that very spot.

When that night had passed, the Blessed One sat on the seat prepared at the head of the Saṅgha of monks. Once seated, the Blessed One addressed the monks:

"Monks, last night, when night had fallen, the great king Vaiśravaṇa, of surpassing complexion, came to where I was, attended by a retinue of many yakṣas, by a retinue of many hundreds of yakṣas, by a retinue of many thousands of yakṣas, by a retinue of many hundreds of thousands of yakṣas. Having arrived, he bowed his head at my feet and sat to one side. At that time, through the power of the great king Vaiśravaṇa's radiance, the whole of the Jeta Grove was lit up with a great light. Seated to one side, the great king Vaiśravaṇa then offered these verses:

'Perfect Buddha, highest of two-footed beings,
Wise one, what you know
The gods do not know.
Great hero, I pay homage to you.

To all the buddhas of the past,
Those who have arisen now, and those yet to come,
I pay homage,
And to you as well I go for refuge.'

"Then he said: 'Venerable Lord, there are śrāvakas of the Blessed One, monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen, who dwell in remote lodgings, in forest groves, and in outlying places. And there are yakṣas and fierce non-humans who dwell there permanently, some of whom have faith in the teaching of the Blessed One and some of whom do not. Of the yakṣas and fierce non-humans dwelling there permanently, those who have faith in the teaching of the Blessed One are very few, while those yakṣas and fierce non-humans dwelling there permanently who have no faith in the teaching of the Blessed One are far more numerous. Therefore, so that the Blessed One's śrāvakas, the monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen, may thus guard and protect themselves and dwell in ease, so that the yakṣas and fierce non-humans dwelling there permanently who have no faith may come to have faith, and so that those who have faith may grow still more in it, it would be excellent, excellent, if the Blessed One would take up and master extensively this very Āṭānāṭīya Sūtra, the vidyā-mantra and protection that brings beings to bow down at hands and feet.'

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The phrase rendered "through which homage comes to be paid with hands and feet" is uncertain. The Tibetan "sug pa rkang pa" is obscure, and "sug pa" has been taken as "hand." The Tibetan title's "those who roam and those who do not roam" reflects a Tibetan interpretation of the Sanskrit title Āṭānāṭīya. In the arrival scene the Tibetan has a stray "rnam thos pa dang" ("and with Vaiśravaṇa") after the list of the retinue; it is probably a scribal duplication and has not been translated. "Jeta Grove" renders the Tibetan "Prince Jeta's Grove." The lines "Pay homage…" render "phyag 'tshol cig," which may also be read as questions: "Do you pay homage…?" Compare the Pali "vandatha."  The names of the cities, the beings living beyond Uśira and the assembly halls are translated by meaning. This includes Ever-Moving, Sheathed, Golden, Clothed, Body-Abode, Verandaed and Bull-Possessing, as well as the king called Hero. The Tibetan forms cannot be matched with confidence to particular Sanskrit names, though they correspond loosely to the city list of the Pali Āṭānāṭiya Sutta. "Ever-Moving" and "Not Ever-Moving" echo the Tibetan rendering of the sūtra's own title (Āṭānāṭīya), so they may represent Āṭānāṭa. "Hero" may stand for Sanskrit Vīra.

In the city list, the closing "bdag lags so" is read as "dag lags so" ("these are"). "Lotus-Filled," with whom Kubera sports, probably refers to his lotus pool (compare the Pali naḷinī). The phrase "na li ke ra" is taken as nālikera, coconut, which suggests cooking in coconut shells, but this is uncertain. "Chunlak" is an unidentified bird name, left in phonetic form.  Many of the gandharva and kumbhāṇḍa names in the two lists cannot be confidently identified with Sanskrit originals. They are translated by meaning, for example "Intoxicated with Desire," "Joy-Maker," "Five-Holder," "Cuckoo-Voice," "Gold-Goer," "Golden One," "Great Flank," "Left Side," "Accomplisher of Existence," "Concealer of Deeds," and "Possessors of Fine Blankets." The division of some lines into separate names is also uncertain, for instance "Left Side, the Black One and Red One." The Tibetan "gser spyod bu mo gser can" could also mean "Golden One, the daughter of Gold-Goer." Pa ba sangs and Phur bu are the Tibetan names of the planets Venus and Jupiter, and are given as Śukra and Bṛhaspati. Byin and Nye byin are given as Datta and Upadatta. The phrase rendered "makes them bow down hand and foot" is literal, and its precise sense is uncertain. The royal palace "lcang lo can" is Alakāvatī, Vaiśravaṇa's city.  Many of the nāga and yakṣa names could not be identified with confidence and are translated by meaning. Among them are Fine Jambu, Indra's Hand, Broad-Sided, Bright-Sided, Bright-Bowing Victor, Star Joy, Evil Throat, Sure Neck, Level Mountain, Golden One, Long Iron Knot, Five Words, Glad Mind, Three Fruits and Three Necks.

Some of them correspond to names in the Pāli Āṭānāṭiya list. For example, Level Mountain corresponds to Sātāgira and Golden One to Hemavata, but the Sanskrit forms are not secure enough to use.

The Tibetan "ma lan" is taken as a corruption of "ma ldan," Mātali, who also appears in the Pāli list. "Gnod sbyin pa skyong," rendered "the Yakṣa Guardian," may hide a name such as Pāla or Gopāla.

The phrase "sug pa rkang pa la phyag 'tshal bar 'gyur ba" is obscure. Sug pa is taken here in its archaic sense of "hand," giving "bowing at hands and feet."

Pūtana renders lus srul po, a class of foul-bodied spirits. Alakāvatī renders lcang lo can, the capital of Vaiśravaṇa; the preceding piece's "Aḍakavatī" should be harmonized with this form.  The sūtra's Tibetan title literally means "the sūtra concordant with those who roam about and those who do not." It is a rendering of the Sanskrit title Āṭānāṭīya, and is given here in that Sanskrit form.

The phrase rendered "that brings beings to bow down at hands and feet" literally reads "becoming homage to hands and feet." It recurs as part of the sūtra's own description of itself. Its precise sense is unclear; it may be an epithet of the protective spell that causes hostile spirits to submit.

The doubled "Pay homage" lines use an imperative form. This follows the parallel Pāli refrain, in which the spirits urge one another to revere Gautama.  Most of the city, hall, and lake names in this section are given in Tibetan translation and cannot be matched confidently to their Indian originals; they have been translated literally: Roaming Everywhere, Not Roaming Everywhere, Sheathed, New Golden, Clothed, Lotus-Bearing, Earthen, Bodily Abode, Spacious Abode, Balconied, Bull-Bearing, and the king called Hero. The phrase "Roaming Everywhere and Not Roaming Everywhere" echoes the Tibetan rendering of the sūtra's own title (Āṭānāṭīya). The Tibetan "sar pa" before "Golden" is read as "new" (gsar pa). "Balconied" may correspond to the Pāli Bhagalavatī. "Coconut" renders the transliterated "nālikera"; the Pāli parallel at this point has a different word, tuṇḍikīra.
