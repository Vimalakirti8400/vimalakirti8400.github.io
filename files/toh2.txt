The Sūtra of Individual Liberation (Prātimokṣa Sūtra)

The Prātimokṣa Sūtra, known in Tibetan as the Sosor Tharpa'i Do (སོ་སོར་ཐར་པའི་མདོ་), or in English as the Sūtra of Individual Liberation, is one of the most foundational texts of Buddhist monastic life, setting out the rules of conduct and training that govern the lives of fully ordained monks. It belongs to the Vinaya, the broad category of canonical literature concerned with monastic discipline, and takes the form of a sūtra whose verses and injunctions are traditionally recited at communal confession ceremonies held twice monthly within the monastic community. Opening with a homage to the Buddha as the Omniscient One who discovered the treasury of the Three Jewels and crossed the ocean of suffering, the text frames its detailed disciplinary code within a devotional and doctrinal vision of the Buddha's incomparable wisdom and compassion. This text is Toh 2 in the Kangyur, where it is classified among the Vinaya scriptures.

In the language of India: Prātimokṣa Sūtra. In the Tibetan language: The Sūtra of Individual Liberation. The first section.

Homage to the Omniscient One.

The banner of his renown rings out through all three worlds.
The sound of the sublime Dharma he proclaimed, a lion's roar.
The Omniscient One who found the treasury of the Three Jewels —
at whose feet Brahmā and Indra press the jewel atop their crowns.

He who crossed the fathomless and boundless ocean of suffering —
with bowed head I pay homage to the Sovereign of all beings,
and, that the training-ground of omniscience, a vessel of the Three Jewels,
may be set forth in the midst of the noble assembly.

The Vinaya (monastic discipline) of the Buddha is like a vast ocean,
fathomless and boundless, dwelling-place of all.
Its heart and very essence
is this individual liberation.

This is the supreme guide of all phenomena
under the King of the sublime Dharma.
This is the great trading-house of precepts,
the caravan-assembly of monks.

This is the medicine that removes the harm
done to those seized by moral corruption.
This is the iron hook that curbs
the two-footed youth gone astray in the follies of youth.

This is the means of crossing over
to escape from saṃsāra, deeper than any ocean,
and it is the assured ferry-bridge
for beings journeying to the good destination.

This is the path that defeats the afflictions,
the supreme guide under the King.
This is the staircase and foundation
for those entering the citadel of liberation.

"When I have passed into nirvāṇa,
let this be your teacher" —
thus did the Self-Arisen One, with reverence,
earnestly praise it to the gathered assembly of monks.

The very word "buddha" is exceedingly rare in the world.
Human existence is supremely hard to obtain.
Going forth into renunciation is supremely rare.
Likewise, among those who have gone forth,
perfect excellence in moral discipline is supremely rare.
And even with fully purified moral discipline,
a good spiritual friend is supremely hard to find.

In a world where a buddha has appeared,
where a human birth and the going-forth are attained,
where excellence in moral discipline is achieved,
and where a good friend, though rare, has been found —
those who are wise, who wish to benefit themselves
and who desire to bring these things to fruition,
those striving in their vows should diligently apply themselves
to hearing the individual liberation code.

A monk who is tamed in the faculty of self-mastery,
who has gone forth in the manner of a buddha that does not waver —
all those who genuinely wish for liberation
should always guard the individual liberation.

Even over ten million aeons, hearing of the individual liberation,
grasping it, retaining it, and keeping it
is hard to find,
and practising it is even harder to find.

The arising of buddhas is a blessing.
The teaching of the Dharma is a blessing.
The harmony of the Saṅgha is a blessing.
The austerity of those in harmony is a blessing.
Meeting with noble ones is a blessing.
Keeping the company of the virtuous is a blessing.
If the childish are not encountered,
one will always abide in happiness.

It is a blessing to see one endowed with moral discipline.
It is a blessing to see one of wide learning.
It is a blessing to see an arhat, fully free from renewed existence.
It is a blessing to enter the pleasant ford of a gentle river.
It is a blessing to be a person habituated to the Dharma.
It is a blessing to have attained wisdom.
It is a blessing to have exhausted pride and conceit.

Those who are resolved and have tamed their faculties,
who have grown old in tranquil hermitages —
those of wide learning dwelling in forests,
whose youthful passions have subsided — their abiding is a blessing.

Venerable ones, as much of life has passed, so much less remains. Venerable ones, since old age and death are drawing near and the teaching of the Teacher will also decline, you venerable ones should practise with vigilance and diligence. The awakening of the Tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly complete buddhas, and all similar wholesome qualities — the aspects of awakening in harmony with such awakening — are likewise to be attained through vigilance.

The śrāvaka Saṅgha of the Blessed One has few concerns and few duties, so what is the first business the Saṅgha should perform? Venerable ones, ask those who have not yet arrived about their intention and their purity.

And having asked, one should then speak.

To that lion of the Śākyas,
joining the palms of ten fingers,
the Prātimokṣa was recited
for the sake of taming — hear it from me.

Having heard, one should accomplish
just as the great sage has taught,
and with respect to minor infractions,
make yourself one who takes them seriously.

The mind-horse, hard to rein,
that strains always and bolts away —
for that, this Prātimokṣa
is a fine, sharp, hundredfold bridle.

Those great ones who, by word alone,
turn back and do not cross the boundary —
they are fine human horses
who will surely win the battle against afflictions.

Those who lack this bridle
and will never come to desire it —
they are churned about by the battle of afflictions
and will wander, utterly homeless.

Venerable ones of the Saṅgha, please listen. Today the Saṅgha will observe the restoration ceremony (poṣadha) of the fourteenth or fifteenth. If the time has come for the Saṅgha and it is agreeable, may the Saṅgha grant its permission. Today the Saṅgha will perform the restoration ceremony and recite the Prātimokṣa Sūtra. This is the motion.

Venerable ones, let us observe the restoration ceremony. Recite the Prātimokṣa Sūtra. Any one of you who has a transgression, confess it. If there is no transgression, remain silent. If you remain silent, I shall understand that the venerable ones are fully pure. Just as a monk, when questioned individually, gives a reply, so too in this assembly of monks this proclamation is to be made up to three times. Furthermore, if a monk in such an assembly of monks has a transgression, is mindful of it, and yet does not confess it when it has been proclaimed up to three times, that constitutes deliberate false speech. Venerable ones, the Blessed One has taught that deliberate false speech is an obstructing factor. Therefore, any monk who has incurred a transgression and desires to be fully purified should confess that transgression, remembering it and acknowledging it as seen. If one confesses, one will abide in a state of touching happiness. If one does not confess and does not acknowledge it, one will not.

Venerable ones, I have now set forth the preamble to the recitation of the Prātimokṣa Sūtra. And so I ask the venerable ones: are you fully pure in this? I ask a second time: are you fully pure in this? I ask a third time: are you fully pure in this? If the venerable ones are fully pure in this, they should remain silent as before, and that is how it is understood.

A summary verse:

Unchaste conduct, theft,
taking of human life —
together with false speech,
these four teachings are here declared.

Venerable ones, these four factors that constitute defeats arise from the fortnightly recitation of the Prātimokṣa Sūtra.

Furthermore, any monk who, having entered upon the training equally with other monks, without having relinquished the training and without having declared the training weakened, engages in unchaste conduct — the practice of sexual intercourse — even with a being born in an animal realm: that monk has incurred a defeat and may not remain.

Furthermore, any monk who, while residing in a village or a wilderness belonging to others, takes what is not given in a manner that counts as theft — however much he takes without it being given, such that a king or a great minister seizes him and says, "You, man, are a thief, a fool, an idiot — you have stolen," and whether he is executed, bound, or expelled — if a monk takes in such a way without it being given, that monk has also incurred a defeat and may not remain.

Furthermore, any monk who intentionally, with his own hand, deprives a human being or one who has taken on human form of life, or provides a weapon to that person, or procures a weapon for that person, or causes that person to die, or speaks words of praise for dying to that person —

"O man, what use is this wretched, impure, evil life to you? O man, death would be better for you than living" — and if, through the desire of his mind and the deliberate thought of his mind, by various means he causes that person to die, or speaks words of praise for dying to that person, and the one who undertook that act dies as a result, that monk too has incurred a defeat and may not remain.

Furthermore, any monk who, without direct knowledge and without full knowledge, has neither the superhuman quality of a noble one, nor attainment, nor understanding, nor vision, nor the practice of meditative experience at any level — yet claims, "I know this, I see this," and then, wishing to purify a transgression that has arisen, at another time, whether asked or unasked, says this: "Venerable ones, I said I knew without knowing" — who said "I see" without seeing, and spoke emptily and falsely — except for the conceit of claiming to know, that monk too has incurred a defeat and may not remain.

Venerable ones, I have now set forth these four categories of defeat. If a monk commits any transgression among them, just as he was from the very beginning, so too he is afterward one who has incurred a defeat; he may not remain living together with monks or sharing their goods.

Concerning this, I now ask the venerable ones: "Are you fully pure in regard to this?" Let me ask a second time, a third time: "Are you fully pure in regard to this?" When the venerable ones are fully pure in regard to this, they remain silent, and that is how it is understood.

Summary verse:

Emission, sexual speech, flattery and service,
A cell, a large dwelling, and the groundless claim,
Just a little division of the Saṅgha and following that side,
Disparaging a household and words of displeasure.

Venerable ones, these thirteen matters involving remainder for the Saṅgha arise from the Prātimokṣa sūtra recited at each half-month.

If one intentionally causes emission — except during a dream — that is a matter involving remainder for the Saṅgha.

Furthermore, any monk who, with a mind inflamed with desire, makes bodily contact with a woman — grasping her hand, grasping her arm, grasping her braid, or stroking or rubbing any limb or part of a limb, claiming it as his own — that is a matter involving remainder for the Saṅgha.

Furthermore, any monk who, with a mind inflamed with desire, in the presence of a woman, utters lewd words connected with sexual intercourse, such as a young man would say to a young woman, that is a matter involving remainder for the Saṅgha.

Furthermore, any monk who, with a mind inflamed with desire, in front of a woman's body, speaks words of praise of his own body's service with the intention of serving himself, saying: "A monk like me, possessed of moral discipline, having virtuous qualities, living in chastity — if one were to serve him by means of this act connected with sexual intercourse, this woman would be the highest among all who have rendered such service" — that is a matter involving remainder for the Saṅgha.

Furthermore, any monk who speaks to a woman using a man's words, or to a man using a woman's words, soliciting her as a wife or a mistress — even if it be only for a moment's meeting — that is a matter involving remainder for the Saṅgha.

If a monk builds a dwelling for himself, raised by his own effort, ownerless, built for his own benefit, that monk should have the dwelling built to the proper measure. The measure of the dwelling is this: in the interior, twelve cubits in height by the measure of the Sugata, and seven cubits in width. He should bring monks to inspect the site. The monks who are brought should examine whether the site is suitable, free from dispute, and fit to build upon. If the monk finds the site unsuitable —

— disputed, or unsuitable to build upon, and he causes a dwelling to be built, raised by his own effort, ownerless, for his own benefit; or if he does not bring monks to inspect the site; or if, having brought monks, he does not show them the site — if it exceeds the proper measure, that is a matter involving remainder for the Saṅgha.

If a monk causes a large monastery to be built for the Saṅgha, with an owner, he must bring monks to inspect the site. The monks who are brought must examine whether the site is suitable, free from dispute, and fit to build upon. If the monk finds the site unsuitable, or disputed, or unfit to build upon, and he causes the large monastery to be built for the Saṅgha, with an owner; or if he does not bring monks to inspect the site; or if, having brought monks, he does not show them the site — that is a matter involving remainder for the Saṅgha.

Furthermore, if a monk, having grown angry and wrathful with the intention of separating this one from the celibate life by any means, accuses a pure monk who has no transgression with a groundless charge of a matter entailing defeat — whether asked about it at another time or not asked, the charge being groundless, and the monk having spoken out of aversion while abiding in aversion — that is a matter involving remainder for the Saṅgha.

Furthermore, if a monk, having grown angry and wrathful with the intention of separating this one from the celibate life by any means, accuses a pure monk who has no transgression with a charge of a matter entailing defeat that is based on something other than what was said — whether asked about it at another time or not asked, the charge being based on something other than what was said, and through that charge being based on something other than what was said he has taken up some matter conceived from the merest hint, a matter merely imagined, and the monk has spoken out of aversion while abiding in aversion — that is a matter involving remainder for the Saṅgha.

Furthermore, if a monk, for the purpose of dividing a harmonious Saṅgha, persistently takes up and holds to a schismatic position that would bring about division, the monks should address him thus: "Venerable one, do not persist in this for the purpose of dividing a harmonious Saṅgha. Do not take up and hold to a position that would bring about division. Be in harmony together with the Saṅgha. Do not split the Saṅgha's harmony. Take delight in concord and be without dispute. Mixed together like water and milk, with one voice speaking as one, dwelling in happiness, illuminating the Teacher's teaching — venerable one, abandon this very basis through which you would divide the Saṅgha."

When the monks have so instructed him, if he abandons it, so much the better. If he does not abandon it, they should instruct and admonish him a second and a third time for the sake of his abandoning it. If, having been instructed and admonished a second and a third time, he abandons it, so much the better. If he does not abandon it, that is a matter involving remainder for the Saṅgha.

If there are one, two, or many monks who are companions of that monk, who side with the one who speaks in discord, and if they say to the monks: "Venerable ones, speak nothing against this monk, whether in terms of virtue or fault. Why so? Because, venerable ones, this monk speaks the Dharma and speaks the Vinaya. This monk, having properly taken up and held to Dharma and Vinaya, applies designations accordingly. This monk speaks with understanding and not without understanding. Therefore we too approve of and accept whatever he approves of and accepts" —

The monks should address those monks in these words: "Venerable ones, do not say as you have been saying — that whether in virtue or fault, nothing should be said against this monk. Why so? Because, venerable ones, this monk does not speak the Dharma and does not speak the Vinaya. Rather, venerable ones, this monk speaks what is not the Dharma and speaks what is not the Vinaya. This monk, having improperly taken up and held to what is not the Dharma and not the Vinaya, applies designations accordingly. This monk speaks without understanding and does not speak with understanding. Therefore, venerable ones, do not approve of and accept whatever he approves of and accepts. Venerable ones, do not wish to divide the Saṅgha. Rather, venerable ones, desire the unity of the Saṅgha. Venerable ones, be in harmony with the Saṅgha. Do not split the Saṅgha in harmony, cherish one another without quarrel, and, like water and milk blended into one, recite together with one voice. If you illuminate the teaching of the Teacher, you will abide in contact with happiness. Venerable ones, abandon this conduct of siding with and speaking in discord with those who divide the Saṅgha." Thus they should be instructed.

If, when those monks are instructed in these words by the monks, they relinquish that basis, then so much the better. If they do not relinquish it, they should be instructed and admonished a second and a third time for the sake of relinquishing that basis. If upon being properly instructed and admonished a second and a third time they relinquish that basis, then so much the better. If they do not relinquish it, this is an offense involving remainder for the Saṅgha.

Suppose a great many monks are residing in dependence upon a certain town or village. Suppose they are corrupting households and practicing evil conduct, and the monks see, hear, or come to know clearly that they are corrupting households; the monks see, hear, or come to know clearly that they are practicing evil. The monks should then address those monks in these words: "Venerable ones, you are ones who corrupt households and practice evil conduct. You have been seen, heard, and it is clearly known that you corrupt households; you have been seen, heard, and it is clearly known that you practice evil. Venerable ones, it is enough for you to reside here. Depart from this place today." Thus they should be instructed.

If those monks say to the monks: "Venerable ones, some monks here act out of desire, some act out of aversion, some act out of delusion, and some act out of fear. Thus, for the very same transgression, some monks are expelled and others are not expelled" —

Then the monks should address those monks in these words: "Venerable ones, do not say as you have been saying — that some monks here act out of desire, some act out of aversion, some act out of delusion, and some act out of fear, and that for the very same transgression some monks are expelled and others are not expelled. Why so? Because those monks do not act out of desire, do not act out of aversion, do not act out of delusion, and do not act out of fear.

"For it is you venerable ones yourselves who are householders defiled by evil, practicing wrongdoing. You are seen, heard, and fully known to have disgraced households. You are seen, heard, and fully known to be practicing evil. Therefore, venerable ones, abandon this manner of speaking — words such as 'acting out of desire,' 'acting out of aversion,' 'acting out of delusion,' and 'acting out of fear.'"

When the monks have thus admonished those monks, if they relinquish that ground, so much the better. If they do not relinquish it, they should be admonished and instructed properly a second and a third time so that they may relinquish that ground. If, upon being properly admonished and properly instructed a second and a third time, they relinquish it, so much the better. If they do not relinquish it, this becomes a matter for the Saṅgha to complete.

Now suppose a certain monk has become by nature one who is difficult to admonish, and when the monks speak to him in accordance with the Dharma and in accordance with the Vinaya — drawing on the precepts included in the formal recitation, and on those included in the sūtras of the Sugata — he says: "Venerable ones, whether you speak virtuously or wrongfully, say nothing to me. And whether I speak virtuously or wrongfully, I will say nothing to you venerable ones either. Venerable ones, stop speaking words to me. As for whatever you may say to me, it is no concern of mine." If, when the monks speak to a monk in this way, he conducts himself as one who will not respond, then the monks should address him thus: "Venerable one, when the monks speak to you in accordance with the Dharma and in accordance with the Vinaya — drawing on the precepts included in the formal recitation, and on those included in the sūtras of the Sugata — do not conduct yourself as one who will not respond. Venerable one, you must indeed respond. When the monks speak to the venerable one in accordance with the Dharma and in accordance with the Vinaya, let the venerable one likewise speak to the monks in accordance with the Dharma and in accordance with the Vinaya. Why so? Because it is by each one responding to the other, by each one guiding and instructing the other, and by each one drawing the other out of transgression that the retinue of the Blessed One, the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly and completely awakened buddha, will flourish. Therefore, venerable one, abandon this conduct of yours — this manner of conducting yourself as one who will not respond."

When the monks have thus admonished that monk, if he relinquishes that ground, so much the better. If he does not relinquish it, he should be admonished and instructed properly a second and a third time so that he may relinquish that ground. If, upon being properly admonished and properly instructed a second and a third time, he relinquishes it, so much the better. If he does not relinquish it, this becomes a matter for the Saṅgha to complete.

Venerable ones, I have now recited the thirteen factors of the Saṅgha's remainder. Of these, nine are by virtue of the first transgressions alone, and four are by virtue of up to the third instance. If a monk has committed any one of these transgressions and knowingly conceals it for as long as he conceals it, he must undergo the practice of suspension against his will for that same length of time. Having undergone suspension against his will, thereafter for six days he is to perform the practice of probation for the satisfaction of the Saṅgha. Having performed the practice of probation, he is to proceed toward rehabilitation, having fulfilled it in accordance with the Dharma.

Having satisfied the Saṅgha of monks, he is to be rehabilitated wherever there is a Saṅgha assembly of twenty monks. If a Saṅgha assembly that is incomplete by even one monk rehabilitates that monk, then that monk's rehabilitation is not accomplished, and those monks are to be censured. That is the procedure for it.

Therein I shall ask the venerable ones: "Are you pure in this matter?" I shall ask a second time and a third time: "Are you pure in this matter?" If the venerable ones are pure in this matter, they should remain silent. That is how it is to be understood.

In summary: solitude with a refuge present, and sitting.

Venerable ones, these two indeterminate rules arise from the recitation of the Prātimokṣa sūtra, chanted fortnight by fortnight.

Furthermore, if a monk is alone with a woman in a secluded place with a refuge present, sitting on a seat in a manner suitable for sexual intercourse, and if a trustworthy laywoman were to speak of it as entailing any of the three rules — defeat, or a rule requiring the remainder of the Saṅgha, or any one of the rules entailing downfall — and if the monk acknowledges having sat there, then he is to be made to carry out whatever rule or rules the trustworthy laywoman has spoken of, whether the three rules entailing defeat, or the rule requiring the remainder of the Saṅgha, or whichever rule entailing downfall she has named. The monk is to be made to carry out that very rule or rules that she has stated. This is an indeterminate rule.

Furthermore, if a monk is alone with a woman in a secluded place with a refuge present, sitting on a seat in a manner not suitable for sexual intercourse, and if a trustworthy laywoman were to speak of it as entailing either of the two rules — a rule requiring the remainder of the Saṅgha, or any one of the rules entailing downfall — and if the monk acknowledges having sat there, then he is to be made to carry out whichever of the two rules, the rule requiring the remainder of the Saṅgha or the rule entailing downfall, that the trustworthy laywoman has spoken of. The monk is to be made to carry out that very rule or rules that she has stated. This too is an indeterminate rule.

Venerable ones, I have now recited the two indeterminate rules. Therein I shall ask the venerable ones: "Are you pure in this matter?" I shall ask a second time and a third time: "Are you pure in this matter?" If the venerable ones are pure in this matter, they should remain silent. That is how it is to be understood.

In summary:
Carrying, setting down, placing and putting aside,
Taking away, and accepting;
Begging, upper robe, lower robe together,
And the separate sending of the value.

Venerable ones, these thirty rules entailing forfeiture and downfall arise from the recitation of the Prātimokṣa sūtra, chanted fortnight by fortnight.

If a monk who has received his robes brings out a surplus garment, he may hold it for up to ten days without distributing it. If he holds it beyond that, it is a downfall with forfeiture.

If a monk who has received his robes brings out a surplus garment, and if even for a single night he is separated from any one of his three robes outside the boundary — unless the Saṅgha has granted permission — it is a downfall with forfeiture.

If a monk who has received his robes brings out a surplus garment, and if he wishes to obtain a certain out-of-season cloth, that monk may accept that cloth. Having accepted it, if he is to depart immediately, he should quickly make it into a robe and keep it. If he is not departing and has no imminent prospect of making up the deficit from cloth not yet arrived, that monk should set that cloth aside until the end of one month. If he keeps it beyond that, it is a downfall with forfeiture.

Furthermore, if a monk causes a nun who is not his relative to wash an old robe, or causes it to be cleaned or pressed —

it constitutes a downfall with forfeiture. Furthermore, if a monk accepts a robe from a nun who is not his relative — except in exchange — it is a downfall with forfeiture. Furthermore, if a monk goes to the home of a householder or a householder's wife who is not his relative and solicits a robe, except at the proper time, it is a downfall with forfeiture. The proper time in this case is as follows: when a monk's robe has been stolen, destroyed, burned, carried off by the wind, or carried off by water — that is the proper time. If a monk's robe has been stolen, destroyed, burned, carried off by the wind, or carried off by water, he should go to the home of a householder or a householder's wife who is not his relative and request a robe. In that case, if a brahmin or a faithful householder wishes to offer many robes at the proper time, that monk may accept from him at most enough for an upper robe and a lower robe. If he accepts more than that, it is a downfall with forfeiture.

If a householder or a householder's wife who is not his relative, for the sake of a monk, has set aside funds for cloth, thinking, "With these cloth funds I will purchase such-and-such a robe, and when the monk by the name of so-and-so arrives, I will present him with a suitable robe at the proper time" — then if that monk, without having been previously invited, forms some notion of personal advantage and goes to that householder or that householder's wife who is not his relative and says, "Venerable one, please take the cloth funds you set aside on my behalf and with those funds purchase for the Venerable such-and-such a robe, and present it to me as a suitable robe at the proper time" — if the robe is completed, it is a downfall with forfeiture.

If both a householder and a householder's wife who is not his relative, for the sake of a monk, have each set aside separate cloth funds, thinking, "With each of our separate cloth funds we will each purchase such-and-such separate robes, and when the monk by the name of so-and-so arrives, we will each present him separately with a suitable robe at the proper time" — then if that monk, without having been previously invited, forms some notion of personal advantage and goes to both that householder and that householder's wife who is not his relative and says, "Venerable ones, please take the separate cloth funds you have each set aside on my behalf and with those separate funds purchase such-and-such separate robes — combining the two together — and present me with one suitable robe at the proper time" — if the robe is completed, it is a downfall with forfeiture.

If, for the sake of a monk, a king, a great minister, a brahmin, a householder, a townsman, a villager, a wealthy person, a merchant chief, or a caravan leader sends cloth funds by the hand of a messenger, and that messenger takes those cloth funds and goes to where that monk is and says to him, "Noble one, please take note: a king, a great minister, a brahmin, a householder, a townsman, a villager, a wealthy person, a merchant chief, or a great caravan leader has sent these cloth funds for you — noble one, please accept these out of compassion" — that monk should say to the messenger: "Dear messenger, monks may not accept cloth funds. When we find a suitable robe at the proper time, we will accept it." If that messenger then says to the monk —

— "Is there anyone who serves as steward for the noble ones, anyone who carries out the duties of a steward for the noble ones?" — then the monk who desires a robe should say to the messenger: "Dear messenger, this person here is a steward for monks; this one carries out the duties of a steward for monks" — and should indicate either an ānandāpindika or a lay steward. The messenger should then take those cloth funds, go to where that steward is, and upon arriving say to the steward: "Dear steward, please take note. With these cloth funds, purchase such-and-such a robe, and when the monk named so-and-so comes, provide him with a suitable robe at the proper time." Having thus given clear and full instructions to the steward, the messenger should then go to where that monk is and upon arriving say to him: "Noble one, I have given proper indication of the steward I was told to point out to you. Go to him, and he will provide you with a suitable robe at the proper time." The monk who desires a robe should go to the steward and say: "Dear steward, I need a robe. Dear steward, I need a robe" — and should remind him two or three times. Having reminded him two or three times, if the robe is then produced, that is well and good. If it is not produced, he should remain silent without speaking in that direction for a fourth, fifth, or sixth time. If, after remaining silent up to a fourth, fifth, or sixth time, the robe is produced, that is well and good. If it is not produced, and he then goes in search of the robe in order to procure it and the robe is thereby obtained, that incurs a transgression entailing forfeiture. If it is not obtained, he should go to wherever those cloth funds came from. Alternatively, through a trustworthy messenger he should send word: "Dear ones, you should know that whatever cloth funds you sent on behalf of such-and-such a monk have served no purpose whatsoever for that monk. Dear ones, please take care that your own wealth is not lost." That is the proper procedure in that case.

The summary verse:

Wool alone, in two parts and in six,
a span, the road, washing, gold and silver,
the one with explicit marks, and trading —
these are the matters here.

Furthermore, if any monk makes himself a sitting mat of wool, it incurs a transgression entailing forfeiture.
