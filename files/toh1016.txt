The Noble Dhāraṇī Called "Completely Pacifying Fever" (Āryajvarapraśamanīnāmadhāraṇī)

The Noble Dhāraṇī Called "Completely Pacifying Fever," known in Sanskrit as the Āryajvarapraśamanināmadhāraṇī and in Tibetan as 'Phags pa rims nad rab tu zhi bar byed pa zhes bya ba'i gzungs, is a short protective scripture meant to relieve those afflicted by fever. It opens at the great charnel ground of Śītavana near Rājagṛha, where a monk asks the Buddha to protect beings tormented by fever. The Buddha then turns to Ānanda and explains that a fever spirit called Garland of Flames dwells in the region, striking beings with fevers that recur daily and at other intervals. The text presents the Buddha's teaching of a dhāraṇī, a sacred incantation, whose recitation pacifies such illness. It is Toh 1016 in the Kangyur, belonging to the class of dhāraṇī texts, the incantations valued in the Tibetan tradition for healing and protection.

In the language of India: āryajvarapraśamanināmadhāraṇī. In the language of Tibet: The Noble Dhāraṇī Called "Completely Pacifying Fever."

Homage to all buddhas and bodhisattvas.

Thus did I hear at one time. The Blessed One was staying at Rājagṛha, in Śītavana, the great charnel ground. Then a certain monk went to where the Blessed One was. On arriving, he bowed his head at the Blessed One's feet and sat down to one side. Seated to one side, that monk said this to the Blessed One: "Venerable Blessed One, there are beings here who are tormented by fever. Blessed One, please protect them."

Then the Blessed One looked to one side and said to the venerable Ānanda, "Ānanda, in this region there dwells a fever called Garland of Flames. It afflicts beings in the form of a fever that comes daily, every second day, every third day, and every fourth day. These are the bases of the secret mantra that protects against it:

tadyathā jvalani jvalani prajvalani pratipani praśamani. May my fever go away! Do not stay! svāhā.

By whatever truth and words of truth the Buddha is unsurpassed in the world, and the Dharma and the Saṅgha are unsurpassed in the world, by that truth and those words of truth, may my fever go away and not stay! svāhā.

sidhyantu mantrapadai taratu vidyataṃ brahma anumote svāhā."

Recite this twenty-one times over a black thread and tie twenty-one knots.

When the Blessed One had spoken thus, those monks rejoiced and praised what the Blessed One had said.

This completes the noble dhāraṇī called "Completely Pacifying Fever."

It was translated, edited, and finalized by the Indian preceptors Jinamitra and Dānaśīla and the chief editor and translator, the monk Yeshé Dé.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The name of the fever, "Garland of Flames," translates the Tibetan literally. I could not confidently identify a Sanskrit original, though something like Jvālāmālā is likely. The four kinds of fever are the classical recurring fevers: one that comes daily, one every second day, one every third day, and one every fourth day. The final instruction about the thread is phrased as a ritual directive, so it is given as an instruction. The Tibetan "mdud pa dor" is read as "tie knots."
