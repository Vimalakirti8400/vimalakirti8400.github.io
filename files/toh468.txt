The Tantra Called the Glorious Great Vajrabhairava (Śrī Vajra Mahābhairava Nāma Tantra)

Toh 468 in the Kangyur is the tantra known in Sanskrit as the Śrī Vajra Mahābhairava Nāma Tantra and in Tibetan as Dpal Rdo rje 'jigs byed chen po'i rgyud, rendered into English as the Tantra Called the Glorious Great Vajrabhairava. It belongs to the class of Anuttarayoga tantras and centers on Vajrabhairava, a wrathful deity described as the King of Wrath who inspires terror in all the gods, presenting instructions for his ritual accomplishment. The text sets out the practical context for this practice, specifying a range of suitable locations — from charnel grounds and river banks to crossroads and mountain peaks — in which the mantra practitioner should work toward the fulfillment of various purposes. As a tantra devoted to one of the most important wrathful meditational deities of the Vajrayana tradition, it holds a significant place among the tantric scriptures preserved in the Kangyur.

Revised by Vajraghosa.

In the Sanskrit language: Śrī Vajra Mahābhairava Nāma Tantra. In the Tibetan language: The Tantra Called the Glorious Great Vajrabhairava.

Homage to the Glorious Great Vajrabhairava, King of Wrath.

Then the accomplishment of Vajrabhairava
is here explained completely and correctly.
It is supremely fierce toward all the gods
and causes them great terror.

For this practice, first the mantra practitioner should reside in whatever pleasing place suits them, and there accomplish all purposes. The places mentioned for this are: a charnel ground, the bank of a river, an isolated tree, a crossroads with a single marker, a four-way crossroads, a mountain peak, an empty house, the dwelling of the Mamos, a god's temple, a battlefield, a city, a forest, or a village — in any such location the mantra practitioner should take up residence.

A yogin who has received the empowerment of the Glorious Great Vajrabhairava should accomplish through this rite the following: subjugation, hooking, killing, driving away, separating, paralysis, the sword rite, eye medicine, the underground rite, pills, transmutation into gold, essence extraction, wealth, elementals, the walking dead, flesh-eaters, yakṣas, female yakṣas, nāgas, and so forth.

As for the rite itself, the mantra practitioner first smears the body with the great ashes of burnt corpses. At midnight, naked with disheveled hair, using the yoga of the noble buffalo-faced one, one draws the maṇḍala in a charnel ground or similar place.

Square in form, with four gates,
adorned with four archways,
bells, silk streamers, and the like,
entirely spread with garlands of flowers.
In all the corners and in the spaces
along the gateway thresholds,
ornamented with vajras and jewels,
and likewise with half-moon forms.

In the center, one draws the wheel — a circular grid of nine cells. One gate is to be left open, while all the remaining sides are to be completely closed off.

In the center one draws a human corpse: the head toward the east, the hands toward the south, the intestines toward the west, the feet toward the north. In the southeast corner, a skull. In the southwest, a skull-grove. In the northwest, a cloth from the charnel ground. In the northeast, a man impaled upon a bright stake.

These are the five mudrās of the charnel-ground seal placed in the center of the wheel.

Then the outer mudrās of the wheel: on the eastern panel, one draws a curved blade, a single-pointed lance, a pestle, a flaying knife, a single-pointed vajra, an iron hook, and the like. On the southern panel, one draws an axe, an arrow, a khaṭvāṅga, a staff, a wheel, a vajra-noose, and the like. On the western panel, one draws a vajra, a small hammer, a tent, a bow, a bell, a wind-banner, and the like. On the northern panel, one draws a threatening finger gesture, a three-pronged peg, elephant hide, a vajra-hammer, a spear, a fire-hearth, and the like. On all four gates and corners, one draws great walking corpses.

If one desires the supreme spiritual attainment, one offers great butter-lamps. As flesh offerings one presents: human flesh, donkey, she-mule, dog, fox, buffalo, elephant, ox, yak, sheep, deer, pig, and the like. Furthermore, one offers the flesh of: vulture, owl, crow, hawk, otter, black turtle, laughing thrush, peacock, domestic fowl, monkey, great evil-omened creatures, and the like.

With utterly concentrated meditative equipoise, one performs the rites of eating, feast offering, fire offering, and torma offering. On all the corners of the maṇḍala, one sprinkles blood repeatedly. Remaining in the yoga of the union with the buffalo face of the Glorious Vajrabhairava, one offers all the divine food substances.

Then the mantra practitioner performs the preliminary conduct.

One anoints the body with Vairocana while alone, or together with a harmonious companion with whom one is at peace. Stripping naked, one loosens the hair. At midnight, one sounds a bell, uttering "ha ha," and settles into meditative equipoise. One who desires the accomplishments (siddhi) should enter into the maṇḍala of the Great Glorious Vajrabhairava.

One offers drink such as exquisitely fragrant beer, and the various flavors of food. Then, from outside, one visualizes the accomplishments and what is not accomplishment, and above that one contemplates the Great Glorious Vajrabhairava with the buffalo face.

Remaining in front of him in the right-contracted posture, with one-pointed mind and with pride, one recites the ten-syllable mantra in very even, settled meditative equipoise.

If a great terror then arises, the mantra practitioner must not be afraid. If one becomes afraid, that constitutes the obstacle of a great obstructor. If an obstacle arises, the accomplishment will not be achieved. Therefore the mantra practitioner must not be afraid. In that very fearlessness, the Great Glorious Vajrabhairava is pleased. Having been pleased, he declares, "What do you desire?" At that point the knowledge-holder should speak, saying: "A sword, subterranean treasure, eye medicine, the transmutation of gold, essence extraction, or whatever other accomplishment I desire in my mind — grant these to me without fail." Even if the mantra practitioner does not achieve all of those in a hundred attempts, all the subtle activities of that practice will certainly be accomplished.

This is the first chapter, showing the maṇḍala, from the Tantra of the Yoga Wheel of the Great Glorious Vajrabhairava, known by the name Mañjuśrī.

Then the rites of the assemblage of activities are fully explained. Accordingly, in due order, activities such as slaying and expelling are to be performed by the wrathful mantra practitioner while abiding in the practice of the Great Glorious Vajrabhairava. First, for a time, the mantra practitioner performs slaying. Why? Because yoga is the principal. In the tantra of yoga, one who possesses the divine practice first performs that, for it is the principal of all practice.

The activity here is as follows. The mantra practitioner who desires to slay draws the wheel of the Great Glorious Vajrabhairava in sixteen spokes on charnel-ground cloth, using a quill pen made from the root feathers of a great crow, or a pen made from human bone, naked, with disheveled hair, facing south — writing it in the practice of the buffalo face — using poison, blood, salt, emblic myrobalan, neem, and the juice of datura.

One places it in the center of two fires, together with the name of the one to be accomplished against, and surrounds it with the ten-syllable mantra, writing eight HŪṂ syllables. On the corners one writes PHAṬ syllables. One anoints one's own body with hot butter. One inserts the yantra between two skulls, places it on top of a tripod, lights the charnel-ground fire blazingly, presses down with the left foot, and recites the ten-syllable mantra. Through this, the target will die without doubt in an instant.

Furthermore, if one desires to strike with epidemic disease, one places that same wheel neatly between two fish skins of a human, digs down half a cubit beneath a fireplace, inserts it, and lights the fire — then the person will be seized by epidemic illness.

Here is taught the practice of the sixteen-spoked wheel. However, the particular feature of this version is that the thirty-two-syllable mantra is combined with the ten-syllable mantra together with the name of the one to be accomplished against, merging the syllables and mantras.

Together with the substances mentioned previously, and with the remnants left by crows — impure leavings — one smears these together with Vairocana on charnel-ground cloth. One then draws the wheel according to the rite described previously. If it is hidden in a charnel ground, the person will die. If it is hidden at the base of a single tree, the person will be expelled.

If it is hidden at the base of a single tree, the person will be separated. If it is hidden in water, the person will become mad. If it is placed in a house, the person will be pacified. If it is retrieved and thoroughly washed, the person will be freed.

There is also another rite that is taught: the Wheel of Yama is drawn on charnel-ground cloth according to the previously described rite, and then one makes an effigy of the one to be accomplished against, eight finger-breadths in measure, using charnel-ground charcoal. The mantra of the wheel is inserted into the chest of this effigy, and it is pierced at the five vital points with a stake made of human bone. All the joints are filled with sharp thorns. This is then placed between two skulls.

Then, in a pit or at seven locations, one buries earth shaped as a figure of Yama about a cubit in size — with a gnashing face, a great belly, a single face, two arms, holding in his hands a sword and a lasso, naked, and with disheveled hair. The two skulls are placed at the chest of this figure. At midday or midnight, one hides it in a charnel ground or a cremation site, orienting the head facing south, and buries it face down. When one returns home, entering into meditative equipoise and applying the sādhana of the mahā face together with the name of the one to be accomplished against, one recites the ten-syllable mantra. If this is done, within three days the person will die. If the image is retrieved and washed, the person will be freed.

Next, a rite for causing madness is taught. One writes the ten-syllable mantra on the skin of a chameleon using poison and other such substances. That skin is then filled with dust from the footprint of the one to be accomplished against and soil from the trail of their urine, and bound with that person's hair. Then, from outside, one sprinkles it together with Vairocana and donkey urine, filling a clay bowl. The wheel is placed inside this, set upon a cremation fire, pressed down with the left foot, and while applying the sādhana of Yama one recites the ten-syllable mantra. If this is done, within one day the person is driven mad. This is the rite for those who cannot be subdued by subsequent measures. The mantra practitioner performs this rite in a state of extreme wrath.

There is also a rite for separation. One draws the Wheel of Yama according to the previously described rite, and using earth from seven locations one fashions a buffalo and a horse. One places these two one upon the other, inserts the wheel into the chest of each of the two, and sets them at the base of a neem tree. The wheel too is written with the blood of a buffalo and a horse. This is a special application of the mantra: if performed in very deep meditative equipoise, within three days it causes separation.

If the mantra practitioner then wishes to drive someone away, one fashions a vixen from earth of seven locations. On its back one visualizes the syllable YAṂ fully transforming into a wind maṇḍala in the shape of a half-moon, and above that one visualizes the one to be accomplished against. Behind that person one visualizes the form of Yama holding a club in his hand, striking the person with that club and, with face looking southward, dragging them away. If the wheel, along with the name of the one to be accomplished against written out in full according to the rite described above, is inserted into the chest of this figure, within seven days the person will without doubt be driven away.

There is yet another rite that is taught. On the day of the sun, one smears an acacia tree with piping-hot butter and climbs it, setting a butter lamp there. The butter of the body is scraped off with a fish-scale and retained in that location. That butter is then taken and the banner cloth of the Great God (Śiva) is taken and burned; the soot from the smoke produced during its burning and that butter are combined together, and this mixture is smeared on the sandal of the one one wishes to drive away. If it is also smeared on both hands and feet of that person, they are driven away in an instant.

There is yet another rite that is taught: on charnel-ground cloth or on birch leaves, one draws the wind maṇḍala using a quill pen with crow's blood and crow's feathers as the ink.

In the center of the wind maṇḍala, one writes the name of the person to be acted upon, binds it at the throat of one who eats the torma offering, then seizes a crow and, holding it in the conjunction of the skull, faces south and hurls it. In that very instant, like the crow, the target is sent spinning across the earth in all directions.

There is yet another rite of driving away: one smears hot butter on a nest taken from a neem tree in which one who eats the torma offering has dwelt. One then takes the ash from burning that nest with wood from the father tree of the neem, and pours it over the head of the person to be driven away. That person will be expelled.

There is yet another supreme rite of separation: feathers of a crow and an owl, and a single hair each from a brahmin and a fierce man, are combined together. One kindles a fire in datura wood and burns them in a smokeless flame. Taking that ash and reciting the ten-syllable mantra, if one places the ash between a woman and a man, they are separated in that very instant.

Next, one takes charnel-ground cloth and, having sprinkled it with poison and other such substances, writes the name of the person to be acted upon. One takes thorns, serpent hair, and serpent skin, inserts them into it, and fashions it into a bundle. If this is burned in the house of any person, those within will fight one another.

Now, for one who wishes to kill: one fashions an effigy from urine and feces, thoroughly smears it with powdered bone, combs it with the hair from a charnel-ground fire, faces south, and with the conjunction of the skull, at midnight stabs and burns it. If this is done together with the name of a given person, that person dies in that very instant. This rite was taught by Vajrabhairava the Great himself.

Now, for one who wishes to drive away: on banner cloth, one draws, using a sixteen-petal wheel, the ten-syllable mantra together with the substances and charnel-ground charcoal previously mentioned, writing the name of the person in question. If this banner is planted on a neem tree, that person will be expelled.

For one who wishes to paralyze: if one writes the name of the person clearly on the wheel of Yamārāja, either inscribed on copper or on cloth, using lac and turmeric, and leaves it in place, that person will become paralyzed.

If the mantrin wishes to bring someone under his power: soil from an anthill, saffron, musk, lac, and turmeric are combined together. If one makes a sixteen-finger effigy of the person to be subjugated, whether male or female, and draws the wheel of Yamārāja on barley husks using musk and blood from the ring finger, places it in the heart of the effigy, keeps it in one's own home, offers red flowers at the three times of day, faces west, and recites the ten-syllable mantra, then within seven days even Śakra himself would come under one's power — to say nothing of an ordinary person. If one further wishes to summon that person, one draws a wheel on a skull using musk and blood from the ring finger, heats it over an acacia fire, and recites together with the name of the one to be called. In that very instant, like the force of the wind, that person will be summoned.

If the mantrin wishes to drive someone mad: one takes datura fruit and mixes it with the powder of wood eaten by wood-worms, combined with raw flesh, and gives it to the target as food and drink, while also reciting the mantra. In that very instant the person goes mad, and within seven days dies.

Then yet another rite is taught: the mantra of Yamārāja is written on charnel-ground cloth using poison and other such substances. One makes an effigy using large butter lamps and Vairocana, places a wheel in its heart, goes to the father's dwelling place, and, with a sharp weapon, stabs it in the fire used for cremating corpses. The yogin then remains in the conjunction of the stacked-elements practice, anoints the body with Vairocana, faces south, and burns it. The target will die swiftly.

Now there follows a rite involving the forehead, beginning with the crow and —

One takes the bones of an owl, and upon these two, using iron thorns, one writes the ten-syllable mantra together with the name of the target. Remaining alone in a place, one recites it together with that person's name, and when one drags them one against the other, a separation will occur.

Then, upon the petals of the Yamārāja wheel, one writes the names of the woman and the man, inserts them into the belly of a frog, and hides them beneath a threshold inscribed with the name of the target. That person's limbs will be pierced as if by weapons.

Then follows this rite for inflaming a girl with desire. One writes the mantra of Yamārāja on charnel-ground cloth using poison and other such substances, builds an effigy about a cubit tall from the ashes used to cremate corpses, places a wheel in its heart, presses it down with the left foot, and recites the ten-syllable mantra, saying: "By the conjunction of Mahī, may the daughter of such-and-such a great one swiftly be inflamed with desire." She will indeed be inflamed.

These rites must not be disclosed to anyone else. If a mantrin of deluded character performs them, he will certainly fall into hell.

In order to benefit those who harm the Three Jewels, who show contempt for the guru, who have broken their samaya vows, who slander the mantras and tantras, and who harm sentient beings, the mantrin should apply injury with a mind moved by compassion. If done for other purposes, the samaya is broken and one falls into great calamity.

I have explained this also in other tantras. It is declared in verse:

Those persons who revile the ācārya,
who denigrate the Great Vehicle,
who slander the tantras' yoga and primordial wisdom —
they should be forcefully slain,
or at the very least driven from their dwelling.
A yogin who, lacking compassion,
applies such acts indiscriminately toward others —
upon that one, precisely the same act
will come back in return.
Why does the yogin undertake this?
Solely so that sentient beings may attain.

This is the second chapter — the chapter on the accomplishment of all rites — from the tantra of the yoga of the glorious great Vajrabhairava, known as Mañjuśrī.

Then is thoroughly proclaimed the collection of attachment-mantras in their proper order. The mantrin should dwell in a very pure region and there collect the mantras. As follows:

In the sixth, the first; in the fifth, the fifth; in the sixth, the second, furnished with the second vowel-letter; in the second, the third, again furnished with the second vowel-letter; in the seventh, the third as well; in the fourth, the third, furnished with the thirteenth vowel-letter; in the fifth, the fifth, furnished with the eleventh vowel-letter; in the sixth, the first; the first of the sixth becomes doubled; then add the wind-seed; in the fifth, the fifth, pressed together with eleven; in the fourth, the third, pressed equally with the thirteenth; in the sixth, the second, furnished with the fifth vowel-letter; in the third, the fifth, then add the wind-seed, furnished with the full thirteenth vowel-letter; in the fourth, the third, then add the wind-seed; in the sixth, the first; in the fourth, the third, and to that the wind-seed furnished with the thirteenth vowel-letter; in the fourth, the fifth, furnished with the third vowel-letter, then add the fire-seed, and likewise the wind-seed, then add the final letter furnished with the eleventh vowel-letter, and likewise the excellent wind-seed; in the sixth, the first, then add the final letter furnished with the eleventh vowel-letter; then again the wind-seed; in the second, the first — make that doubled; in the fourth, the fifth, furnished with the third vowel-letter, then the fire-seed; in the fifth, the fifth, then add the wind-seed.

In the first, give Vairocana; joined with the lord of the supreme; in the fifth, the second; and in the third, the first — the learned one should recite these twice. This is the root mantra of the great wrathful one of what is called the Mañjuśrī Mahābhairava Tantra Wheel, the buffalo-faced one. By reciting it one hundred thousand times, one accomplishes all activities. This is the king of mantras; what has not arisen will not come to arise.

In the fourth syllable of the fourth, furnished with the fourth vowel-letter, give the fire-seed below; then the lord of the realm at the sixteenth end. In the second of the seventh, furnished with the fourth vowel-letter, and in the first of the third, affix the fire-seed below — the lord of the realm at the sixteenth end. In the fourth of the sixth, furnished with the third vowel-letter; also the first of the first, furnished with the seventh vowel-letter; give the fire-seed below. In the first of the fourth, furnished with the second vowel-letter; the fifth of the fourth — the learned one recites twice. In the first, give Vairocana; to the seed of the eighth semivowel, furnished with the sixth vowel-letter, adorned at the crown with a bindu; in the fifth, the second; and in the third, the first. This is the king of mantras; it is the application of the buffalo-faced one, and it accomplishes all activities. It is renowned as the mantra of activity. If one recites it three hundred thousand times, one accomplishes all activities.

In the first of the sixth, and also in the fifth of the fifth, furnished with the second vowel-letter; the first of the fourth — place its fifth on top; the first of the first; in the first, give Vairocana; to the seed of the eighth semivowel, furnished with the sixth vowel-letter, adorned at the crown with a bindu; in the fifth, the second; and in the third, the first. This is renowned as the heart mantra of the buffalo-faced one. If one recites each syllable one hundred thousand times, one accomplishes whatever activities there may be in the three worlds, all of them without exception. At the time of this ritual of gathering the mantras, one should make offerings. This is the third chapter, teaching the gathering of mantras, from the Tantra of the Yoga of Glorious Great Vajra-Bhairava, called Mañjuśrī.

Thereupon the accomplishment of Vajra-Bhairava is fully explained. Preceded by the selflessness of all phenomena, one is to visualize the deity. First of all, by reciting the mantra of natural purity, having rendered all phenomena into the mode of selflessness, one visualizes in the middle of space the wind maṇḍala — dark blue in appearance, arisen from the form of the syllable YAṂ. Above that, one visualizes the moon maṇḍala arising from the first vowel-letter. Above that, one visualizes arising from the form of the syllable DHĪḤ the form of Mañjuśrī as a youth. There, one visualizes the sun maṇḍala arisen from the form of the syllable A. From that sun maṇḍala, rays of light stream forth, intensely summoning the Tathāgatas dwelling in the ten directions, the bodhisattvas, the goddesses of knowledge (vidyā-devatās), the wrathful ones, and so forth; they come and are visualized as seated upon the sun maṇḍala. Then Mañjuśrī and those Tathāgatas and the rest become unified and are visualized in the sun maṇḍala. In the aspect of blazing rays of light, one visualizes them as radiantly pervading a hundred thousand leagues in every direction. Above that, one visualizes the syllable HŪṂ, dark blue in color, perfectly endowed with the five rays of light. From that HŪṂ, light streams forth in the form of rays, re-enters as previously described, and from that syllable one is to visualize the great vajra standing upright, black in color and endowed with the five rays of light.

From that vajra, one visualizes filling the sun as sesame seeds fill a sesame pod — an immeasurable multitude of subtle forms of buddhas, bodhisattvas, wrathful ones, and vidyā goddesses (Skt. vidyādevī), all dwelling upon the sun. Those buddhas and the rest then thoroughly tame sentient beings, bringing them to full ripening, and establish them in awakening. Thereupon one visualizes all of them gathered together once more and fully entered into the form of the vajra.

That very form of the vajra has nine faces. It is the great terrifying vajra with the face of a buffalo (Skt. mahiṣa), blazing like the fire at the end of an age, capable of consuming the three realms. The mouths are gnashing. The tongue coils as it utters "Hā Hā." The furrowed brow of the glaring, wrathful gaze is terrifying. From alongside those wrathful brows, the eyebrows and eyes are wrathful, roaring like the time of a great and terrible eon. It devours human blood, fat, marrow, and flesh. It points the threatening finger at gods and the rest, both worldly and supramundane. It devours Śakra (Indra), Brahmā, the Great Lord (Śiva), Viṣṇu, yakṣas, and the like. The crown is adorned with a skull — terrible, with a crest of gnashing skulls — making it a crown of skulls, utterly fearsome. Crying out "PHAṬ!" and adorned with the ornament of a garland of hanging skulls, it has sixteen feet. Naked in form, the male organ is upright. The left leg is extended. The belly is vast. The hair is wild, standing upright. It instills great terror, crying out "PHEṂ!" It is possessed of thirty-four arms.

It holds a fresh elephant hide. This is the glorious great terrifying vajra with the buffalo face, to be thoroughly visualized. Having thus stabilized the visualization extremely well, the mantrin should always meditate in deep equipoise through the practice of suchness.

The first right hand holds a curved knife, the second holds a single-pointed weapon, the third holds a pestle, the fourth holds a flaying knife, the fifth holds a single-pointed vajra, the sixth holds an axe, the seventh holds a short spear, the eighth holds an arrow, the ninth holds an iron hook, the tenth holds a club, the eleventh holds a khaṭvāṅga staff, the twelfth holds a wheel, the thirteenth holds a vajra, the fourteenth holds a vajra hammer, the fifteenth holds a sword, the sixteenth holds a bell.

The first left hand holds a skull cup, the second holds a severed head, the third holds a shield, the fourth holds a leg, the fifth holds a lasso, the sixth holds a bow, the seventh holds entrails, the eighth holds a hand bell, the ninth holds a hand, the tenth holds a charnel-ground shroud, the eleventh holds a man impaled upon a sharpened stake, the twelfth holds a fire pit, the thirteenth holds a skull bowl, the fourteenth points the threatening finger, the fifteenth holds a three-pronged trident, the sixteenth holds a charnel-ground cloth blown by the wind. The two principal hands hold the fresh elephant hide.

Among the right feet: the first treads upon a human, the second upon a buffalo, the third upon an ox, the fourth upon a donkey, the fifth upon a camel, the sixth upon a dog, the seventh upon a sheep, the eighth upon a fox. The right feet ride upon these.

Among the left feet: the first treads upon a vulture, the second upon an owl, the third upon a crow, the fourth upon a parrot, the fifth upon a hawk, the sixth upon a great garuḍa, the seventh upon a lizard, the eighth upon a laughing one. The left feet press down upon these.

The first and principal face of the glorious great terrifying one is very dark, in the form of a buffalo, of intensely wrathful aspect. On the three faces of the right horn: the first is blue, the second is red, the third is yellow — these three faces of the lord are intensely wrathful. On the three faces of the left horn: the first is white, the second is smoky, the third is black, intensely wrathful. These gnashing faces are to be thoroughly visualized.

Between the two horns, the central face is red and utterly terrifying, and one visualizes blood dripping from the mouth. Above, the face of Mañjuśrī — slightly yellowish, momentarily wrathful, adorned with the ornaments of a youth, with the five braided locks at the crown — is to be thoroughly visualized.

All the faces are to be visualized as having three eyes. Thus one always fixes one's attention single-pointedly in this way. Having meditated that this very one — the glorious great Vajrabhairava (Skt. Vajrabhairava), he who bears the face of the buffalo — is oneself, the yogin who abides in the state of the hero then recites the mantra aloud. At the time of recitation, one visualizes a solar disc at one's own heart center, and upon it one places the syllables of one's own mantra. Blazing, with a mind outwardly undistracted, one performs the recitation.

The mantra practitioner who abides in this practice
should always perform every act as Bhairava.
Meditate while dwelling in a charnel ground,
and always consume the five nectars.
By this there is no other means of accomplishment than accomplishment itself.
Moreover, consuming large amounts of oil lamps,
the yogin who wanders at night
should recite the root mantra three hundred thousand times.
The mantra practitioner who recites this
will come to perform every act.

This is the fourth section on visualization, taken from the tantra called the Mañjuśrī — that is, the glorious great Vajrabhairava.

Then the glorious great Bhairava teaches and fully expounds the ritual of the painted icon. One uses a hero's cloth, or cloth that has covered a corpse, or cloth bearing flowers, or cloth on which a male child has been born. If none of these are to be found, any cloth will do. The painter should be excellent, free of conceptual agitation, not prone to anger, pure, skilled, of a composed mind, endowed with faith, compassionate, free from craving, and thus endowed with such qualities. With the hands of such a painter, one draws the image on the hero's cloth. If one desires accomplishment, one makes offerings to the hero. The ritual procedure is as follows: dwelling in a secret place, one draws with deep meditative equipoise. The painter and the practitioner may see the image, but it must be drawn so that other worldly people cannot see it. It should depict sixteen feet, thirty-four hands, nine faces, naked, of a black color, with the left leg extended, bearing a form that terrorizes even the greatly terrifying.
