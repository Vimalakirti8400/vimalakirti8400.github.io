The Means of Accomplishing Sixteen-Armed Hevajra (Hevajraṣoḍaśabhujasādhana)

The Means of Accomplishing Sixteen-Armed Hevajra, in Sanskrit Hevajraṣoḍaśabhujasādhana and in Tibetan Kyé Dorjé Chak Chudrukpé Druppé Tap, is a short sādhana, a manual of daily deity practice, centered on Hevajra in his form with eight faces and sixteen arms, embraced by his consort Nairātmyā. It guides the practitioner through the whole day, from rising at dawn, protection, offerings to the deity and the eight goddesses, the four immeasurables and meditation on emptiness, through the generation of oneself as the deity, empowerment, mantra recitation and the giving of torma, and on to the yogic practices to be applied when eating, in sexual union and when falling asleep. Its colophon names its author as the paṇḍita and master Kṛṣṭa. It is Toh 1297 in the Tengyur, where it belongs among the tantra commentaries as one of the many practice texts of the Hevajra cycle.

In the language of India: he vajra ṣoḍas bhuja sādhani. In the language of Tibet: The Means of Accomplishing Sixteen-Armed Hevajra.

Homage to Mañjuśrī, the ever-youthful.
Homage to Vajraḍāka.

To that Heruka I bow,
By merely tasting the savor of whose cultivation
This grasping at things as real is cast far away
And all good qualities turn to light.

Rising from bed at dawn, one visualizes at the heart a sun disc arisen from raṃ, and with its light makes oneself stainless. Then one recites oṃ rakṣa rakṣa hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā three times and performs the protection of the place, of oneself and of the yoga. Entering the meditation room, one sits on a comfortable seat and makes a maṇḍala with the mantra called oṃ vajra rekhe hri. In the center of what has been blessed with the mantra called oṃ vajra midinī vajībhava vajra bandha hūṃ, one recites oṃ āḥ hūṃ and places a flower. In the east one does so with gi gorī namaḥ. In the south, with caṃ corī namaḥ. In the west, with vaṃ vetālī namaḥ. In the north, with hūṃ ghasmarī namaḥ. In the northeast, with paṃ pukkasī namaḥ. In the southeast, the corner of fire, with śaṃ śavarī namaḥ. In the southwest, the quarter of Nairṛti, with caṃ caṇḍalī namaḥ. In the northwest, the corner of wind, with ḍi ḍombiṇi namaḥ.

With oṃ deva picu vajra hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā one properly worships the Blessed One, and one worships Nairātmyā as well with the mantra oṃ a ā i ī u ū ṛ ṝ ḷ ḹ e ai o au aṃ svāhā. Again, one should worship the Blessed One with the eight phrases: oṃ aṣṭan nāya namaḥ oṃ piṅga ujña keśa varta sane namaḥ oṃ catur viṃśati netāya namaḥ oṃ ṣeḍaśa bhujāya namaḥ oṃ kṛṣṇa jimūta vapuṣe namaḥ oṃ kapāla mālā aneka dhariṇi namaḥ oṃ āddhāta krūra cittāya namaḥ arbhan du daṃṣṭiṇe namaḥ. One offers the flowers and the rest, blessed with oṃ vajra puṣbe āḥ hūṃ oṃ vajra dhūpe āḥ hūṃ oṃ vajra ghanna āḥ hūṃ oṃ vajra nevidye āḥ hūṃ, and recites the verse of the three refuges three times. Having again made offerings and prostrated, one gives the torma and requests departure with vajra muḥ.

After that one should cultivate loving-kindness, the wish to establish all sentient beings in the bliss of the deity; compassion, the wish to remove all their suffering; joy, the wish that the bliss of the deity should continue for them without interruption; and equanimity, the wish to bring them close to the unsurpassed path.

Again, focusing with the mind on all phenomena, one sees that when examined they have the nature of emptiness and luminosity, and one blesses this with oṃ śunyatā jñāna vajra svabhāva akonahaṃ.

Immediately, one visualizes aṃ and hūṃ upon a sun arisen from raṃ. With their light one establishes in perfect awakening the sentient beings dwelling in measureless and limitless world systems, and imagines them gathered back into those two syllables and dissolving into them. Thereby, in a moment, one should meditate on oneself, fully complete, as the Blessed One together with the Blessed Lady: sixteen-armed and eight-faced, wearing the five seals, striding with four legs, trampling the hearts of the four māras in the half cross-legged dancing pose, with a garland of heads hanging down, the body the color of a blue cloud at the time of the world's destruction, wrathful, with reddish-yellow hair blazing upward, possessing twenty-four eyes, and holding skull cups in his sixteen hands. In those of the right are an elephant, a horse, a donkey, an ox, a camel, a human, a śarabha and a cat. In the eight on the left he holds Earth, Water, Wind, Fire, the Moon, the Sun, Yama and Dhanada (Kubera). On his crown he bears a crossed vajra. He steals away the suffering of saṃsāra, and he is embraced by Nairātmyā, who holds a curved knife and a skull cup.

After that one visualizes hūṃ upon a sun at one's heart, and at the heart of Nairātmyā the syllable e upon a moon. At the crown is the syllable oṃ, and at the throat one does likewise. Having set the syllable hūṃ at the heart, one places Mohavajrī at the eyes, Dveṣavajrī at the ears, Mātsaryavajrī at the nose, Rāgavajrī at the mouth, Īrṣyāvajrī at the body, and Nairātmyā at the mind. With the light from the heart one invites the buddhas and requests empowerment with oṃ abhiṣiñcaturmā sarva tathāgata. Then, as the buddhas confer empowerment, one imagines that a rain of flowers and saffron falls, and one should imagine that the goddesses praise oneself.

When one grows weary of meditating, one should recite the mantra oṃ deva picu vajra hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā. When rising from the session, one dissolves the goddess into the seed syllable at one's own heart and should remain in the form of the deity.

At the beginning of meditation one should also give torma. Its sequence is this. One imagines a wind maṇḍala from yi; above it, from raṃ, a fire maṇḍala; above that, from ā, a skull cup. Above that one visualizes the substances called go ku da ha na and ma ra mu su bi, blessed with oṃ. Above them, from the transformed syllable hūṃ, is a white vajra. From the union of vajra and lotus, fire blazes, and the substances are cooked and become liquid. By its light one sees the forms of all the tathāgatas, gathers the nectar of the seed syllables of their hearts, makes it dissolve into the liquid, and should bless it with the mantra oṃ ā hūṃ. This is the rite for purifying the substances of the torma and the rest.

From space, within a source of phenomena, at the center of an eight-petaled lotus, one visualizes the circle of the Blessed One's maṇḍala, or the solitary hero. On the petals of the directions one visualizes Indra and the others, each embraced around the neck by his own consort, standing with the left leg extended, black in color, with Akṣobhya as crown ornament, and holding vajra and bell. Above that one should visualize Brahmā in the same way, white in color, with Vairocana as crown ornament. Below, one should visualize the nāgas and the others who dwell beneath the earth as red, with Amitābha as crown ornament.

One visualizes, on the lotus-petal tongues of them all, a white vajra arisen from hūṃ, and the goddesses Gaurī and the rest, arisen from the seed syllable at one's heart, should fill them with completely purified nectar. Its mantra is this: inda jama jala jakṣa bhūta vahni vayu rakṣa canda srujja mādapappatāle a ṭha sabba svāhā aṃ deva liṃ bhuṃ ja ji gha phulla dhūpa māṃsa piṃgha a māsppa kājās sādha khānti khuṇe pheḍa gāda oṃ akāro mukhaṃ sarva dharmmāṇāṃ āryanutpanda tāt oṃ aḥ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā. Having recited this three times, one should give the purifying torma, with this mantra called oṃ hrī vi śudhhāḥ dharmā ssarba pāpaṃ viśodhaya sarva vikalpa naya hūṃ. Having made offerings mentally, one requests them to depart with this, called oṃ vajra muḥ.

At mealtime, too, one prepares the food substances in the same way, makes one's own tongue a white vajra arisen from hūṃ, visualizes the Blessed One at one's heart, and gathers all the tathāgatas into him. Then, with the fire arising from the seed syllable at the heart of the wisdom being, one purifies the food substances and performs a fire offering.

At the time of passion, too, one regards the wisdom partner as having the form of Nairātmyā, and imagines oṃ on her head, hūṃ at her heart and āṃ at the center of her lotus. One blesses the lotus with this, called oṃ padma sukha dhara mahā rāga sukhaṃ datta catur ānanda bhaga viśva hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ karyaṃ kuru ṣva me. One places the three syllables on oneself, visualizes a vajra arisen from hūṃ, and its jewel from oṃ. Having blessed the vajra with this, called oṃ vajra mahā dīṣa catur ānanda dayaka khaga mukhe ekarāso nātha hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ katraṃ kuru ṣṭa me, one should begin the union with siṃ siṃ siṃ ha ha ha. Having recognized the moments through the guru's instructions, one draws up the nectar at the culmination and should satisfy oneself.

At bedtime, one resolves that all this diversity has the nature of the coemergent, and should then fall asleep.

So that the childish may understand,
By this means of accomplishment I have made
Of the Teacher, the eight-faced Protector,
May all beings become Vajradhara.

The Means of Accomplishing Glorious Sixteen-Armed Hevajra, composed by the paṇḍita and master Kṛṣṭa, is complete.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

At the throat the Tibetan reads only "one does," where the sequence of crown, throat and heart leads one to expect a syllable, most likely āḥ; it is rendered as written. The wind maṇḍala is said to arise from "yi," evidently standing for the usual yaṃ. The ten syllables go ku da ha na and ma ra mu su bi are the standard abbreviations for the ten sacramental substances, the five lamps and five nectars.  The phrase rendered "draws up the nectar at the culmination" reads literally "takes the completion and the nectar by taking," evidently referring to the yogic technique of drawing the essence upward taught in the guru's oral instructions. The author's name is spelled Kṛṣṭa; it may stand for Kṛṣṇa (Kāṇha), a well-known Hevajra master, but it is given here as written.
