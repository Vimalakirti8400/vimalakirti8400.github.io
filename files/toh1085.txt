The Dhāraṇī Called Glorious Mahākāla (Śrīmahākālanāmadhāraṇī)

The Dhāraṇī Called Glorious Mahākāla, the Protector, known in Sanskrit as Śrī Mahākāla Nāma Dhāraṇī and in Tibetan as Dpal mgon po nag po zhes bya ba'i gzungs, is a short scripture centered on a protective incantation associated with the wrathful guardian deity Mahākāla. It is set soon after the Buddha's awakening, while he is still dwelling at the foot of the tree of awakening, having subdued Māra on the vajra seat. There Mahākāla, described as the great commander of the gods, the Lord of Hosts, and the glorious great Black Protector, approaches the Blessed One, bows at his feet, and offers him his own knowledge mantra, asking the Buddha to accept it. The text is Toh 1085 in the Kangyur and belongs to the class of dhāraṇī texts, the collections of sacred formulas valued for their protective and ritual power.

In the language of India: śrī mahākāla nāma dhāraṇī
In the language of Tibet: The Dhāraṇī Called Glorious Mahākāla, the Protector.

Homage to all the buddhas and bodhisattvas.

Thus did I hear at one time. Not long after the Blessed One had subdued Māra on the vajra seat and become manifestly and perfectly awakened, he was dwelling at the foot of the tree of awakening. Then the great commander of the gods, the Lord of Hosts (Gaṇapati), Mahākāla, who wields power over the lord of the desire realm, the glorious great Black Protector, came to where the Blessed One was. He bowed his head at the feet of the Blessed One, sat down to one side, and said this:

"Venerable Blessed One, I offer you this knowledge mantra of mine. Please accept it.

oṃ śrī mahākāla ghaṇa ghaṇaṭi svāhā
namaḥ śrī mahākāla gaṇe gutiśāya
namo yoginaṇīna
tadyathā
oṃ hile hile kite kiteni cide cideni svāhā"

By reciting this, all illnesses, all epidemics, all grahas, all calamities, all bhūtas, all malicious meditations, and all evil glances are bound: above, as far up as the Paranirmitavaśavartin heaven; below, as far down as the hells of sentient beings; and horizontally, all around, as far as the encircling mountains.

Furthermore, he is the guardian of temples and maṇḍalas, the physician for the disease of the three poisons, the life-pillar of all vidyādharas, the war god of yogis, the lord of those who subdue māras, the one who utterly destroys Laṅkāpurī, the city of the rākṣasas, and who shatters the yakṣa's daughter, the lightning of the sky, into dust like clouds. He has promised and vowed to protect the teaching of the Three Jewels.

This completes the noble dhāraṇī called Glorious Mahākāla, the Protector.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The glossary treats "Lord of Hosts" as the separate god Gaṇapati. Here, however, it stands in a single string of epithets for the one speaker. The single ergative-marked subject identifies this speaker as Mahākāla, so the phrase is rendered as an epithet of Mahākāla.

The phrase "the lord of the desire realm," over whom Mahākāla holds power, probably refers to Māra, or to Śiva as Kāmeśvara.

The term rendered "calamities" (mi ngas) is uncertain. It may mean sudden misfortunes or terrors.

The phrase about the yakṣa's daughter (sprin rdul du rlog pa) is syntactically ambiguous. It could also be read as "shatters to cloud-dust" or "scatters like clouds into dust." It could also be read as "shatters to dust the clouds of the yakṣa's daughter, the sky-lightning."

"Encircling mountains" renders kho ra khor yug ... ri, probably the ring of mountains (cakravāla) at the edge of the world system.
