The Commentary on the Difficult Points of the Hevajra Tantra, called Padminī (Hevajratantrapañjikā Padminī nāma)

The Commentary on the Difficult Points of the Hevajra Tantra, called Padminī, known in Sanskrit as the Hevajratantrapañjikā Padminī and in Tibetan as Kye'i rdo rje'i rgyud kyi dka' 'grel padma can, is an Indian commentary on the Hevajra Tantra, one of the principal scriptures of the highest class of Buddhist tantra. It opens with homage to Mañjuśrī, to Vajrasattva as lord of the ḍākinīs, and to the vajraḍākas, and celebrates connate joy as the union of wisdom and means before beginning its explanation. It then works through the root tantra from its opening words, "Thus did I hear," clarifying difficult terms and their deeper meaning. The work is traditionally associated with the Indian master Saroruha, a figure closely linked with the Hevajra tradition. It is Toh 1181 in the Tengyur, where it belongs among the tantric commentaries devoted to the Hevajra cycle.

In the Indian language: hevajra tantra pañjikā padma ni nā ma. In the Tibetan language: The Commentary on the Difficult Points of the Hevajra Tantra, called Padminī.

Homage to Mañjuśrī, Lord of Speech, the Youthful.

To glorious Vajrasattva,
Wheel-turning lord of the ḍākinīs,
Who through the five wisdoms and the three bodies
Protects beings, I pay homage.

To all the vajraḍākas, as many as there are,
Who cut the bonds of conceptual thought
And fully engage for the sake of the world,
I always pay homage.

Having bowed to the connate, lovely joy,
The glory endowed with perfect selflessness,
Wisdom and means as one,
I shall explain this and its reality.

On "Thus did I hear" and so forth: "Thus" means compiling the meaning exactly as it is. "By me" means heard by myself. "At one time" means at the time when he was dwelling. Where? "In the bhaga of the vajra consort, the body, speech and mind of all the tathāgatas," that is, the nature of the supported maṇḍala. The supporting maṇḍala is also the bhaga, that is, in the celestial palace at its center.

Outer, secret, and likewise inner,
And suchness: the meaning is fourfold:
The maṇḍala, the action seal,
The body, and the absence of intrinsic nature.

"Heard" means dripping into the bhaga of the consort. vi means "various." ja ha ra means "carrying off." "Thus by me" means in that manner.

Alternatively, "Thus did I hear" means dripping into the four wheels, that is, dwelling in them. Alternatively, "thus" refers to perfect union, "by me" is self-grasping, and "heard" is its destruction. "At one time" means in the moment that is not shared by all.

Alternatively, "thus by me" means from the four divine abidings (brahmavihāras), and "heard" means arisen.

"The Blessed One" is the nature of great bliss. "Dwelt in the bhaga of the vajra consort, the body, speech and mind of all the tathāgatas" means through emptiness; this is what is to be accomplished.

"There the Blessed One spoke" means in that place. Alternatively, "spoke," having looked upon the fortunate ones, in his nature as the enjoyment body, is the act of addressing them.

"The heart of the three secrets of all the tathāgatas": the heart is Vajrasattva. It is the very heart of the secret of secrets of that. "The vajra heart, the heart of all the tathāgatas, the nature of the three bodies": this is declared to be the result itself.

Because qualifiers and the thing qualified were spoken for the name of Vajrasattva, the question about the three bodies is "Vajrasattva." The explanation of "by this wisdom" is "by reasoning," that is, the Dharma body. Alternatively, by this definitive analysis, "wisdom and reasoning" means "through union with compassion."

"Great" means the bodhisattva. Because it is to be realized by them, it is the gnosis whose nature is wisdom and means. Being filled with those flavors is the enjoyment body.

"Samaya" means that which brings one rightly and causes one to attain. The samaya being, by fully engaging for the sake of those to be trained, is the emanation body.

The question about the meaning of what is to be accomplished is "Hevajra." Because it protects sentient beings through the means of bliss, it is great compassion. Alternatively, it is called mahā kaṃ: mahā is "great," and kaṃ is "bliss," meaning "encompassed by great bliss."

Therefore, as for "the tantra whose nature is wisdom and means," tantra is the union whose nature is nondual. "Tantra" is what is to be expounded. "I shall explain; listen" means "grasp it!" That itself is "the power of gazes, summoning, and the great signs." And what is that? It is "paralysis and expulsion," and so forth. Likewise, it is the cause of generating and sustaining the yoginīs. "Power" means the miraculous power of might. "Wisdom" is suchness. "Consciousness" means Vajrā and the rest. "Deities" means the cause of the two-armed one and so forth. "First" means at the beginning of the tantra. "One" means nondual. Because he is Heruka and also the cause of generation, it says "Heruka is the cause of generation."

"By the very entity": here, having contemplated it as real, sentient beings are liberated, and not otherwise. "Bound by the bond of entities" means through attachment to them. "By fully knowing non-entity" means by nondual gnosis; thus one becomes liberated. "Likewise" means in the sequence that has been taught. "Heruka should be meditated upon" means one should meditate with full knowledge. "Non-entity" means having become devoid of intrinsic nature; therefore one should first meditate on entity. That very non-entity is what is to be fully known. Being free of both entity and non-entity, one should meditate on Heruka, whose nature is entity and non-entity.

Where should one meditate? It says, "Great gnosis abides in the body." Great gnosis is the gnosis of great bliss. "Free of all conceptual thought" means the nature of nonreferential gnosis. "Performing the actions of all things" means by destroying them. "Although it abides, it is not produced by the body" means: although it is to be examined in the body, it is not born from the body. It is pervasive, unborn, unceasing, and so forth.

The question posed with the thought, "How is it to be examined in the body?" is "O Blessed One." In order to discern individually what is to be accomplished, it says "those." The channels of means and wisdom, the two, and of their very indivisibility, are the three principal ones among these. The one is divided by function into the Indestructible (Abhedyā) and the rest. Through the indivisibility of the three vajras, it is well known there as the Indestructible. Through its division into the tips of hairs and so forth, that same one is applied as the Subtle Form (Sūkṣmarūpā). Because it is comprised of inanimate matter, it is the Embodied (Rūpiṇī). Because it abides in the body, it is the Excellent (Bhadrā). Because it lies on the left side, it is the Left (Vāmā). Because of its roundness, it is the Dwarf (Vāmanī). Because of its tortoise shape, it is the Tortoise-born (Kūrmajā). Because it cultivates existence, it is the Cultivator (Bhāvakī).

Because it bestows empowerment, it is the Empowering (Sekā). Because it expels the afflictions, it is the Faulty (Doṣavatī). Because it makes the body move, it is the Abiding (Viṣṭā). Because it gives birth, it is the Mother (Mātarī). Because it goes everywhere, it is the Night (Śarvarī). Because it is peaceful, it is called the Cool-giving (Śītadā). Because its very nature is insatiability, it is Lalanā. Because it is formless, it is Avadhūtī. Because it gives taste, it is Rasanā. Because it is like the moon, it is the Measure. Because it is like great darkness, it is the Black One (Kṛṣṇavarṇā). The Common (Sāmānyā) is so called because of its equal state. Because it gives all causes, it is the Cause-giver (Hetudāyikā). Because it is free of all union, it is the Disjoined (Viyogā). The Joy-maker (Premaṇī) is so called because it is made up of joy. The Accomplished (Siddhā) is so called because it grants accomplishment. The Fiery (Pāvakī) is so called because it burns the afflictions. The Good-minded (Sumanā) is the goddess of wisdom. The Threefold-entering (Trivṛttā) is that which enters in three ways. Because it possesses all the qualities of desire, it is said to be the Desirous (Kāminī). Because it is the mistress of the house, it is the Household (Gehā). Because it performs fierce actions, it is the Fierce (Caṇḍikā). Because it destroys the māras, it is the Destroyer of Māras (Māradārikā). These will be explained below.

"The three existences" means the nature of the three bodies, by way of the form of the three channels. "Transformed" refers to these thirty-two. Ultimately they are of the nature of Avadhūtī; therefore they are free of grasped and grasper. "Or else, they are expressed by the characteristic of being the means of all" means that, in the form of the fifteen yoginīs, all of these are taught as characterized by being the means of meditation.

"That and that very one" is the "summation," because the vajra of great bliss sums up all phenomena into one; or else, what is to be explained by way of the divisions of supreme great bliss is the characteristic of division. Āli and kāli themselves are means and wisdom; they are also Lalanā and Rasanā, or else Caṇḍālī and the Indestructible. The abodes, that is, the Dharma and the rest and, as appropriate, the body and the rest, are in each respective one.

Explaining what was said earlier: "e va" and so forth, that is, emanation and the rest, in their order. "The wheel that teaches that" is also connected with what follows, like a crow's eye. For "various forms" and so forth, its explanation will be "various forms" and so forth. That mind vajra is to be understood in the manner of suffering and the rest. It is in the manner of the four realities: there, its nature as oneself is reality. That same one, through the illusory nature of the deity's form, the embodiment of complete enjoyment, is reality. That same one, through its nature of knowing and protecting, is the reality of wisdom. That same one, in the manner of the utterly nondual great bliss that is self-aware, is reality. That same one, through its divisions into small, middling, great, and the greatest of the great, is the nature of the joys and the rest. That same one, the abodes indicated by Sthavira and the rest, will be explained below.

The moon and the sun are the winds of the right and left nostrils. These are āli and kāli; moving from both by half-watches through day and night, they make sixteen transitions. As it is said:

At the times of those transitions,
by quarters fire and wind,
and likewise Mahendra and water,
move throughout from their maṇḍalas.
It moves above, below, in the middle, and beneath.
There are sixty-four daṇḍas;
for good and bad actions, half of that.
A watch is thirty-two nāḍikās,
four by day and four by night.
Therefore, through the purity of the watches,
the transition is the wheel of Dharma,
the daṇḍa is enjoyment and emanation,
the nāḍikā is the great purity.

Caṇḍālī is that which uproots the afflictions of existence. "Blazing" means spreading, up to "illuminating." "Āli" means a line. "At the navel" means at the center of the lotus of great bliss. "Burns" means renders devoid of essential nature. "The five Tathāgatas" are the five aggregates. "Locanā and the rest" are the elements, earth and the rest. When are they burned? When the self is burned, that is, when grasping at a self is burned. "Vajra" is Vajrasattva; his family is the abode. This is the chapter that brings realization of that, the first.

To show that this same one, in the form of mantra, is able to accomplish various aims, the chapter of mantras is taught. I shall explain the accumulations. To complete the accumulations of merit and wisdom, the torma mantra is taught, beginning with "oṃ." "akāro mukhaṃ" is the first door. "sarbadharmāṇāṃ" is "of all phenomena." From what? They are unborn from the very beginning. That which makes all phenomena unborn is A. Therefore the meaning is this: being intensely devoted to all phenomena as unborn, as the vajra of great bliss, one should give the torma in order to satisfy all sentient beings, who arise in dependent origination from A and are like a dream and an illusion. "oṃ āḥ hūṃ" means blessing by body, speech and mind themselves. It is "phaṭ" because it repels the malevolent māras and the rest. "svāhā" pacifies the mind into the suchness of body, speech and mind.

Although the families are limitless, because it gathers all the families, "all the Tathāgatas" are the seeds of Vairocana and the rest; it is the mantra of the place that truly reveals and generates them. Since he is the chief in the tantra, the mantra of that very deity should first be stated. It is taught as "oṃ deva picu vajra." Because, apart from the garland mantra of Hevajra, this is definite, "all" is said; and because it is the place that generates them, endowed with all the excellent mantra words, it is "words." Those that have oṃ at the beginning are "beginning with oṃ." "Interspersed with phaṭ" means inserted in between. Those that have svāhā at the end are "ending with svāhā." That which both begins with oṃ and ends with svāhā and is interspersed with phaṭ is what is so described. Which mantra is it? The three hūṃs. From what? From the recitation of the mantra, many words are taken up; and Heruka is also Akṣobhya himself; and he is invoked as one in body, speech and mind.

"City" means a place where people live. "Disturbing": the mantra by which a city is thrown into turmoil is the mantra for disturbing a city. On the petals of the eight directions in the emanation wheel, one sends out the first letters of the eight classes of consonants, with wisdom as the principal. By that very means one brings all the various beings under control and gathers them in through the union of great bliss. If one then recites the mantra as red in color, the city is thrown into turmoil.

"The yoginīs" means the yoginīs of the nonduality of wisdom and means, that is, Nairātmyā and the others. "Seeds" are the mantras, words that rouse reality; taken in their proper order, they are the fifteen vowels.

As for trailokyākṣepa: trailokyā is the three realms, and kṣepa is gathering them into the nature of nondual great bliss. So trailokyākṣepa truly invokes the Blessed One (the Buddha) as the one who gathers the three realms into the nature of nondual great bliss. jvala jvala means "Make the suchness nature of great bliss blaze in all things!" bhyo means "O Blessed One." kiṭi kiṭi means "Destroy, destroy all phenomena and the māras!" vajra means "O Hevajra." "That blesses body, speech and mind" means that it blesses body, speech and mind as vajra and brings one to that nondual great bliss; whatever mantra does so is given that name. The meaning is that after reciting these, one should meditate on the three vajras as indivisible.

In "the mantra for purifying the ground," the ground is the ground of the maṇḍala and the ground of yoga. Purifying means making it emptiness and then setting up protection with the vajra feet, so the meaning is "the mantra that protects it."

In the passage beginning "for paralysis," the meaning is "mantras for paralysis and the other rites." Here the yogin, joined with the yoga of Vajrā, first does extensive approach recitation of the mantra oṃ hūṃ svāhā. Then, gazing straight ahead without moving, with the breath calmed, he should practice on swaying grasses such as thatch grass. When they become still, he should paralyze the target in the same way.

Through the yoga of Gaurī and the yoga of Kurukullā, one recites the mantra oṃ aṃ svāhā. With a gaze directed to the left and the breath held, one should practice on lotus flowers and the like, and then bring the target under control in the same way. In the same manner, in other cases too, one should apply the practice by following whatever applications are suitable. As it is said:

Mantra recitation, and likewise fire offering,
meditation on reality, and worship:
through compassion the mantra is accomplished;
otherwise it turns to the greatest affliction.

As for āḥ phu: having recited this mantra, one makes an image of Ananta out of sandalwood. Above the navel it has a human form, and below it is made as a nāga's tail. It has four arms: the left hands hold a skull cup of nectar and a lotus, and the right hands hold a nectar fruit and a jewel. Above, it is adorned with seven hoods. One should bathe it with the five nectars, namely milk, curd, butter, honey and sugar, and then worship it with blue utpala flowers. "Spun by a dark girl" means that one should wind indigo-dyed thread around it eight times, toward the eight directions. "Fiercely" means with the sound of the mantra clearly voiced. "With an angry mind" means with a mind of great aversion. "In a solitary place" means where people will not hear the sound of the mantra recitation. The arjaka is a white shrub. "Just as its blossom" means: just as its blossom, touched by the sun, splits in an instant into many pieces, so the heads of the nāgas split.

The rite for splitting the clouds of a great rainstorm is taught in the passage "the clouds."

The practice of the vajra curved knife that is taught is a practice with stone fiber. One grinds Vairocana and the rest, the five nectars, with the stone fiber, together with the axe-cutter plant, and makes a pill the size of an eye. This is placed in the mouth of a Heruka image and the mantra is recited. One recites a hundred thousand times in a single sitting, for as long as it takes until the pill comes out of the image's own mouth by itself and is handed to the practitioner; then it is accomplished. If one draws a line with that stone fiber around the neck of a vase or water pot, the same line appears around the necks of the target and his retinue; this produces immediate conviction. If even such a person does not turn back from harming the Three Jewels and the like, he should be cut down. "Vajra curved knife": what is like a curved knife of vajra is a vajra curved knife. For example, a curved knife made of vajra surely cuts whatever it strikes; in the same way this too cuts off the enemy's head.

The practice with the vajra axe is taught in the passage "god." On the fifteenth day under the Puṣya asterism, one takes the seed of Brahmā, which is the seed of the palāśa tree. One mixes it in equal parts with the axe-cutter plant, kneads it with Akṣobhya, and from it makes an enemy-axe. At the time when the sun is seized by Rāhu, one presses it under the foot and recites the mantra. Having first recited ten million times and accomplished it during the day and night of the solar eclipse, one grinds it and makes a tilaka shaped like an axe. Then whatever god one bows to will shatter.

"Fire of rice husks": one digs eight finger-widths into the ground and places an arka leaf in it. One blows up a fire of rice husks on top of it and recites ten thousand times; the target is then struck with fever. If one takes the leaf out again and washes it with milk, he is freed from the fever.

"One who wishes to make him vomit liquor" means one who wishes to make him vomit liquor that has first been produced in his belly through mantra. At the target's navel one should meditate on a wind maṇḍala arising from yaṃ as it transforms. Above it one should meditate on liquor in the belly arising from maṃ as it transforms. One visualizes the target too with loosened hair, vomiting liquor, and recites the mantra one hundred and eight times: oṃ hevajra madya mudgiriya hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā. After that he will vomit the liquor.

Summoning, taught with reference to a yogin who has previously accomplished the mantra of Kurukullā, is in the passage "a woman."

The rite for holding fast the moon and the sun is taught in "the moon and the sun." First one should accomplish the mantra by reciting it seventy million times as the approach. Make a moon and a sun from ground śāli rice, cook them in steam, place them in a bowl into which vajra water has been poured to a depth of four finger-widths, and recite the mantra. This is the mantra for the sun. The mantra for the moon is oṃ vajra candra macala macala tiṣṭha tiṣṭha he vajrāya hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā. By reciting it ten thousand times, the moon will stand still. By taking it out of the urine, it is released.

The vajra star is taught in "oṃ nagra." First one should accomplish the mantra by reciting it a hundred thousand times and performing a fire offering of red lotuses. With the mantra oṃ nagrā nagrā kumārī keḍi byalocane svāhā, during the night watch on the thirteenth or the seventh day of the month, one should incant a girl's body and her two eyes one hundred and eight times, make offerings, and then bring down the prognostic sign.

Now the deities for the applications taught earlier, such as paralysis, are taught in the passage beginning "Vajrā." This is the explanation of the second chapter.

By the principle stated as
"Having the nature of mantra, of form, and of Dharma,
Yoga is held to be of three kinds,"
having set out the yoga that consists of Dharma and of mantra, he now teaches the yoga that consists of form. "The chapter on the deities": because they play in the manner of great bliss, the nature of all phenomena, they are deities; the deity itself is "deity." The text that brings this to understanding and establishes it is the "chapter."

"The yogin's generation of the awakening mind, through loving-kindness and the rest, accomplishes the aims of both self and others, so one should engage in it." This teaches, first, the meditation on the immeasurables:
"First one should meditate on loving-kindness."
One should cultivate the loving-kindness that brings immeasurable benefit and happiness to all sentient beings. Next is compassion, whose very essence is the intention to free them from suffering and its causes. The mind that rejoices in those who, being free of ignorance, fully enter the side of virtue is joy. Having no attachment toward those who have stilled existence and entered sameness is equanimity.

Alternatively, loving-kindness is sameness, whose nature is to bring joy and bliss. In karuṇā, "ka" is great bliss and "ru" is what gathers; compassion is striving for the nondual great bliss. Joy is delighting in the experience of the supreme innate in that very bliss. In that same state, to be equanimous in every way toward the afflictions, without examining them, is equanimity.

Next, "the awakening mind of emptiness": that all phenomena are beyond any reference point and devoid of essential nature is emptiness. That itself is awakening, the realization of the ultimate nature of all phenomena; it is the state of the innate Perfection of Wisdom. It also includes signlessness and the rest. In this emptiness there is no cause or effect whatsoever, anywhere. Thus, since all phenomena have the nature of being without cause and effect, there is no aspiration of anything for anything at all.

The four limbs are taught:
"In the second, gather the seed."
The seed is the cause of generation. "Gathering" means being gathered through generation; the sense is generation from hūṃ and the like. Alternatively, what is gathered by the seed is the four awakenings. Then comes the completion of the form, and after that the placing of the syllables.

After meditating on emptiness, "from raṃ" means: one visualizes a sun arising from raṃ, and on that sun a vajra of many colors arising from hūṃ, the nature of the five Tathāgatas; this is the crossed vajra (viśvavajra). "Fence" means: one visualizes four-cornered crossed vajras radiating forth, in the nature of blazing vajras, to the extent of the world-system and so on, reaching below the earth and above up to the abode of Brahmā. Above, too, there is a net of vajra nature. "Also" means that one should visualize the ground below as blazing with vajra nature.

Then, on a lotus that has arisen in its center, "a corpse": this is the nature of the seal of the selflessness of all phenomena. As the tantra itself says, "The wise one, embodying the Dharma." "The yogin, abiding upon it" means one should visualize oneself, abiding upon it, as Heruka himself. "At one's own heart" means at the heart of the corpse. "Embodying anger" means the purification of anger. This teaches the meditation of the solitary hero.

Generation through the five awakenings, surrounded by the eight yoginīs, is taught in "or else": after meditating on loving-kindness and the rest,
"Visualizing the Lord in the sky,
One should worship him with the eight goddesses."
"The deer-marked one," that is, the moon, is the awakening mind. "The sun vessel" is the lotus vessel filled with Ratnasambhava. The water is Akṣobhya. The medicine is the five lamps. The vajra is Vairocana. The flavor is honey. Through realizing all phenomena to be like echoes, the hand drum is beaten. Ḍombī embraces him about the neck and makes offering through the union of method and wisdom as nondual.

After that comes the awakening of emptiness. After that, in the center of the protection wheel, one should visualize an eight-petaled lotus surrounded by the eight charnel grounds, and on the center of the lotus a corpse. At its heart, "āli the moon, kāli the sun" means visualizing a moon from the vowel series and a sun disc from the consonant series. "In its center" means in the center of the vajra, and "that very seed" is hūṃ. "Sattva" means Vajrasattva, and this was spoken by the Tathāgata. On what basis? Because it is said, "whose nature is supreme joy." "Emanates" means from that very hūṃ. "The light of one's own body" means rays of light in the likeness of Hevajra's body, and the yoginīs as well. "Gather" means to draw in, that is, to absorb. "Into the heart" means into the hūṃ, and this is the wisdom that accomplishes all activities. After that the yogin, whose essence is the utterly pure dharmadhātu and who embodies anger, becomes Hevajra. Through the purity of method and wisdom he appears blue and red. As for "breathed out," "breathed out" means having breathed out upward, that is, a corpse. "Charnel ground" here is the dwelling place of hungry ghosts, corpses and the like.

Alternatively, the charnel ground is the body. "The Lord delights" refers to Vajrasattva. "With eight yoginīs" means with the eight beginning with Gaurī. "Delights" means that he rejoices in nondual union with his own wisdom consort. If one wonders how the body is a charnel ground, the text says, "or by the principle of the exhaled breath." Breath here means by this principle of the life-wind and its exertion.

That king of mind becomes four-armed. If one wonders how, the text says, "through the purification of the four māras." The color and nature are as stated before; the meaning is that black is Akṣobhya.

Alternatively, with color and shape, reddish-yellow hair bristling upward, adorned with armor and the five mudrās, seated in the posture of dalliance, surrounded by the charnel grounds and so forth, he is absorbed in coemergent union, nondual with the Blessed Lady Vajraśṛṅkhalā. "The corpse that is the very self of the three realms" means the Blessed One together with the Mother, pressing down the corpse, which is the whole of the three realms without remainder, whose nature is selflessness.

This concludes the explanation of the chapter on the deities, the third.

In "I shall explain the chapter on the empowerment of the deity," the deity is the one already described and the one yet to be explained. "Having first caused the wisdom circle to enter, one confers empowerment": it is empowerment because the water of wisdom washes away all the stains of the afflictions. Or else, "deity empowerment" means empowerment conferred by the deities. "The seed in one's own heart" means that on the sun at Hevajra's heart is one's own seed, which is hūṃ. "By the eight goddesses" means by Gaurī and the rest. "Having fully emanated them from one's own seed and made offerings, they are to be entreated" means making supplication. "And with vajra songs" refers to songs such as the one beginning kolla ireṭṭhi abola. The lord of one's own family is Akṣobhya. "One should abide in the body of the deity" means that, with an identity whose nature is illusion-like, nondual great bliss, one should firmly resolve upon all phenomena in this way and abide thus in every circumstance.

This concludes the explanation of the chapter on the empowerment of the deity, the fourth.

Now I shall explain the chapter on the reality of mantras and deities. Therefore, by means of that, the reality of all phenomena is taught. "There is no form" refers to the form sense base. "Seer" refers to the eye sense base, and to the nature of a self, a person, and so forth. If one wonders how they do not exist, it is said: form and the rest are without intrinsic nature by their essential nature; that is the meaning of "by essential nature." "There is no touch" and "there is no tangible object" negate earth and the other elements, or the form aggregate, or the ten sense bases. It shows that the four aggregates, that is, the mind sense base or the sense base of mental objects, lack essential nature. In the passage beginning "there is no mind," mind is the consciousness aggregate, or mentation. The mental factors are the aggregates of feeling, perception, and formations, or the phenomena that are the objects apprehended by mentation. "Also do not exist" means that they definitely do not exist. Negating these phenomena as unarisen from the very beginning serves to negate clinging to them as real entities. "There is no entity" means the absence of an entity with respect to a determinate place and time. For what is nonexistent in every way and entirely, there is likewise no application of negation at all.

Well then, if that is so, how and where is the meditation on reality to be examined? In response, the text says, "the mother and," and so forth. "Should be worshipped" means that one should bring about a peak of the joys and the rest. "By one who knows the union" means by knowing the nondual union of means and wisdom. "Always" means with the mind not wandering elsewhere for even an instant. That one should worship not only the mother but others as well is taught in the passage beginning "the dancer." The explanation of this condensed passage beginning "the dancer" will be given by the Blessed One himself in this very chapter. "Distinguishing" means becoming twofold, or coming to be known.

Well then, what does this attachment to reality accomplish? In response, the text says "the five mudrās"; by saying that, it means the mudrās of the five families. If one wonders what comes of that, it says, "as the cause of liberation." What the Tathāgata taught conventionally, non-abiding nirvāṇa, the state of coemergent great bliss, is liberation; and they are its cause. If one wonders what these mudrās are like, it is said that "vajra" is merely a name. Or else, by the five Tathāgatas, or by nondual wisdom. What is meant by "by this" is sealing; for that reason they are called mudrās. Or else, "by this" means by this sealing with the seal characterized by the five families, in examining reality; mudrā is by this very cause of the seal of reality.

Well then, is there nothing else besides the five families? In response, it says, "the families are of five kinds." "Supreme" means that these five families are supreme, since they comprise everything; the meaning is that the other families, and others still, also arise in dependence on these. If one wonders what those families are, the text gives the passage beginning "the vajra mudrā is Ḍombī." If one wonders whose families these are, it says "of the Tathāgatas," that is, of the buddhas Vairocana and the rest. "In brief" means by condensing.

If one wonders what the meaning of "Tathāgatas" is, it says "into suchness." Suchness is the very lack of intrinsic nature of all phenomena, as taught; they are thus-gone through the application of abiding without abiding in that way. "Glorious" means endowed with the accumulations of merit and wisdom. Having gone into suchness by that very accumulation of wisdom, they come back again into saṃsāra by the power of the accumulation of merit; the meaning is non-abiding nirvāṇa.

"In brief, they are of five kinds" means that Vajrasattva is included within the five Tathāgatas. "Moreover, they are condensed into three": if one wonders on what basis, the text says, "by the division of body, speech, and mind." The meaning is that although they are indivisible in the nature of Vajrasattva, they are threefold through the division of body and the rest.

If one wonders what the three are, the text says, "The five families are the five elements." This means the nature of the five aggregates, that is, earth, water, fire, wind, and space.

Having explained the meaning of mudrā earlier, it now states the meaning of "family." "Family is called number" means that this great bliss, through its divisions into joy and the rest, is designated "family."

Now comes a passage meant to lay the foundation for teaching the reality of mantras and deities. "There is no meditator" refers to the agent. "Nor is there meditation" refers to what is meditated on. Alternatively, as for "There is no meditator": if one asks from what and for what reason, it is that there is no entity whose nature is the five aggregates. What is stated here is the reasoning: for that reason there is also no mantra and no deity. The meaning is that entities do not arise.

Someone may think, "Well then, if there is no mantra and no deity, then since no phenomena exist at all, one's toil bears no fruit." To answer this, the text says "exist." Someone may then think, "But if they exist, that is to hold the eternalist view." To answer this, it says "without elaboration." Elaboration means the apprehended object and the like, imagined through delusion. Therefore they "abide" through emptiness, that is, in the state of innate suchness, by the union of non-abiding. For that very reason the text will later say:

Certainly all these entities
Are completely pure in suchness.

If one wonders what the names of those tathāgatas are, and which the six families are, the text says, "Vairocana, Akṣobhya," and so on. "Ratna" is Ratnasambhava. "Ārolika" is Amitābha. "Rolika" means dispute. Since there is no dispute in great passion, whose nature is supreme joy, it is called ārolika, "without dispute."

So that these may be understood by other names as well, the text says, "Brahmā, Viṣṇu," and so on. If one wonders why they are given these names, the text says, "Brahmā, by nirvāṇa," and so on. Nirvāṇa is the mirror-like wisdom. Therefore the buddha Vairocana, whose nature is nirvāṇa, is called Brahmā. The utterly pure dharmadhātu is the nature of all phenomena, so it pervades, that is, abides in all; therefore Akṣobhya is called Viṣṇu. With the nature of accomplished activity, he bestows on all these sentient beings virtue characterized by worldly and transworldly benefit, and so is Śiva, "the Peaceful": this is Amoghasiddhi. With the nature of the wisdom of equality, all phenomena are equal, and so "All" is the supreme jewel, Ratnasambhava. With the nature of discriminating wisdom, reality is discerned in its particulars through great bliss that is just like space, and so "Reality" is Amitābha. "Joy": by the state that is simply the nature of realizing innate great bliss, he is "the Fully Awakened," that is, Vajrasattva. Gathering all the families together, there are six tathāgatas. For that reason, "the Fully Awakened" means a buddha who is especially exalted.

Alternatively, wisdom itself is the family: that which is to be reasoned out and examined is the family. Within it there is neither the defined nor the definer, and so it is in emptiness. "There is no meditator" means that there is no reference point in the yogin. "Likewise there is no meditation" means that in that state there is no mantra of the mantra and no deity. "From the absence of elaboration" means that great bliss itself, the very nature of mind, is mantra. The deity too is the very nature of the play of great bliss; or else what arises from the body is the deity, and since it is beautiful in that way it is Vairocana. Nondual wisdom, by its very indivisibility, is Akṣobhya. Because it is fruitful, it is Amoghasiddhi. Because it is the cause from which the jewels of the qualities of all buddhas arise, it is the supreme jewel. Because non-elaboration has no object of dispute, it is Ārolika. Through the mode of gathering together each and every thing, the being is Vajrasattva; having the mode in which the three realms of existence are one, it is "that which abides."

Thinking that this same point should also be explained by the words "Brahmā" and so on, the text says "Brahmā." If one wonders on what grounds, it says, "Brahmā, by nirvāṇa": the utterly peaceful nondual wisdom, whose very nature is nirvāṇa, is Brahmā. Because it pervades all phenomena it is Viṣṇu. Because of its mode of peace everywhere it is Śiva. Because it abides as the self of all and as the nature of all phenomena it is "All." By being the supreme bliss, the great bliss without reference point, it is also "Reality." "Joy": since it is realized through the union of wisdom and means as nondual, its very nature of realization is "the Fully Awakened," that is, awakened in a special way. Or else, because it arises from the body, it is also called "deity."

It is also called the Blessed One (Bhagavān). If one wonders why, the text says "possessing bhaga." As for "bhaga":

Lordship and beautiful form,
Glory and fame and wisdom,
And then, because he comes to possess diligence,
He is therefore renowned as bhaga.

Alternatively, because it tears out by the root the māras of the afflictions and the rest, wisdom itself is called bhaga, "the vanquisher." As it is said:

Bhaga is said to mean destroying.
Because it vanquishes the māra of the afflictions,
And wisdom vanquishes the afflictions too,
Therefore wisdom is called the destroyer.

Then the text explains the meaning of the words "mother" and the rest, shown earlier. "Mother" means one who gives birth: that which gives birth to beings is the whole world. Or else "beings" means what is dependent, and "giving birth" means moving. Or else "giving birth to beings" is the great bliss that gives birth to beings; because it gives birth to beings, wisdom itself is called mother. She herself is the sister, because she distinguishes and reveals joy and the rest. She herself is the washerwoman, because through great passion she purifies what has the nature of passion. She herself is the daughter, because she draws forth the qualities of all buddhas. She herself is also the dancer, because she dances as the nature of all phenomena, or because she moves through the union of non-abiding.

Because it is not an object that can be touched by any of the afflictions arising from that conceptual thought, it is untouchable; that is why it is called the outcaste woman.

Now the text teaches that all the activities of body, speech and mind of the yogins of glorious Heruka, whose nature is such nondual great bliss, have the nature of mudrā, mantra and meditative absorption, and that its nature, in its entirety, is manifold. It says: "Utterance is known as mantra recitation." It teaches yet another line of reasoning: "Because one utters the vowels and consonants." There is no utterance apart from the vowels and consonants, and each of them, too, has the nature of mantra. As it is said: "The mantras of the vowels and consonants, and whatever arises from them." And again it is said: "There is definitely no mantra at all; it abides because there is accomplishment."

What arises from pressing is the maṇḍala drawn by it; by pressing, it turns back those who are fixated on drawing maṇḍalas. Moving the hands is an opening-out by dancing in various ways; it is a particular kind of hand movement. "The mudrā of all of these is the mudrā": this teaches the suchness that is the cause of such things. This is the supreme mudrā, which appears distinctly by its very nature of directly seeing great bliss and the deity.

"Whatever the mind thinks of there": whatever is thought of through that nondual union is itself meditative concentration. If one asks why, it says: "Because it is contemplated, it is meditative concentration." Because it thinks, it is thought; it contemplates in the manner of suchness, and that itself is meditative concentration. For this yogin of suchness, whatever he focuses on and thinks of appears as that nondual great bliss, and so becomes meditative concentration. There is nothing to be thought of apart from the aggregates, elements and sense sources. And since these are not divided, whether as single or as many, all are completely pure; there is no remainder elsewhere. As it is said:

Wherever the mind may think,
one should know to join it to that very thing.
Wherever beings move,
it is because they are of its nature.

"For one who abides in the state of Heruka,
as many movements of the limbs as there are,
and all the collections of utterances,
so many are mantras and mudrās."

"Just as one obtains the bliss of the father": the father is the guru, and this refers to whatever pith instruction he possesses. The bliss is the mere instant of innate bliss. Obtaining means that by abiding in that bliss for a long time, the yogin comes to possess qualities such as the supreme, and practices without depending on his own mudrā.

If one wonders how this is so, the text says "by which, death." "By which" means: when great bliss has arisen, conceptual thought and the net of afflictions born from its power, such as the grasped object, are destroyed, and one comes to enjoy that very innate bliss oneself. That very one is able to realize fully such bliss. It is called "the meditative concentration without entities," mere self-awareness, suchness brought to its culmination; the word "meditative concentration" here expresses ultimate meditative concentration. Therefore there is no object of thought; it is precisely that which has suchness as its object.

This concludes the explanation of the fifth chapter, the chapter on suchness.

Next is taught the conduct of yogins who have attained heat through the yoga of suchness, beginning "Then, another." "Then" means: other than the explanation of suchness for the yogin of suchness, the conduct of each of them is to be undertaken. "Gone beyond" means the supreme of all conduct; the meaning is that this is the foremost, possessing the most excellent conduct. "By which one goes to the end of accomplishment": "by which" means by the conduct that is about to be explained. "The end of accomplishment" (siddhānta) is the treatise on the conduct of yogins. "Goes" means that it should be explained in this way to those who seek it. If one wonders why, it says: "For the sake of the accomplishment of Hevajra." Hevajra is the sign of that accomplishment; one who has fully understood what was taught practices the conduct.

Now, with regard to his very kingdom of Dharma, I shall explain for the moment all the ornaments of the regent. "By the meditator" means: therefore, by the yogin who meditates on nondual suchness, the eldest son of the Victor. "Should be worn" means that, in particular, the earrings and other ornaments of the five buddha families should be worn. "Half of ten" means the five nectars.

"Through the union of Heruka": whatever joins the cause of Heruka and so forth into the nature of emptiness and so forth, that is the union of Heruka. On "toward its five colors": the five are the brahmins and the rest, and the vajra family and the rest, down to the very last; toward them one practices with equality, begging, touching and the other practices. Or else it refers to the five aggregates as having the nature of the five buddhas. Their equality is taught there: "Fully endowed with the five colors." Since the five colors are gathered into suchness, the one color is nonduality. To consider means to think. If one asks on what grounds, it says "being of various colors"; "because" refers to the ultimate. "Distinctions should not be imagined" means that one should not take them as objects.

Now, so that one may find what is fitting for carrying out the conduct, the text teaches places that suit the meditative concentration of the yogin's conduct, beginning "a single tree." This means a preeminent tree blessed by yakṣas and the like, the abodes of mātṛkās such as Vaiṣṇavī, the abodes of yoginīs, fields and the like. Wilderness means places hard to traverse and long roads. Or else, the body itself, endowed with limbs such as feet and hands, is the single, unique tree, for it bestows the great fruit of great bliss. When the breath has gone out of it, it is the charnel ground. The mātṛkā is the house of the yoginīs whose nature is the channels and so forth; or, since a mother gives birth, for Hevajra himself wisdom is the house, that is, the abode. Night is the very cessation of attachment and the like through wisdom. Solitude is freedom from the conceptions of sattva and the rest. The outskirts are the paths within the cakras.

"Having gained a little warmth" means that one should practice the conduct once one has attained a little yogic power, and not otherwise. This is what the text itself will go on to explain with the words "in a terrifying form." "If one wishes" shows that the conduct is not something that must be performed in every case. Nevertheless, for those who wish to realize accomplishment in this very life, if they so wish, they should then practice the conduct. If one asks whether it is done all alone, the answer is no, as the text states by explaining "with her." Just as it says, it speaks of "a beautiful woman" and so on:

Having taken a human daughter,
one should teach her the Dharma.
She is called the Great Seal;
accomplishment comes together with her.

The "family of the fortunate deity" is the family of one's chosen deity. The intent of the yogin in kurukulle and so on is stated: "Having fully released the seed of awakening." This means by the stage of generating the mind of awakening, and by the stages of conferring the secret empowerment and so on. "Then, supreme, endowed with the vajra" means that one should sing songs endowed with vajra words, not village songs:

One should also sing songs; wisdom
and the yoginī are pleased by them.
Likewise in other ways as well,
one who knows yoga should act thus.

Its purification is stated:

With the pride of Hevajra,
the song is the purity of mantra,
the dance is the nature of meditation.

As for the movements of hands and feet, the text itself states them with "with vajra feet" and "the yoga purified of aversion."

That the samaya is to be obtained in every case is stated: "One should also eat the medicine," which means Vairocana. "Water" means vajra water. "A thief's hair" means that one should make a crown from the hair of a thief who has been pierced by a weapon or killed by strangling. "From that, too, hūṃ arises" means made into bone ornaments, or else "one should join it with Hevajra," which means one should join it through meditation. "With a cord of hair wound twice" means one should tie a girdle of thief's hair wound around twice. The purification of the twofold winding is stated: "by the nature of wisdom and means." "Ash" means on all the limbs. "The hair purifier" means adorned with a sacred thread of thief's hair. "Vajra skull" is Hevajra, that is, by his conduct. "Shame" is restraint. "Activity" is action. "Even oneself" means uprooting the view of a self. "One should give the body as a gift" means in every circumstance, for Vajrayoginī and the others and for the sake of all sentient beings.

"If one examines the fortunate and the unfortunate" means that, being endowed with the practice of the one and the many, one does not apprehend the gift, the giver and so on. Because in the ultimate there is no giving, the yogin does not give; the meaning is that he does not give the body. "Even one who has attained accomplishment" is said in order to show that yogins must certainly bow to the guru, for otherwise they fall away from all accomplishments and drop into hell. That is the phrase "even one who has attained accomplishment," and also "for the sake of avoiding Avīci." Avīci is the great hell, and the meaning is "in order to avoid it."

Activities of body and so on, done without fail, are the training. Entering the maṇḍala and so on is the empowerment. "Shame" means having a sense of propriety, not engaging in activities that conform to one's own caste. "By the essence of all things": all things are the five aggregates. Their essence is the unsurpassed suchness, and in that way it is emptiness. Compassion is stated: "great compassion." Therefore the meaning is that one abides in a body in which emptiness and compassion are inseparable. "One should not fear it" means that when one sees a cause of fear, one should not let the mind become disheartened, since one apprehends it as nondual with oneself. "In the form of a lion" means with the conduct of a lion: acting fearlessly in every way, through that very nature. Compassion is great bliss. "Drink" means after milking the nondual Avadhūtī. "Yogic drink" means that the yogin delights in that drink. Being endowed with the delight it produces, one should not become attached to or delight in the taste of other drinks.

This is the explanation of the chapter on conduct, the sixth.

Now, when a yogin of conduct sees another yogin, or Vajrayoginīs and others abiding in his own samaya, those without the fortune must be identified. Therefore, in order to teach the samaya mudrā that is the means of identifying them, it says: "Then the chapter on signs." The mudrā by which those without the fortune are identified is the sign, and the definitive word that makes it clear is "the chapter on signs." If one wonders why this is done, it is answered with "by merely recognizing which." "Brother" is vajra brother. "Sister" is the vajra sister abiding in one's own samaya. "Without doubt" means without hesitation. "One finger" means "Have you come well?" "Should be shown" means held up. "One should show the ring finger" means one should look with one's own samaya, or show it with another finger. "patisa" means placing the two middle fingers crosswise on the forehead. "Three-pointed" means extending three middle fingers. "The edge of the border" is the place above the center of the forehead. "Frown" means knitting the brow. "One should untie the topknot" means untying the topknot. "Should be done" means what the yogins should say in return. "Back" means turning the back. "Pholong" means showing the tossing upward of balls of leather and the like, used for play with the queen. By the distinction between the mudrā shown first and the reply mudrā shown second, the samaya is to be known, that is, made clear. "Speaks" means that if one sees someone abiding in one's own samaya, one speaks and makes oneself known. "Thus seeing, and if showing a garland" means showing the hand. "Gathering" means saying, "We and this circle."

In response to that, throwing a garland of flowers as the answering sign shows it in this way. "Abide by the pledge" means doing as was said. How one should abide by one's own pledge is this: "Readily obedient, I shall go there." "Outside" means to a charnel ground and the like. "To the gathering" means to the circle. "The domain of the excellent" means to excellent places such as the fields, the sites and so on. "Then they remain" means that they gather there: yogins and yoginīs gather. Other places of gathering, such as the sites, are taught in the passage beginning "The sites and the secondary sites." "These are the twelve grounds" refers to the sites and so on. As for these: here the lords are the buddhas and bodhisattvas, and these places are none other than the grounds such as the Very Joyful. The lord of the ten grounds is the bodhisattva; the protector is the buddha.

The locations of the sites and the rest are taught in the passage beginning "The site is said to be Jālandhara." These sites are thirty-two in number: the head, the crown and so on. The yoginīs are Abhedyā and the others; condensed, and condensed further, they are gathered into the twelve grounds.

The days on which the yoginīs gather at the sites are then taught. What is called "the fortnight of the pretas" is the dark fortnight. "The banner" means one who has been hanged. "One killed by a weapon" means one who died fighting. "Seven times" means one born seven times. Since one has become free of wrongdoing, one kills by the rite of killing. If one wonders why, then, it is not to be eaten, the text says, "One should generate compassion": this secret rite is not for the sake of eating, but in order to liberate, through compassion, the beings of the lower realms. Or else, one should slay all the things that have gathered there.

If one thinks that in that case, through emptiness alone, it would become like the awakening of the śrāvakas, the text says, "One should generate compassion." The meaning is that if one makes aspirations with compassion, there will be no fault in the emptiness endowed with great bliss. "Without compassion there is no accomplishment" means that one will not attain the siddhi of the Great Seal (Mahāmudrā). Therefore one should first generate compassion.

If one wonders, "Then, if the rite of killing is not for the sake of eating, what is it for?", the text says, "To bring in the malicious": the malicious are brought into the teaching. "To bring in" means to cause them to enter fully; it is for that purpose. "By the rite" means by the rite of separating the union; "principal" means by the very rite whose purpose is to bring them in as primary. The days taught earlier for the sake of gathering are, moreover, day and night.

Their purification is taught in "In that too, it should be understood thus." "There is nothing that may not be done" means that what is reviled in the world, since it does not conflict with compassion, is done without sin, with a virtuous mind. "Just as oneself" means through emptiness and compassion; in the same way one should regard sentient beings. If one wonders how oneself is to be regarded, the text says, "Likewise oneself, self and other": oneself too is to be treated in the same way, for as oneself is, so others are, and not otherwise. Having rightly contemplated phenomena in this nondual way, "one should undertake eating, drinking and the like," that is, with a mind of inseparability.

"The state of glorious Heruka" is the state to be known; the meaning is abiding in the sense of glorious Heruka. One may wonder: what is the meaning of Heruka, abiding in which one's acts of body and speech have the nature of mudrā and mantra? In answer the text says, "śrī is nondual wisdom." he is he; its meaning is the emptiness of cause and the rest: through this emptiness of cause and result, of grasped object and grasping subject and so on, there is signlessness. "ru is free from aggregation": the aggregation is earth and the rest, present everywhere. Being free of that, and so free of wishing for it, wishlessness is the meaning of ru. ka means emptiness: "not abiding anywhere" means that everything whatsoever is empty; the meaning is that there is no practice of abiding in anything at all. This is the state of glorious Heruka.

"Of whatever beings, wherever they are" refers to the flesh of the hanged man and the others at the gathering and the like. "By the wise" means by the yogins and yoginīs; the wise are those skilled in yoga. "Those very beings" means that beings in other births come under the power of the yogins and yoginīs: they become obedient and delight in the practice. If one asks, "Is it to be eaten just as it is?", no: the text says, "by the yogin of vajra and skull cup," that is, through the yoga of glorious Heruka taught above. Or else, "vajra" means the seed syllables of the tathāgatas, bhrūṃ and the rest. kapāla is the lotus vessel arisen from a. "By the yogin" means that, having first performed the yoga of wind and fire below, one makes it one with the vajra nectar through blazing and boiling. Or else, the conceptualized aggregates of beings are the flesh; through these, one enjoys them in a way that brings beings and their aggregates under control.

This concludes the explanation of the seventh chapter, establishing the signs, the sites and so on.

Now, in explaining the wheel of the channels, the text teaches meditation on the fifteen yoginīs, divided into the generation and completion stages, in the passage "Then, the circle of the yoginīs." The yoginīs, Nairātmyā and the others, gathered in the maṇḍala, or gathered in the lotus in the manner of wisdom, are the circle of the yoginīs; I shall explain that. "Imagine the bhaga in the element of space" means that after meditating on emptiness one imagines the source of phenomena (dharmodaya), the purity of space. Within it, "What is to be meditated in the center is, as appropriate, preceded by the circle" means that meditation on the maṇḍalas is done first; the meaning is that the deities are to be meditated in the order of their arising. "As appropriate" means in the order of meditating on earth and the rest. Explaining that, "circle" means maṇḍala. "What falls" is earth, meaning the earth maṇḍala. "Water first" means one should meditate on the water maṇḍala before the fire maṇḍala. Likewise fire comes before wind, and then the great wind. These maṇḍalas, as appropriate, are the goddesses: the purity of Locanā and the rest. And the meditator at that time is the yogin.

"As they arise" means that they abide as that very nature, in the order in which the maṇḍalas arise. "Arisen from the source of phenomena" refers to the circle of the maṇḍala that arises from the transformation of the four disks in the center of the source of phenomena. "Two-tiered" means a two-storied palace. "By the hub it is one": because it is indicated by the hub, "the lotus" means the central lotus. "By the triangle": as indicated by the source of phenomena, this is the other tier, the outer one, that is, the square enclosure of the outer Gaurī and the rest. "In its center" means in the center of the maṇḍala.

On that, at the heart of a corpse, is a moon, arising from the transformation of the āli placed upon it. On the moon are the fifteen seeds, ā and the rest. "Afterward it is pressed by the sun" means that after that it is pressed down by the disk of the sun, which arises from the transformation of the kāli. "The two" are the sun and the moon. Wisdom and means are united, and "union" is the great bliss that arises from the joining of wisdom and means. The text itself says that āli abides as wisdom, in the form of the moon, and in the form of kāli is the sun.

"In their midst, Gaurī and the rest": this is because ā and the rest, their mantras, are inseparable from the mantra deities. "The seeds" are the fifteen vowel letters, ā and the rest. "Emblem" is in the singular because the emblem is one and the same, the curved knife. "All become one" means that, having first emanated and gathered back, the moon, the emblem, the seed and the sun become blended into a single nature. "Accomplishing" is the wisdom of accomplishing activities. "Form" is the bodies of the yoginīs. "Complete" means born. "Pure" means utterly pure, the purity of the sphere of reality (dharmadhātu).

"Āli and kāli joined together" refers to the very union of moon and sun, which come from what has become āli and kāli in the form of means and wisdom. "As the seat of Vajrasattva": this is the cause of the generation. The seed of inseparability is the very nature of the inseparability of wisdom and means, that is, Vajrasattva. As for its "mass arisen from the syllable": it is a syllable because it does not change, owing to the nondual union of wisdom and means. The mass that arises from it is of the nature of great bliss.

"Neither hūṃ nor phaṭ is wanted" means that neither the vajra arising from hūṃ nor the fixing done with phaṭ is accepted. "The form of the being" means this: from the gathering into one of the forms of the awakening mind, which became the moon and the rest, the forms that generate the being, there comes "the lord of the maṇḍala," that is, the principal goddess Nairātmyā. The lord of the maṇḍala is of a mode nondual with the chief of the maṇḍala. "Faces, emblems and so on accord with the son" means that, just as the son, the resultant Vajrasattva, has faces, emblems and so on, so too does she. Her being "like a moon gem" means that she is white. Alternatively, the moon is the awakening mind, and the gem is the jewel; the innate that is generated by what the jewel bestows, which is like a radiance, is the very light of the moon gem.

When all has thus been completed, "Just as the yoginī Nairātmyā" refers to the five awakenings. Likewise the text itself teaches Gaurī and the rest as having the essential nature of wisdom and means. If one wonders by which wisdom and means, the text says "Wisdom is āli" and so on. "By the division of moon and sun" means by distinguishing them correctly, one by one. From that very thing they become Gaurī and the rest. "By the division of the syllables" means by their respective divisions. "The others, individually" means that these, Nairātmyā and the rest, also have the emblem in common.

"Two-tiered" was explained earlier. If one wonders on which tier, and how many yoginīs, and which ones, are placed there, the text says "the inner tier" and so on. "Of Indra" means on the petal in Indra's direction, the east. She who is endowed with the station below is the Lower One: her face turned downward, she has the form of saṃsāra and the nature of compassion. She who is endowed with the station above is the Upper One, Khecarī, of the nature of nirvāṇa and of utter emptiness. In their center is the yoginī Nairātmyā, whose nature is existence and peace; she is nonabiding nirvāṇa, the very body in which emptiness and compassion are inseparable.

"Holding a curved knife and skull cup in hand" refers to the curved knife and the skull cup. "Holding" means being joined. "In hand" means in the hand, that is, "by these." The text itself explains, "One hand is occupied with a skull cup": those whose one hand is occupied by a skull cup are what is meant by "one hand occupied with a skull cup."

"The six faults, pride and the rest,
Are cut off by the curved knife; therefore it is held."
Pride, ignorance, doubt, attachment, anger and view are the six root afflictions, pride and the rest, together with their negative propensities. Because the knife cuts these off, the hands of all of them hold curved knives.

On "The lotus vessel of the head
Of the conceptual thought of thing and no-thing":
because the conceptual thought of things and of the absence of things has been torn out at the root, it is a lotus vessel, that is, a skull cup. Moreover, the text says, "It is filled with the blood of the four māras": the blood is that of the four māras. "Drinking it is the cause of accomplishment" is to be understood by the order just taught.

Now, in order to remove the fault of clinging to color with regard to the meaning of sameness, the meditation of the six limbs is taught, in the passage beginning "First." "The end of the joy of cessation is likewise" means that the end of the joy of cessation is the innate; since that is nothing but the innate at the end of the joy of cessation, it should therefore be meditated upon. That is the meaning. Alternatively, the especially exalted joy is the special joy, and what lies at its end is the innate; or else the very words "the end of the joy of cessation" teach the innate, and "that innate joy" is the remainder of the phrase, to be supplied. Or again, "likewise" means that the others too should be meditated upon as before.

"The generation stage itself also": there are two stages, according to the twofold division into relative truth and ultimate truth. "The sphere of space": the purified element of space, the lotuses themselves, are the sphere of space. In that, what is called the bhaga is the awakening mind, bodhicitta. "To be meditated on in its center" means that one should meditate on the characteristic of the union of method and wisdom. The bliss that arises from that is the cakra, that is, the deities gathered and assembled together.

"Self-aware as is fitting": that too is bliss. By the very power of reasoning, self-awareness is the absence of entities such as a self and a person. "The awakening mind is the deity" means the deity whose characteristic is the inseparability of emptiness and compassion, the deity that is selflessness (Nairātmyā). "Just as substances come to arise" means that it is by the characteristic of not discarding what arises from that; the sense is "the stage of arising."

For that reason it is said that, through the division of the awakening mind into relative and ultimate, the innate is also of two kinds. "Therefore the innate is of two kinds" means "through the two kinds of innate." In order that this be realized, it is said: "For now, the queen." That is, since method and wisdom are each divided into two, the sense is that there are four kinds. For that very reason, joy also: "By its divisions it is fourfold." Here, if one wonders what "that very" refers to: "Because the innate is fourfold." The innate itself follows the four joys, through divisions such as lesser and middling.

"From the side of the completion stage" means that the innate does not follow from the generation stage. That is its definite place.

"The first joy is the hero": the hero is Hevajra; he is of the nature of joy and of the nature of bliss. Exceeding that is wisdom; the nature of joy itself is supreme joy. The joy that is the extreme joy arising from these, gathering everything without remainder, is the nature of the innate. "Through that bliss-means, all is known": having become the means of such bliss, one knows all and attains omniscience. Alternatively, "the joy of extreme joy" is the third joy, and "without remainder" means pervading the whole body. Alternatively, "Through that bliss-means, all is known" is the fourth, the means of that very third joy. "All is known" means whatever is meant by the nature of omniscience, gathering all, the indivisible nature of Vajrasattva. Or else "all" is the nature of the five aggregates, and because the Tathāgatas gather them, it is the very nature of emptiness. "Knowing," too: since the great bliss, the nature of the innate, is self-awareness, it is therefore "knowing all."

Now, the definite place of the innate on the side of the generation stage is stated elsewhere, in its true sense, as: "Joy is but a little bliss." "The innate, the remainder of joy": the remainder, that is, the innate joy itself, singled out. Therefore one should meditate on it pervasively in the completion stage. To make this clear: "It is held that the first is to be known." "Supreme joy is kept within saṃsāra": this is bliss that has an object. "Nirvāṇa is the joy of cessation": the joy of cessation, whose nature is freedom from attachment. "The Middle Way is mere joy": this means that the middle of mere joy is not some superior Middle Way; or else, it is the mere joy in the middle between supreme joy and the joy of cessation, the mere innate bliss. The sense is the nature of the nirvāṇa that abides neither in saṃsāra nor in nirvāṇa. "By that very innate, this is abandoned": the cessation of conceptual thought and the like is the abandoning. As it is said:

Attachment itself and freedom from attachment,
Being mingled, are free of concepts;
Likewise attachment and non-attachment
Become one taste in a single instant.

Therefore, in every respect, even through the generation stage with the action seal (karmamudrā), the innate itself is held to be principal. It is this that should be meditated on, accomplished, and examined.

Wondering by what means it is found, it is said: "The innate does not come from another's explanation." It does not come from explanation by others such as the guru, since it cannot be expressed in words. "Nowhere" means that it is not found through teaching and the like, since it cannot be shown. If one thinks, "In that case, pleasing the guru, listening, training and the like are pointless," the answer is: it comes to be known by oneself; it is found by oneself. If one then thinks, "Even so, it would be found by anyone at all," it is said: "Through merit, and having pleased the guru, through the lineage of means," that is, through two causes. If one wonders, "Even so, how does one come to know it by oneself?", the meaning of what is said is this: first one generates the accumulation of merit by pleasing the guru and so on; then, having come to know it through the succession of the guru lineage, through permission and diligence the innate arises from oneself. It is not found through merely hearing the teaching, through merely generating merit, or without a cause.

Wondering how one is to carry out what is taught by the method of the guru lineage, it is said: "The lesser, the middling and the excellent." To explain the meaning of this, it is said: "The lesser, the meaning of the subtle place." The lesser are the most subtle particles, wisdom, and the like. The excellent are mountains, oceans, and the like. The middling are pots, cloth, and the like. Stable and moving are whatever things there are. Those are spoken of by this. "All those" means all these. "Equally" means they are to be viewed so. "Selflessness" means that these are without self. This shows that they are not to be viewed as a self. Stating it as equality: "They should be made the same." These that are to be made equal are also not to be made into entities; or else, these are made so through the innate.

This explains "one should meditate on reality." "Equal taste": the meaning of that very equal taste is stated by "equal and the same." Everything being the same by the nature of suchness is the taste of that equality. The wheel is the wheel of yoginīs, and this is said in summary: "by meditating as one taste." Or else, it is the self of all. Or else, it will be explained as "or having abandoned all." Having said "having abandoned all," it then says "the self of all, arisen from me." Thus, by practicing after knowing the sequence of the lineage of gurus, one attains accomplishment, and the innate draws near.

"Perfectly in equipoise" states that very point. As for "eating and drinking" and "uninterrupted, from that it comes about": because of this, the training is to be like that. "Uninterrupted" means everywhere, in the manner of the innate, without the continuity of any state being broken, that is, continually, by the nature of the innate. "From that it comes about" means it is attained.

"Even though all beings meditate": at first, meditating on all phenomena with analyzing wisdom is to make them an object of apprehension. After that, since no entity is seen, there is nothing to meditate on. Therefore "there is nothing to be meditated on," because all phenomena are fully known as the innate suchness, means that since there is no entity, there is nothing to apprehend and nothing to meditate on.

The same is stated: "the stable and the moving," and "grass, shrubs, vines and so on," that is, plants and the like, and humans and the like, when these are meditated on supremely in the manner of reality. "Having the nature of the entity of the self" means having the nature of one's own entity, that is, having its very form. Just as the self is not apprehended in any way at all, anywhere, as the ultimate of all, so too these are likewise; or else they are also seen as the suchness of the form of great bliss, the yogin's own form. "They are one" means in the manner of reality. Their "other" means there is nothing beyond.

When one wonders why that is their nature: "self-awareness is great bliss"; the meaning is that one should meditate in such a manner. When one wonders what comes of that, it says: "from self-awareness comes accomplishment," that is, the accomplishment of the Great Seal (mahāmudrā). "By meditating on self-awareness" means that where there is no self-awareness, there is ultimately no meditation; it is merely a designation for an object that is nondual. "From the nature of self-awareness" means that even actions done by the body and so forth are, for self-aware yogins, endowed with the nature of great bliss.

If that is so, one may wonder how, from its virtuous and nonvirtuous karma and from its afflictions, suffering and so forth come about, each different from the other. To this it says: "from dispute karma is born." By the power of beginningless ignorance, the dispute of conceptual thought and the like, which does not fully know the nature, conflicts with great bliss, self-awareness. But when one wonders how this can be while one is endowed with that very great bliss, it says: "one robs oneself." That is, by realizing that bliss through conditions such as the instructions of one's own guru, karma, afflictions and the like are entirely stolen away. Without that realization, one is made to wander in saṃsāra by one's own karma and the like, and not by another, such as the Lord (Īśvara).

Through realization one is oneself the king, and so he is called "king." "Lord" too means endowed with power by being supreme over all. When one wonders what his lordship is like, it says "attachment, aversion." Attachment and the rest, when purified, are the tathāgatas Amitābha and the rest, and these are spoken of with the words "attachment" and so on. They too abide in the state of the Great Vajradhara; in that same manner, the yogin who abides in the state of the innate is Vajrasattva. "Not equal even to a sixteenth part" means they do not measure up; therefore he is the chief and lord even of the tathāgatas.

"The wisdom born from the source of phenomena": that from which the nature of phenomena arises is the source of phenomena (dharmodaya). Whatever wisdom arises from it is equal to space, the nature of suchness. "Endowed with means" means endowed with objectless compassion, great bliss; the meaning is the awakening mind whose very being is emptiness and compassion. "The three worlds are born from that" means that from that ultimate awakening mind the three realms of existence too are born. When one wonders how, it says "from the nature of wisdom and means." Being born from it means having the essence of a cause that accords with it; ultimately it is of an unborn nature. Therefore all these are of the nature of the bodhisattva, and it teaches that the yogin should show this so that its arising does not conflict in the slightest.

In showing that it is the very nature of means and wisdom, it says: "the substance is the Blessed One," meaning means that has an object of apprehension. Its bliss, having the nature of the innate, is the Beloved, that is, objectless wisdom, having the nature of suchness. "This, free of the union of one and many" means the innate nature called the awakening mind, Vajradhara. "Instant" means in the instant of the innate and the rest. "Supreme" means uncommon. "Joy" means great bliss. "These stages of blessing" means the wisdom of blessing in which the innate arises, together with its nature; these stages, together with the nature of Vajradhara, are the lord of Vajrasattva's blessing.

"The nature of omniscient wisdom" means whatever is the wisdom of omniscience, together with its nature; it is the nature not other than innate self-awareness and supreme great bliss. "In an instant all disputes are abandoned" means that in the instant the innate arises, everything becomes that and its very nature, and so there is no dispute in the manner of duality.

All disputes belong to the domain of duality. The meaning is: in nonduality, who would dispute with whom, and by what means? If one wonders what that dispute is, it is said: "Intrinsic nature is known through true feeling." The same text says: "In the heavens, in the human realm, and beneath the earth as well."

On "All the tenets of the Vedas": the tenets are what the followers of the Vedas propound. The accomplishment meant here is not the accomplishment of the Great Seal (Mahāmudrā). "Existence" is saṃsāra. Whatever is the thorough knowledge of the real through purification, that is accomplishment; the meaning is that this is not it. If one wonders what it is in that case, it is said: "Again, in other births."

"As the stream of a river flows, and as the stream of a burning lamp, elsewhere": for example, a burning lamp set by the flowing stream of a river dispels the darkness, yet it does not stay, and so it serves no purpose. In the same way, if one has understood the meaning of Hevajra and then toils elsewhere, the fruit does not stay, and that very toil is pointless. Alternatively: wherever the stream of a river flows, its current is unbroken, and the stream of a burning lamp is self-luminous; in the same way one should always abide in union with reality. This is said of yogins. Or again: just as, when the stream of a river flows, its current is unbroken, so the yogin too abides in self-luminosity, again like the stream of a burning lamp. This should be understood from the oral instruction granted by the guru.

The explanation of the chapter on the circle of yoginīs, the eighth.

Now the text teaches purification by dividing it into supreme and not supreme: "supreme." Here, supreme purification is suchness, and the ordinary one is the nature of the deities. This is taught in the words "Certainly all these things." "The deities are taught" means "I shall explain." The five aggregates are form and the rest. The six faculties are the inner sense sources. The six sense sources are the outer sense sources. "Pure by their essential nature" means that this nature is made one whose very self is suchness. "All are pure" is said of all the things mentioned above.

If, divided into aggregates, sense sources and elements, these are pure by their essential nature, what is purification for? To this the text says: "Obscured by ignorance and affliction, they are to be purified." Although they are pure by nature, they are obscured by the adventitious affliction of not truly knowing the nature of the aggregates and the rest. Therefore one applies oneself by way of realizing that nature.

"Through self-awareness, the knowing of oneself": suchness here is not the same as in the way of the Perfections. Rather, it is the mode of awakening perfected in great bliss, self-awareness that is supreme bliss; self-awareness is great bliss itself. Because it is exalted above those others, it is not something within the domain of the eye and the other senses. "The pure thing" is the pure nature.

"Since beings are actual buddhas" means: they have the nature of the five Tathāgatas as divided, and the nature of Vajrasattva, whose very self is undivided suchness.

On "Form grasped by the eye": since form is what is grasped, it is the grasped; and the eye is the one who grasps, or the instrument. "By this" means "it grasps." Likewise with sound and the ear and the rest. On "known as what is tasted by the tongue": because it is tasted, it is the tasted, that is, flavor. Knowing is so called because it makes known. "Substance" is the tangible, that is, earth and the rest.

If these things are thus impure, what is to be gained by abandoning them and purifying them? To this the text says: "The yogin should rely on them." If one asks on what grounds, the text says: "For which reason they are to be relied upon." If one thinks that by relying on them one would remain in saṃsāra, the text says: "Having made them free of poison through purification." For example, if one partakes of poison without knowing its reality, it becomes harmful. But if one knows the reality of that very thing, it is made free of poison; and when eaten by one who knows its reality, not only is it harmless, it even destroys poison previously taken from elsewhere. In the same way, if one relies, without purification, on sense objects, which are poisonous, they give rise to faults such as attachment. Relying on the former kind neither produces anything nor destroys anything. But when one relies on the other, made free of poison through the purification of suchness and so on, then it both produces good qualities and destroys faults. Therefore one should rely on them diligently.

Now the purification is taught by division into the aggregates and the rest: "The aggregate of form," and so on. Now the division of the directions not taught earlier is given: "In the northeast, Pukkasī," and so on.

The purification of Vajrasattva is taught: "The arms." "The feet" means with the left and the right. The four legs are the purification of victory over the four māras. "The eight faces, through the liberations" means the purification of the eight gateways of liberation, beginning with "one having form sees forms." "The three eyes, purified, are the three vajras" means the purification of Khecarī, Bhūcarī and Nairātmyā, whose nature is the vajra of body and the rest.

"Called aversion, the Cakriṇī" means aversion purified. "Cakriṇī" is the mistress of the wheel. "Also called" is the expression for the purification of attachment and freedom from attachment, meaning Nairātmyā. "Attachment" is said to be attachment purified. "The secret Gaurī" is the Gaurī of the inner tier. "Delusion is likewise called Vajrā" means Vajrā is delusion purified.

"In the context of the generation stage": the intent is that in the context of the completion stage, the aggregates and the rest, as suchness itself, are called the supreme purification.

"By whatever things" means: by whatever things, the aggregates, attachment and the rest, worldly people are bound, and through karma, afflictions and so on are afflicted with suffering. "By those very things" means that through the purification of the aggregates, attachment and the rest, they are freed from bondage.

"Therefore the world, through delusion" means: by becoming deluded. If one wonders from what, "because they do not know reality" means: because they do not know that entities are like illusions and are completely pure as the very nature of suchness. Not knowing, they abandon reality and never attain accomplishment. Therefore, for one who fully knows reality, there are no entities such as smell. Hence, since the mind is impure, it is through suchness and the like that this impurity is purified. Moreover, since it abides in the state of the innate, and everything is of its nature, how could this be purified? The tantra says: "Through that itself everything is purified; one should realize that beings are pure by intrinsic nature." That is, all entities are utterly and completely pure, having arisen from the essence of purity. The meaning is that this very arising from the innate should be seen as the aggregates and so forth being innate.

This is the ninth chapter, the explanation of purification.

Thus, without having received empowerment, one has no fortune to meditate on reality. And since empowerment does not take place without a maṇḍala, the maṇḍala, the means of making reality clear, is taught for the sake of empowering disciples: "Next I shall properly explain." "In what sequence" means: what the sequence of drawing the maṇḍala is. "By what rite" means: by empowering the disciple; that too I shall fully explain.

"The yogin should purify the ground," and so on: in a place such as a pleasure garden, the mantra practitioner, having first performed the approach, and wishing to make a maṇḍala in order to gather disciples or to perform a consecration, worships the earth goddess Pṛthivī with flowers and so forth.

With the mantra oṃ bhu khaṃ,
holding a vase, yellow in color,
having first summoned her,
contemplate her in the sky as one who knows.

Having properly blessed it with oṃ tiṣṭha vajra hūṃ, one should dig down to about the depth of the knee. Then remove the ashes, charcoal, husks, stones, bones, rubble and so forth that come up, sprinkle that earth or other earth with fragrant water, and fill the dug hole with earth. Reciting hūṃ, sprinkle it with fragrant water, tamp it down and make it level. Then invite the earth goddess into it with oṃ medinī vajri bhava vajra bandha hūṃ, make her enter, and imagine that it is blessed.

If one is quite unable
to remove all these faults,
lay the hand upon it and recite the mantra;
that itself is purity, O greatly renowned one.
Rooftops, on stone, cattle sheds,
temples, riverbanks, royal sites and the like
are not to be purified by digging,
nor are places that were purified before.

Thus one should purify the ground. The rite of drawing the maṇḍala will be explained there.

"With effort, make a vajra from hūṃ" means: from the transformation of raṃ comes a sun, and from the hūṃ resting on that sun arises a crossed vajra; make the ground of the nature of that crossed vajra, and so purify the ground. The place for drawing the maṇḍala is taught: "a pleasure garden," and so on. The explanation of "excellent" is taught: "with powders of the five precious substances," and so on. The maṇḍala endowed with a definite measure is taught: "a maṇḍala of three cubits." Its meaning is this:

With the pride of Vajrasattva,
stand with the right leg extended;
joining the two hands as Heruka,
the master should enter the maṇḍala.
Proclaiming hūṃ hūṃ
and hi hi, terrifying,
the mantra practitioner holds vajra and bell.
Gaurī and the others make the request:
"Peace of all the Tathāgatas,
abode of all the Tathāgatas,
Vajrasattva, perfectly arisen,
chief of the maṇḍala, please teach!
All the marks perfectly complete,
all that are not marks put to flight,
Samantabhadra, perfectly arisen,
lord of the maṇḍala, please teach!
The Dharma of peace, arisen from the supreme,
purifying the conduct of wisdom,
Vajraratna, perfectly arisen,
lord of the maṇḍala, please teach!
Great mind of all sentient beings,
stainless through natural purity,
Vairocana, greatly arising,
lord of the maṇḍala, please teach!"
Then, rising from the seat of the mudrā,
gazing all around,
the one who utters hūṃ,
performing the vajra dance with the feet,
circles about, full of pride,
and gazing with the vajra gaze,
should meditate on blazing vajras beneath the feet,
emanated from hūṃ.
The wise one twirls the vajra
and diligently rings the bell.
By this purifying of the ground
the protection too becomes great.
By this utterance even great
obstructors are driven away:
"Great obstructors, go away!
Whatever body and the rest there are,
arisen from the wisdom of great bliss,
have arisen as the nature of Vajrasattva.
I, the glorious power of compassion,
set up the protection wheel;
possessing a body of blazing vajras,
arisen from the three bodies, I hold you back.
If you transgress,
you will come to terror, and not otherwise."
Having taken possession of the ground,
strike it with the ritual dagger, so there are no obstructors:
oṃ vajra kīli kīlaya bandha hūṃ phaṭ.
Having bound the boundaries, the fence and the directions,
one should establish the ground in preparation.
The yellow goddess arisen from vaṃ
holds a golden vase.
Worship her with the five offerings,
and make the request with the mudrā of Akṣobhya:
"O goddess who makes offerings to Hevajra,
holding various jewels,
goddess, be my witness.
Since I, named so-and-so,
am to draw the supreme maṇḍala,
please purify the site of the maṇḍala."
With fragrant sandalwood and the like,
as the goddess's place,
do it in the sequence of the maṇḍala.
Invite her into that wheel-shaped diagram,
and perform worship, praise and so forth.
Long-necked, with hanging bellies,
free of black at the base,
eight vases, in the initial order,
furnished with mango leaves and the like;
wrap their necks with cloth.
Having drawn the emblems of the deities,
place the five grains, five medicines and five jewels
inside the great bellies.
Tie on flower garlands and vajra tops,
and with their own mantras
the mantra practitioner consecrates them.
Offer arghaṃ, cool water for the feet, worship and so on.
White flowers should be placed inside.
Having filled them with fragrant water,
set them out in the order of the rite,
or else worship as before.
Taking incense in the hand,
the wise one makes supplication to the chief of the circle.

Kneeling before Nairātmyā,
pay homage to and praise the Vajra Goddess Nairātmyā,
queen of vidyā-mantras:
"I wish to draw the maṇḍala.
In order to make offering to the power of compassion,
come here for the sake of the disciples,
and grant me your permission.
Buddhas, heroes and ḍākinīs,
supremely devoted to the welfare of beings,
buddhas, think of me!
Mighty guardians of the world,
the maṇḍala of Nairātmyā and the others
I wish to draw for the sake of beings.
I, the vajra holder, am called such-and-such.
With such requisites as I am able to provide,
show loving kindness
to me and my disciples,
and may all who are assembled in the maṇḍala
come near as well."
Having supplicated the circle three times,
the wise one performs the offerings and the rest.
Having made thorough protection, he goes outside.
The disciple, holding a garland of flowers,
sincere in mind and with great enthusiasm,
and with deep humility as well, should stand there.
Then all of those disciples,
having mentally placed themselves within,
supplicate the self-appearing lotus circle
from the door of the house:
"You, great hero, my Teacher,
who strive for the welfare of all beings,
Protector, grant me the pledges!
Grant me also the supreme vow!
The Buddha, the Dharma and the Saṅgha,
and the awakening mind as well, grant me!
Having wholly abandoned conceptual thought,
I wish to enter the delightful city."
Then, singling out one chief disciple,
the master should speak these words:
"Come here! The great vehicle's
clear way of mantra conduct
I shall teach to you, my son.
You are a vessel for the great way.
The buddhas who arise in the three times,
holders of body, speech and mind,
through the power of the vajra mantra
truly attained unequaled wisdom
and conquered the most fearsome armies of Māra.
The heroes such as Śākyasiṃha
came in keeping with the world,
turned the wheel, and passed into nirvāṇa.
Therefore, intelligent son, by this
you must attain omniscience!"
Having done the confession and the rest three times,
at the heart, the throat and the crown
imagine that the sky-goers, the earth-goers
and the underground-goers abide.
With sandalwood and the vajra fist,
properly pronounce oṃ āḥ hūṃ.
At the heart, the head and the throat,
give flowers and the rest in order:
flowers at the head, incense in front,
lamp and perfume at the heart,
each properly recited with its own mantra.
The yogin should worship the disciple.
A tooth stick twelve fingers long, with a flower at its tip,
not crooked, not split, without insects,
of aśvattha or udumbara,
or of moyoba wood, should be given.
Facing east or north,
smear a patch of ground the size of a cowhide;
having chewed the stick, it should be thrown.
It indicates the accomplishments such as pacifying.

To confer, after the master empowerment, the secret empowerment and the wisdom-gnosis empowerment, it says: "The vidyā is to be brought to him." This refers to the passage "mother, sister," and so forth. Since in the secret empowerment doubt might possibly arise, the binding of the faces of both is taught, in the passage beginning "of the mudrā." The wisdom empowerment is taught: "It is to be done with that very one too." This means at that very maṇḍala site, with that same consort of wisdom or with another. The nature of wisdom-gnosis is taught: "self-awareness." Knowing the mingling of means and wisdom is the wisdom of sameness in which both are all-joy, or the nature of emptiness and compassion. "Passion and dispassion mingled" means the nature of the sameness of supreme joy and the joy free of joy. In order to indicate that it pervades everything, it says "That itself," and so forth. "Whatever others there are, those": this means that the imputed forms just taught are also of its nature. Because their nature is not known they have other names, or are known through another identity, but ultimately they are simply the coemergent.

That itself, together with what purifies it, is what is spoken of in the passage beginning "Beings, consciousness, and form." He himself said:

It truly abides like an illusory form;
in whatever aspect it is imagined,
in that form it appears.

What is to be indicated as the lord of all is shown one by one. "First, mere joy": joy, supreme joy and the rest are the means to it. The joy of cessation is not truly what is sought, because it is the stage that gives rise to it, that is, what serves to bring it to mind. Therefore what is free from the characteristics of those three is the fourth. It completes the count of four, but it is not something that abides immediately after the third.

"Having heard this" refers to the nature of the great innate being. "Vajragarbha and the other wise ones" means the bodhisattvas. "Supremely amazed" means they marvel. To be endowed with the innate is to attain it. Having attained it, swooning in self-awareness through the intense innate bliss, they fully withdraw grasped and grasper and the rest, and the senses and the rest, and are absorbed into mere suchness. Having attained emptiness, they look with compassion upon beings according to their fortune, and go to those world-systems, thinking, "May they attain great bliss, whose nature is emptiness and compassion." That is the meaning.

It is also said: "First, joy is the form of beings," and so forth. In "the form of beings," "beings" is merely a conventional designation. The same holds for supreme joy and the joy of cessation. "The innate is not in the three" means that in this way the innate is supremely excellent, and this too the Blessed One (the Buddha) made clear. That is the meaning.

Having thus found it, the Blessed One spoke in order to make it clear to others as well: "It is neither passion nor dispassion," and "Nor is a middle apprehended." A middle exists only in relation to a beginning and an end; since these do not exist, a middle too is not apprehended. This is as in "Beginning, middle and end are emptiness." Likewise, in what will be explained later: "because there is no beginning, end or middle."

Alternatively, to someone who wonders, "If the innate is neither passion nor dispassion, what then is it?" he says "the middle": the middle that is freed from passion and from dispassion is the innate. "Not apprehended separately" means it is unapprehendable and unchanging.

To someone who thinks, "If it is like that, then since it is not an object for worldly people, it can be neither realized nor attained," he says, "That, moreover, is to be examined first in the joy of cessation." "That" means its characteristic. Here it is in no way realized from anything else; it becomes clear through the power of meditating on the guru's instructions.

To show the sequence in which it appears distinctly through meditating on the guru's instructions, he says "first." At the time of training it first appears distinctly as if veiled. Then it is somewhat clear, like an illusion. Then what is to be examined becomes very clear; "simultaneous" means momentary. "It appears like a dream" means that just as it was seen before, it appears clearly, indistinguishable from a dream. Likewise the innate also appears as clarity itself. By meditating on it still more clearly, it is as though dream and waking were indistinguishable; it appears equally clear in all states. Then the accomplishment whose characteristic is being indistinguishable in all states while appearing distinctly, the accomplishment of Mahāmudrā, is achieved. So he says, "The accomplishment has the characteristic of indistinguishability."

"He spoke thus": its meaning is that the maṇḍala is taught. To someone wondering what it is like, he says, "Square, blazing all around," and so forth. In "When the maṇḍala is complete, set up the victorious one," the victorious one is the victory vase. "In the east" means to the right of the eastern door. "New" means because it is unused. "Well endowed" means because it is endowed with the tathāgatas and the rest dwelling in it, or because they have been placed in it. "Of proper measure" means with water twice the measure of the maṇḍala. Its meaning is stated:

Found in the earth of a charnel ground,

The preliminary approach is taught: "A hundred thousand recitations of the lord of the circle," and so forth. "The one taught earlier" means with this: oṃ rakṣa rakṣa hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā. "Then one should give a torma" means at that stage of making the maṇḍala. "Just as taught" means in the chapter on the deities. "Making clear" means empowerment. "According to the rite in this or another tantra" means having first brought the disciple in, through the sequence of water and the rest. "In one's own maṇḍala" means in the maṇḍala of one's own chosen deity. "The offering and supplication, as is well known" means that just as a disciple makes offerings and supplications to the guru, so it should be understood here as well.

"Then draw Gaurī and the others" means that on the inner tier one should draw the curved knives of the five, Nairātmyā and the others, and below them one should draw the emblems of Gaurī and the others. That is the meaning. He himself said, "In the east," and so forth.

"Likewise below, and likewise above": below is Bhūcarī, and "likewise" means the curved knife. Above, Khecarī is placed in the same way. Here, according to the oral instructions, Bhūcarī is placed close to the emblem of Vetālī, and Khecarī close to the emblem of Gaurī on the outside.

"Having taken on the pride of Vajrasattva" means acting as Vajrasattva. "Pride" is the name for taking on the sense of oneself as that deity.

"By proclaiming hūṃ hūṃ" means uttering "hūṃ hūṃ". "Proclaiming" is the name for the effort made to drive away obstructors.

So one enters the maṇḍala and should circumambulate it. Having completed the samādhi of one's own deity, one worships the diagram with flowers and so forth, fumigates it with incense and offers praise. After that, one empowers the disciple. "Explaining reality fully" is for the sake of the disciple.

"Pure" means free of all obscurations. "Having the nature of wisdom" means it has the very nature of luminosity, which is the innate. "Saṃsāra is purified" means that ultimately there is neither existence nor nirvāṇa, so there is not the slightest difference between them. The meaning is that what is called nirvāṇa is existence itself fully understood.

That same point is stated: "Even where supreme joy is absent". Here "joy" is the gnosis in which means and wisdom are nondual, and in it nothing to be meditated on can be found. The meditator is of the nature of the subject's body, the deity, and so forth. Ultimately, what has become the object of meditation and the meditator does not function as grasped object and grasping subject. Because the body has no real existence, the text says "no flesh and no blood", and so on.

Alternatively, the object of meditation is the supporting maṇḍala, and the meditator is the supported maṇḍala.

"There is no body" because the five aggregates are purified as deities. "No grasped object" means form and the rest purified as Gaurī and the others. "No grasping subject" means the six, the eye and the rest, purified as Mohavajra and the others. The absence of flesh and so forth is their purification as Pukkasī and the others. "No nausea" means compassion and the rest purified, whose very nature is Locanā and the others. "No desire" refers to the six universal monarchs, Amitābha and the others. "No meditation" means there is no path. The meditator is the one who meditates: the person, the mind, and so forth. "Fame" is what brings forth the qualities of a buddha. "Enemy" means the afflictions that are to be destroyed. None of these can be found.

If one wonders why, the text says "without conceptualization". By the nature of supreme joy, the innate, none of these are conceptualized. Conceptualization means conceptualizing in terms of distinctions. Because that is stilled, there is supreme oneness in great bliss.

"Then" means that one marvels at the division into various forms, as something never seen before. Or else the forms are called "various" because they are free of any nature of variety.

Now, in order to point out the innate, the text says: "To show that the innate is not to be grasped, the following question of clarification was asked." There is nothing to grasp, and the innate is not something to be grasped. "This question" means that, concerning great bliss, the innate gnosis that is isolated from everything, the one called Vajragarbha asked again: "Why has the self of the elements become innate gnosis? Why is it called the innate?" That is the meaning.

"Body" refers to the bodies of Hevajra and the others, which are innate and pure in their essential nature. To the thought "Pure of what?", the answer is given: they are unborn from the very beginning and without intrinsic nature.

Now it is stated how the self of the elements is, for the time being, produced: "Through the union of vajra and lotus", and "Therefore that bliss is not reality". This means that bliss of that kind is not reality. Even so, it was explained earlier that it serves to point out reality. Then, to the thought "What is reality like?": what is born together from means and wisdom, whatever arises in this way, is born together from the ultimate, and that is reality. To the thought "From what?", the text says "innate by nature". Nature means its own mode: things are innate. How could the self of the great elements, which never depends on external objects and the like, be innate?

So "all things gathered into one" means gathering things as one and nondual. That is gathering, gathered into one, the supreme of bliss. Therefore: "The yoga becomes wisdom and means." Because it is free of the mudrā and the union of her causes, phenomena are unborn. All phenomena are unborn and cannot be found. Therefore the mudrā is nothing other than one's own wisdom. That same point is stated:

Emptiness and compassion, inseparable,
Are declared to be called the awakening mind.

This means that the innate of that kind, known from means and wisdom, great bliss itself, is the ultimate awakening mind, reality.

When one has meditated on an awakening mind of that kind, there is no mantra recitation, no austerity, and so forth. That very awakening mind is itself mantra recitation, for it is of the nature of knowing and protecting. It is itself the observance, for its nature is free of all wrongdoing. It is itself the fire offering, for it destroys all the burnt offerings, the afflictions and the aggregates. It is itself the deities of the maṇḍala, for it is the embodiment of the assembled vajrayoginīs. It is itself the maṇḍala, for it takes up the essence of all phenomena.

Therefore "in brief" means in summary. What use is there in saying more? "Having the mode of the assembled mind": the mind is the awakening mind, and "having the mode of assembly" is the mode in which all phenomena are assembled. The meaning is that it is the very self of all phenomena. Therefore it should be generated, and by generating it, everything is accomplished. That is the meaning.

This concludes the explanation of the tenth chapter, on empowerment.

Next he states the means by which yogins who have reached the rank of master through empowerment and the rest bring beings in, in order to teach them the Dharma. On "evenly, wrathfully": "evenly" means looking downward and to the sides. "Wrathfully" means with wrath. "The forehead" means resting slightly on the forehead. "Making fall": it is the falling gaze because it makes things fall. The word "look" is to be joined with what comes before. For subjugation, the gaze is directed to the left side. This is what is stated by "the two fruits."

"For summoning, toward the right side" means that one should turn the eyes upward, roll them to the right, and look toward the right side. This is what is stated in "Both are to be directed upward." "In the middle" means not letting them move up, down, or to the corners.

Now, for anyone who wonders by which application of the wind which gazes are to be accomplished, he states the passage beginning "For the falling gaze, by emission." Deity yoga and mantra recitation were taught earlier.

Now he states the objects on which the student trains, in the passage beginning "The falling gaze, on a moist tree." He states the purpose of accomplishing the gazes: "should be accomplished" and "should be brought in" mean brought into the teaching.

He states the pledge substance: "And the samaya should be eaten." "For that" means for empowerment, for accomplishing the siddhis, and so on. "Seven-times-born" means one who has taken a human body in seven births; "seven times" means the seventh birth. "Overcoming the absence of joy" means that he generates the innate nature in every circumstance and overcomes what is devoid of joy. "The state of a sky-goer" means holding the rank of a universal monarch of the vidyādharas, which is to find a wisdom equal to space.

Having taught the gazes for the purpose of subjugation, he teaches deity yoga: "The practice of Kurukullā." "Taught earlier" refers to the mantra taught in the chapter on mantras, because mantra and deity are inseparable. "Arisen from hrīḥ" means this: one visualizes at one's own heart, on a moon disc, the form of hrīḥ. With the hooks of its light rays one summons before oneself the guru, the buddhas, the bodhisattvas, and Kurukullā as well, and visualizing them, one performs the confession of misdeeds and the rest. Having meditated through to the end of loving-kindness and the others taught in the chapter on the deity, and of the protection wheel, one visualizes in space a red lotus arisen from hrīḥ. In its center one first sets the seed syllable, or else the five aspects of the circle of the maṇḍala, and from their transformation one meditates on the Blessed Lady Kurukullā. "With one hundred thousand, kings" means by recitation.

Composed by the vajra master Saroruhavajra, the explanation of the first part of the Hevajra Tantra is complete.

"One should consecrate in this way and that": the meaning of this is stated in brief with "abiding in space." "Knowing" means knowing the rite of consecration. "Gone to the far shore of mantra" means having perfected the mantra vehicle, or having completed, through the earlier approach, what was taught before. "Born of the various hūṃ" means that the flowers and so forth are each born from hūṃ. One should offer them with these mantras. "By the sequence of rites of the earlier tantras": this one is the later tantra, while the Tattvasaṃgraha, the Guhyasamāja and so forth are earlier tantras; it means by the sequence of rites taught in them.

Now the characteristics of the hearth are stated, beginning "for pacifying." "The rest are to be accomplished by that same" refers to subjugation, summoning, causing strife, expulsion and so forth. It is also said: "That itself is mantra recitation, that itself is the observance, that itself is the fire offering," and so on.

The principal substances to be burned for pacifying and the other rites are taught in the passage beginning "For pacifying, sesame." In the section on mantras, the mantra for preparing the ground is taught in the passage beginning "Goddess, you."

This is the explanation of the first chapter of the second part.

Although many methods have been taught, the question was asked so that the definitive one would be taught. "Like space" means resembling space. "In phenomena" means: because they are infinite, by that nature which is the absence of intrinsic nature, the stainless nature. Just as a small gourd that has fallen into the ocean drifts with no fixed course, so too sentient beings wander with no fixed course; so by what definite method, and how, will virtue be accomplished? In reply to this comes "Through the yoga of selflessness," that is, possessing the yoga of selflessness, which means possessing the yoga of the generation and completion stages. "Or else" introduces another alternative, or sums up. What shows here the method by which their going becomes definite, through the method of gathering the infinite collections of merit and wisdom, is "with no other thought even for an instant."

"The place is examined": to the thought, "Since everywhere is common and without distinction, what is the stability that comes from the method of definitely determining a place?" it is said, "one's own house." "With this mind whose nature is accomplishment" means having the pride that thinks, "I am pure by nature." The explanation of "with no other thought even for an instant" is given in the passage beginning "the foot."

In the passage beginning "the Blessed Lady," the Blessed Lady is the yoginī. "Should be relied upon" is to be connected with "should be meditated upon." "Not by another form even for an instant" means not by way of division. It rejects the alternative, saying: not by the particularizing mind, the poison of ignorance. "Destroyed to some extent" refers to some of the evil of the obscurations together with their habitual tendencies.

The benefit of meditation of that kind is taught with the words "fear and madness." Madness is the whirling of the mind. "Therefore, examine thus" means: by the yogin. "Results of benefit and harm arise" means that the result of this meditative concentration is benefit, while the result of other thoughts is what does not benefit. "Howling" refers to saṃsāra, churned by intense suffering, conceptual thought and the like.

Well then, those who perform meritorious deeds are indeed the ones fit to enter here, while those who perform evil deeds would be unable to enter, since they would not experience the result. To the thought, "How then can they too enter?" it is said, "Those who commit the five acts of immediate retribution," and so on, because by the power of this very practice those evils are destroyed. Therefore the meaning is that when, by training in it, that evil is gradually exhausted and they gather the collections of merit and wisdom, they too are vessels.

"Through constant application in this way, one attains accomplishment" means one gains some measure of power. "Keep it secret for a month" means not revealing it anywhere, not even slightly. "The conduct is that of the yogin himself" means one should wander about. "Finds the supreme" means one finds what the yoginī reveals. That is why it says, "Having completely abandoned all conceptions."

"As a condition for one's own steadiness" means one should realize that oneself is not carried away by worldly concerns such as suffering. "It is not taught for the sake of enjoyment" means it is for the sake of attaining the Great Seal (Mahāmudrā), not in the manner of enjoyment. "In a fearsome manner" means practicing austerities with one-pointed mind in all circumstances. The explanation of "as a condition for one's steadiness" is: "One should examine one's own mind individually."

To the thought, "How is the mudrā itself to be divided?": here, as for "mudrā," how could a division of mudrā be appropriate? Here it is not appropriate to apply it as a mudrā and some other mudrā. "Mudrā" is, first of all, this mudrā of selflessness; and if there were yet another mudrā besides it, how would accomplishment be attained? The meaning is that there would be no union of means and wisdom. In reply to this comes "the form of a woman."

"The two sides" are the two banks of the lotus. "Bell" refers to the fruit. "Abiding in the center of the lotus" is to be connected with "the center becomes a vajra." "Manifest power" means might, power that has arisen in a manifest way, not merely potential power. For the accomplishment of the Great Seal is the accomplishment of the Great Seal; therefore one's conduct should be carried out with regard to that, and that alone, and not otherwise.

Now, in order to refute birth and death for the yogin who has attained the accomplishment of the Great Seal, it is said, "Even by birth and destruction." "Means and wisdom" refers to whatever is the mode of bodhicitta in which means and wisdom are inseparable.

How could that be bound by arising and perishing?

He states the nature of means and wisdom. "Means is that because of which there is arising" means this: what arises through the power of the yogin's means is birth, awakening, the turning of the wheel of Dharma and so forth, and these are means. Wisdom is the bringing of that becoming to its end. In reality one does not conceive even of birth and the rest, and this is wisdom. Through that very wisdom, what is destroyed here is drawn together, which is to say that all phenomena are made into this and that. Therefore, for yogins, in the ultimate there is neither arising nor perishing. Conventionally, however, they engage in awakening, final nirvāṇa and the like by way of worldly conduct.

"Perishing": perishing through separation from the combination of causes and conditions is death. "There is no perishing, and no exhaustion of non-entity" means that where causes and conditions are never incomplete, there is no perishing. Its meaning is this: whatever has dharma and adharma certainly perishes through exhaustion. But if phenomena are unborn and nonexistent by essential nature, how could that, and those, perish? That is the meaning.

Now he speaks of the great nature of the generation stage, in the passage "the generation stage." "The empowerment of the great seal" is the empowerment of wisdom and gnosis. "To be known" means to be closely examined. To show that great bliss is all-pervading, he says "bliss" and so forth.

In order to teach the completion stage itself, Vajragarbha asked, "the completion stage." "How wonderful! By the force of faith" means with great faith. Its meaning is that in which one focuses in an instant:

The deity endowed with a mind of bliss,
With the mind supremely delighted,
Enjoying whatever pleases, quickly attains.

"I am the entity" is in the mode of means. "I am the non-entity" is in the mode of wisdom. "I am the expounder" is the enjoyment body (sambhogakāya). The Dharma is the Dharma body (dharmakāya). The listener is the emanation body (nirmāṇakāya). Or else, "the nature of all" means that things such as the listener are themselves buddha, and phenomena are pure by their essential nature. "I am what is to be accomplished" is said with respect to the practitioner who does not know. "Likewise also the condition" means likewise also knowledge.

"I myself am the Teacher with the thirty-two marks" means that the enjoyment body teaches the Dharma. "That itself is called semen" is said by way of the emanation, and by way of bliss signifying the Dharma body. Then comes "since that does not exist." Therefore the entities of the enjoyment and emanation bodies are also non-entities in that coemergent. Yet although it abides in that mode, it is not a non-entity, since through extraordinary compassion the two bodies are emanated. Or else, by that mode in which the coemergent is rightly established, I am not of the nature of utter non-entity.

Therefore, if one wonders from what source "all beings are coemergent," he says, "the nature of the coemergent is declared." That is, things are coemergent by nature, and that abides everywhere as unbornness itself: the nature that is the naturelessness of phenomena. Thus, through their dreamlike character, it is established for the time being that things do not exist outside the mind. Moreover, being free of one and many and free of a cause, they lack essential nature and are unborn, like an illusion. Therefore all things are without nature, as it says, "always abiding as the coemergent."

"Possessing the form of a deity's aspect": as for distinguishing the bodies of buddhas from human bodies, for now there is no difference in the aspect of arms and faces, nor any difference in the five aggregates themselves, because flesh and the rest are set out in the manner of Pukkasī and the other goddesses. If one thinks that the bodies of the buddhas are of the nature of consciousness while these are of the nature of earth and the other elements, then "Oh!" expresses not knowing: even right now all these phenomena are seen as of the nature of consciousness, and there is not the slightest difference.

If so, one wonders, how is there cyclic existence? He says, "Nevertheless, through the habitual tendency of nature," that is, because through the beginningless habitual tendency of ignorance the nature does not appear. If one asks from what ignorance arises, it arises from karma and so forth. If one wonders from what ignorance itself arises, it is from another ignorance, as the passage beginning "another ignorance" says. If all phenomena do not exist, then one may ask, from what is there ignorance? That is true: when one realizes this firmly, ignorance does not exist. He teaches so that these may be firmly realized: when there is no ignorance, all phenomena are unborn and are nirvāṇa. That is stated in the passage beginning "by which the act."

In "Buddhas and non-Buddhists (tīrthikas) do not know," "buddhas" refers to the śrāvakas and the like, and "the knowledge of tīrthikas" refers to all tīrthikas. "The one great joy" is coemergent joy. "Divided into five" refers to the number of its divisions. Through the division of the tathāgatas there is the division of Vajrasattva, that is, into the five families. Again, it is by the intention of an inner division within the tathāgatas themselves. "Therefore the essence of one" means that for this very reason everything is the nature of that very unbornness, just as pots, bowls and the like that come from clay do not abandon the form of clay. That itself is stated: "arising from supreme joy." Therefore the meaning is: all that arises from it, including the infinite other families as well.

This concludes the explanation of the second chapter of the second part, the determination of the accomplishments.

Then, "the vajra-holder" is Vajrasattva. "The introduction of all tantras" is the introductory basis of every tantra; that by whose meaning one enters all the tantras without exception is called its means. "Of the yoginīs" means either for the sake of the yoginīs, or the yoginīs, that is, the means by which they are accomplished. The means for that is declared: if one wonders what it is, it is the four beginning with "saṃvara." Saṃvara is supreme bliss, or the self of all phenomena, gathered up by the tathāgatas. "It is to be known from empowerment" means where the empowerment is known from e and vaṃ.

"Perfectly" means that through the supreme empowerment one washes away every stain without remainder. In case one wonders what that is, he said: "great bliss," that is, the gnosis of great bliss. To make that very meaning clear, the yoginīs asked, and from that it is known. Vaṃ is what has become method. "Adorned" means ornamented. This is stated:

E is declared to be called wisdom;
Vaṃ is what is called method.
Made beautiful by what is called vaṃ,
E is indeed beautiful.

"Gnosis of the moments" means this: when the joys and the rest are fully understood through their division into moments, that is the gnosis of bliss, the gnosis of great bliss. The division by moments itself is stated in the passage beginning "Variety." "Knows thus" means that one knows the union (saṃvara) of e and vaṃ.

Variety is varied joy:
that is, kissing, embracing and the like.
Through the union of vajra and lotus,
Maturation is supreme gnosis.
Consummation is the pressing of the mudrā.
Freedom from characteristics is to be examined with certainty.

In case one wonders from what, in what, and how the examination is to be accomplished, it says "master." Taking the master empowerment includes the seven empowerments, such as water and the crown, and by it the body is purified. By the secret empowerment speech is purified. By the wisdom empowerment mind is purified. "It is to be properly examined": the fourth is to be accomplished by what is to be accomplished. Through the sequence of the joys and so on, this is to be understood as four empowerments.

That the single wisdom-gnosis empowerment is itself the form of the four empowerments is stated thus. "Laughter" means the purified laughter, speech and so on: that is the master empowerment, which is striving in variety. Gazing in secret is the secret empowerment. The union of embracing with the hands is the wisdom-gnosis empowerment. "That again" is the fourth. "Embracing in pairs" means that when the two come together with each other, the tantra in question is the one that abides in that gnosis whose nature is the inseparability of method and wisdom.

"Having washed" means making the wisdom partner stainless. "Through the union of vajra and bell" means that, the vajra and other empowerments having been obtained, it is empowerment because the master confers empowerment. "Fair-faced one" carries the sense "is to be blessed." "The disciple is to be recognized" means one should recognize him as fortunate enough for the wisdom empowerment.

Now the rite is stated by which the disciple should make offerings to the guru and supplicate for the wisdom-gnosis empowerment. "Accompanied by the mudrā" means that, having made offerings to the guru, who is accompanied by a mudrā complete with every ornament, one praises him with this, the passage beginning "O Blessed One," which is then spoken.

"Pacified" means great peace, because the fire of the afflictions has been pacified. Vajra yoga is the inseparable nature of method and wisdom. "Solely intent" means intent with a mind that is nondual with regard to it. "Thoroughly accomplishing the mudrā" means being inseparable from the Great Seal through passionate attachment to it and through generating it. "As you" refers to the method by which you, great being, came to have great power. "Me too" means: "By that method, please make me perfect as well!" "Great flesh" means supremely abundant. "Afterwards" means when the disciple has fully attained supreme joy. "Teach" refers to the disciple.

As for "say," if one asks what is to be said, it is "Hold!": that is, "Make reality firm in gnosis in this way, with steadiness." "Having empowered, do" refers to that by which one is to abide, namely the whole collection of its pledges. "Having empowered, one should make reality clear" means that, once the three empowerments of master and the rest have been given, the fourth is taught.

"This very" refers to what you are to know: that itself is all-pervading, nondual, the awakening mind in which emptiness and compassion are inseparable, the reality that has no dual form. "This very thing, by means of your maṇḍala circle" means that it is to be made manifest.

Hearing "by means of the maṇḍala circle," Vajragarbha asked its meaning: "What is the maṇḍala circle?" The Blessed One (the Buddha) said: "Maṇḍa means essence," and that itself is the essence. In case one wonders what that is: "The awakening mind is great bliss." Thus, what takes that essence, maṇḍa, that is, what obtains it, is the maṇḍala. That itself is the circle, the gathered assembly of the yoginīs of Hevajra. "Called the sphere of space" means the purified element of space, the nature of Vajrasattva. "That itself purifies the objects and so on in every respect" means that it purifies. The objects are the sense sources of form and the rest; "and so on" includes the sense sources of the eye and the rest, the aggregates, and so on.

Vajragarbha asked: "Once empowered, by what pledges should one abide?" Here the answer is the passage beginning "For this purpose, living beings too." This meaning is made clear in terms of the definitive meaning. In the phrase "one-pointed mind," living beings here are conceptual thoughts. Gathering living beings into nondual gnosis, which is itself the pacification of conceptual thought, is killing; living beings are the mind. "Because life" means that here there are no living beings apart from mind, and mind too operates in many forms, as sentient beings and so on. Gathering it in is itself the killing.

"Wishing to make the world unsurpassed" is the lie. Sentient beings are pure by their essential nature. Since sentient beings are equal to space, how could they be liberated? Yet it has been said that they are not, for that reason, to be abandoned; so one wishes to make sentient beings unsurpassed. Thus:

What the compassionate one does
Is never to abandon sentient beings;
That sentient beings exist or do not exist,
One should not hold fixed in this way.

To make clear what was said, "One engages in the objects and the rest," he asked, "Whatever those are." To teach their purification he said: "By nature they are unborn from the very beginning."

Precisely because of this, these are unborn, like reflections and the rest: "They are neither true nor false." The passage that shows this unborn state to be dependent origination itself is the one beginning "The fire-drill." "Thus all phenomena, the yoginīs" means this: all phenomena are thus unborn from the very beginning, pure by nature, wholly directed toward the innate alone, not false, not true, and at peace from the very beginning. Having heard in this way that all phenomena are selfless, the yoginīs rejoiced and made the Blessed One (the Buddha) truly pleased. The Blessed One, pleased in turn, spoke fully: this is the passage beginning "Eating and drinking."

A yogin who knows in this way that all phenomena are unborn should act as follows. Here, what is eaten is the five meats and so on, and what is drunk is the bodhicitta and so on. "What may be approached" is the bliss that arises from sense objects; "what may not be approached" is what transcends sense objects. Cleanliness is the notion of purity: one practises no cleanliness beyond discerning the dharmadhātu. "The practice of cold" means that, having rightly understood it as bliss, one does not reject it. Mantra proceeds by the order in which the syllables are individually fixed or not fixed. Conception proceeds by the fixed order of the mantra. Sleep is the sleep of yoga, and it is not to be given up. Because the senses, accomplished as the purity of each respective sense faculty, experience objects in accordance with themselves, one does not prohibit this. The passage beginning "Loving toward friends" teaches the abandoning of attachment and anger. The god is the Tathāgata. "Not bowing" means not giving rise to a notion of difference between them and oneself. The ḍombī and the others, too, one touches as oneself, in the form of the body of glorious Heruka.

Molasses is blood. Liquor is marrow. Poison is fat. Nimba is skin, and that is what comes from childbirth. As it is said:

Sour clears away wind;
sweet clears away bile;
by astringent the eyes are purified;
bitter destroys fever;
salt becomes fire;
pungent overcomes phlegm.

"It is called the five nectars": its explanation is the passage beginning "Bone." Bone is Amoghasiddhi. Liquor is Vairocana. Water is called Akṣobhya. Blood is Ratnasambhava.

These are elixirs;
they destroy all diseases,
they are chief among all that protect,
and they overcome all conceptual thought.

In order to purify the purifications that had not been taught before, he asked: "The sense faculties." Asking for an explanation that shows the intention behind what was taught earlier, he said: "What is the explanation spoken with intention?" The answer is the passage beginning "With intention." "Of the four kinds of tantra" means that it was not taught even in the Kriyā, Caryā, Yoga, or Higher Yoga tantras. "Five families" means that these are the five families, that is, the five buddhas. That very point is stated in "the ḍombī." The dog-cooker woman is the caṇḍālī. "By harming the commitment" means by its being broken. This is the explanation of the third chapter, the chapter on the setting of all the tantras.

Now the chapter explaining what was taught earlier is spoken: "Then." "Doubt arose" means being uneasy because doubt had arisen. As for what was said, "If one sings songs out of joy, then, possessed of the supreme vajra," and so on: what vajra is there in song and dance? What is the dance of the vajra feet? And "the empowerment of the deities": in the chapter on the empowerment of the deities, which seal is to be impressed by which? Hence "Which is to be sealed by which?" "Extraction of the mantras" refers to the chapter on mantras; there the meaning is: which is the seed syllable of which?

On this, first the vajra song is spoken: kolla i re ṭṭhi a. kolla i re ṭṭhi is the sacred place. By union with the bola abiding there; bola is the vajra of the yogin. As for mummuṇi: "The field is renowned as mummuṇi." By union with that which abides there; kakkola is that of the vajra yoginīs. It teaches that the yogins and yoginīs dwelling in the sacred places and elsewhere gather together. Gathered there, acting in accord with their innate nature is taught in ghaṇe kṛpiṭaho bājja i: there the small drum is to be beaten without interruption, which teaches that all phenomena are like echoes. It is to be done first; the meaning is that one performs dance and song. kāruṇe kia ina rolā means one should not quarrel: there one should be loving toward one another and compassionate toward all beings as well. tahiṃ bala khājja i means there, too, one should eat meat. ghaḍheṃ ma aṇa pijja i a i means one should drink liquor that produces intense joy. hale is a call to the yoginīs. kāliñjara paṇi a i means that there the fortunate yogins and yoginīs, drawn from the five families and so on, are to be seated. dundura bājji a i: a dundura is one who has not been empowered and is full of doubt; such a one is to be kept outside and not admitted, and even one who has entered is to be expelled, so that they themselves may be protected from the lower realms. ca u sama kacchuri sihla kāppūra lā i a i means that there excrement, Ratnasambhava, and Amitābha are to be smeared on the body, outside and inside. māla indhaṇa sāliñja tahiṃ bharu khāe a i means that there one should eat vegetables and, above all, the great meat. phreṃ khaṇa kheṭa karante śuddha aśuddhan muṇi a i means that, having given the commitment to one another, they go and come in dance and so on; not thinking "these are pure" or "these are impure," it is without reference point. The meaning is that everything is to be done through practice without reference point. niraṃśu age caḍābi tahiṃ ja śarāva paṇi a i means that in that circle, too, bone ornaments are to be put on the limbs. śārāva is a corpse, which is to be brought in. mala aja kunduru pāṭa i means that in that gathering they unite with one another for the sake of the cause of great bliss. ḍiṇḍima tahinna bājji a i means that there, too, in union, the ḍombī is not to be rejected.

Alternatively, kolla i reṭ means on the head. ji a is abiding. bola is the indestructible vajra holder. mummuṇi re is in the lotus of the navel. kakkolā is wisdom. There, ghaṇe means continuous, never parted from joy. kṛpiṭaho is the indestructible.

"bājjai" means that liquor flows. "kāruṇe" means by means of the gnosis of great bliss. "kiainarolā" means that one does not quarrel through conceptual thought. Alternatively, "kāruṇe" means by the cause of great bliss, and "kiainarolā" means the battle of method and wisdom. "tahiṃ" means there, when the rain falls. "bala khājjai" means eating flesh, that is, the flesh of ordinary things, which is to say, making appearances vanish. "gāḍheṃ" means intensely. "maaṇā" means the taste of compassion. "bajjiiai" means drinking it in, that is, making it one with oneself. "hale kāliñjara" means great bliss, the nature of glorious Vajrasattva. "paṇiaï" means that one should meditate within it. "dundura" means conceptual thought. "bājjiaï" means that all of it is to be abandoned. The passage beginning "causama" means that the tathāgatas, Vairocana and the rest, abide in great bliss itself as coemergent, nondual gnosis.

In the passage beginning "mālai", "mā" is earth, "lai" is water, "indhaṇa" is fire, and "saliñja tahiṃ" is wind. "bharu khāiaï": its meaning is making appearances utterly vanish. "phreṃ khaṇa kheṭa karante" means when wisdom makes the going and the coming. The passage beginning "śuddha aśuddha" means that there is no attention to pure and impure and the like; it is supreme one-pointedness in great bliss. "niraṃśu" is the white awakening mind, free of parts. "aṃge caḍābi tahiṃ ja" means placing it on the limbs. "sarāva paṇiaï" means that, since it pervades the whole body, it is to be introduced, that is, to be spread out. "mala ajo kundu rupāṭai" means that when the yogins gather, one should join there through the true union of method and wisdom.

When method and wisdom are truly united there, "ḍiṇḍima tahi nna bājjiaï" means that through gnosis it is untouchable: since it cannot be touched by conceptual thoughts and the like, it is not something to be touched, and so it is coemergent bliss.

By this the meaning of empowerment is also taught. Alternatively, "kolla ireṭdia" means in the secret place of the navel. "bola" is the vajra. "mumma ṇire" means in the secret place of the navel. "kakkolā" is the lotus. Into that lotus the vajra-natured "kṛpiṭa", that is, the striking, is the movement. "karuṇe" means because great bliss arises. "rolā" means that, not quarreling and being stable, it is continuously the supreme bliss itself. There "bala" is conceptual thought. In the sense of eating or of arising, the bala is to be eaten, and liquor too is to be drunk. "kāliñjara" means that it is introduced through close examination of coemergent joy. "dundura" means the cessation of joy, which is likewise to be abandoned.

The two channels within the place of birth,
from the left and likewise from the right:
from the left the seminal fluid comes forth,
and from the right, the blood as well.
The channel that is lord of the vajra realm
one should purify well on the left.
With the finger stir it a little,
and again with the tip of the tongue.
When the mouth of the channel has opened,
supreme and most intense joy arises.
With the mantrin's vajra, gently,
one should move within and further within.
Placing phaṭ within the jewel,
one should restrain what is born of mind.
Since the awakening mind is not lost,
in an instant supreme bliss arises.
Its nature spreads out without remainder;
the appearance free of interruption,
all-pervading and blissfully at peace,
brings attainment of the highest state.
By losing the awakening mind,
one swoons, and it is not found.

This method, the intelligent should keep from those who lack the fortune. The meaning of the passage beginning "causama" should also be applied as before.

Now the dancing in that very gathering is taught. "The dance in the form of Heruka" means with the pride of Hevajra. "Vajradharma" means singing as the embodiment of speech vajra, or else that the speech vajra yogin should sing. "By the Buddha" means by the embodiment of the dance of Vairocana, or else that the body vajra yogin should dance. "By the mātṛkā yoginīs" means by the yoginīs, the mātṛkās and so on, or else by Gaurī and the others, circling together. "By the Lord of the Gathering" means that the chief of the gathering, glorious Heruka Vajra, is placed in front. There the sign of blessing is taught, and for that it says "scent."

Now the answer to the question about the mudrās is given: "the mudrās." The words mudrā and so on have one meaning. "Marked by the mudrā, whatever family it is should be examined." "Marked by the mudrā" means that by meditating with the families confused one does not obtain accomplishment; nor does a practitioner who does not fully know the family.

The answer to the question beginning "What is the seed?" is given with "āli": the first of the āli is ā, and in the sequence of the second and the rest come ā and the others. The answer to the question beginning "What is taught in the chapter on the families?" is "the chapter on the families." It is taught by way of the distinctive Nairātmyā: lalanā, rasanā and avaddhūtī, the three channels, the chief of which is Nairātmyā.

Then one may think: "Even so, not everything has been included, since the purified yoginīs who destroy the māras have not been mentioned, nor the seed that is the moon of the awakening mind in the form of the āli, whose nature is the sixteen digits." In reply it is said: "the last of all." If one wonders why, it is said: "Because the sixteenth digit is without parts." If one wonders why it does not perform the function of a cause and is without parts, it is said: "Because it performs no function." The meaning of this is part and partless, and having no connection with performing a function; that is by the nature of the dharma realm. By meditating on the fifteen yoginīs, whose nature is the purity of all the goddesses, they are included in that state; it performs no function; and as for where and why one meditates, the function is the very thing to be accomplished.

Now Vajragarbha, with a hidden intention, asks his question, and camphor is the awakening mind. As for "Why does it not certainly arise?", the word "certainly" is not one of acquiescence; it asks why it does not arise, but its meaning is rather "what is to be generated?" And that is by way of the conventional and the ultimate.

Here, "The yoginīs arise": that which gives rise to the yoginīs is nirvāṇa. What arises from it is the conventional nature. That very point is stated: "by the innate nature." "Inexhaustible" means indestructible. "Increases" means that the stream increases. "Sky" refers to what comes into the bird-faced one, that is, what abides in the lotus.

Here the Blessed One (the Buddha) gave his answer in just that way: "by that itself, thus." Therefore the certainty is that it is to be generated as something not to be abandoned. The question about what is to be generated is the line "By what?" The answer here is "the maṇḍala-wheel": by means of the maṇḍala-wheel and the rest, through the generation and completion stages, one should generate the ultimate awakening mind. The word "and the rest" refers to the inner union. "By self-blessing" means that through the union of means and wisdom, having blessed the action seal (karmamudrā), one should generate the conventional awakening mind. The nature of both is stated with the word "conventional." For both, "in the lotus of the consort" means that one should generate it in the lotus of wisdom.

"In just this way is nirvāṇa": nirvāṇa is just like this, the innate nature in which all suffering is completely pacified. Not knowing it fully in this way is saṃsāra. "Apart from saṃsāra, having abandoned it, nirvāṇa will not be known": here "known" means attained. The meaning is that the full knowing of saṃsāra itself is what is called nirvāṇa.

To the thought, "By fully knowing what saṃsāra is there nirvāṇa?", the text says: "Saṃsāra is form, sound, and so on." Delusion is not fully knowing the intrinsic nature. Non-delusion is fully knowing suchness. "By the purity of saṃsāra": the purification of saṃsāra is this: even the taking up of birth and the rest, which the awakened one performs by convention or by wish, is his saṃsāra, namely form and so on. "Because": nirvāṇa is what brings forth the fruit of nirvāṇa.

To the thought, "Is this nirvāṇa like that of the śrāvakas and others?", the answer is no, and so it is said: "The awakening mind is nirvāṇa." The meaning is that what brings one here into this very state of innate great bliss, the indivisibility of emptiness and compassion, is nirvāṇa here, and nothing else.

Now the stage of generating the conventional awakening mind, the cause of nirvāṇa, is taught, in the passage "Fair-faced one." There, "arising from union" means that the awakening mind which arises from the nondual union of means and wisdom is camphor. "Not to be abandoned" means: because one takes up the conventional and makes nirvāṇa permanent; that is the meaning. There, the means for taking up the conventional are stated: "not with the hand." "By reversing" means in order to protect it. "Strength" means capacity.

Now the stage that teaches the characteristic of nirvāṇa alone, namely the generation of the awakening mind and so on, is stated. Camphor is the awakening mind. Nairātmyā is the avadhūtī, and the bliss born of her is also Nairātmyā. That bliss, the nature of the selflessness of all phenomena, is the Great Seal (Mahāmudrā). That which becomes supremely clear is the accomplishment of the Great Seal, whose nature is great bliss.

To the thought, "Where does it abide?", it is said: "in the navel maṇḍala itself." In that navel maṇḍala, the hub of the lotus of great bliss, it gathers the essence of great bliss and abides there in an indestructible way. What arises from that which abides with the nature of embracing it is also "of the nature of the first vowel": the meaning is that, by the nature of A and the nature of non-arising, it is the cause of all and has that nature. "That very thing the buddhas conceive in the mind": this is the expression, and it is the great self-aware being itself. That is one's own wisdom, and that is the Blessed Lady (Bhagavatī): she is endowed with qualities such as lordship, and she destroys the māras such as the afflictions.

"There the yogin should generate": that and its nature are the cause of all; the cause of all, either because it is bound by it or because it is that. "Because it is to be generated by the yogin" means it is to be completed. Completion, too, is the enjoyment of its bliss in an indestructible way. For that very reason, the yogin enjoys by way of the forms and so on of wisdom and by way of the dharmadhātu. That itself also gathers all the heroes and the sovereign heroines of the maṇḍala-wheel.

That also is by the nature of the five wisdoms. To the thought, "How?", it is said: it is the nature of mirror-like wisdom, the cause from which arises the wisdom in which all phenomena are like reflections, and so on. It also pervades or gathers by way of great bliss, whose nature is the equality of all phenomena. It is also discriminating, arising from the mode of nondual, self-aware great bliss; there is nothing more truly real than this. It is also the accomplishing of activities: because the accomplishing of the activities of all sentient beings arises from it, it acts for the benefit of beings; or else it makes sentient beings realize themselves as indivisible from its nature. It also, by way of Vajrasattva, is stainless; it is the very mode of great bliss whose nature is illusion, and that utterly pure dharmadhātu is the lord of all. That is why it is said: "having the nature of the dharmadhātu." That is the nature of the Perfection of Wisdom. "Dharmadhātu" means the ultimate nature of the dharmadhātu, of the essence, of selflessness, of the true limit, and of emptiness.

Now, in order to explain the saṃvara taught in the chapter of the vajra family, Vajragarbha asked, beginning with "of the wheel." "Abiding in the center of the yoginī's body": the seed A of the yoginī Nairātmyā, abiding in the center of the body, is itself the saṃvara, which gathers in the outer mode. That is why it is said: "As outside, so within." That is, what in the outer maṇḍala transforms from A is Nairātmyā, and her purification is in the center of the body. Alternatively, since all outer and inner phenomena are of many kinds, it is saṃvara because it gathers them together.

"Vajra bliss": whatever bliss arises through union together with the means of the vajra is vajra bliss. It is the great seal (mahāmudrā), genuine wisdom. "Vajra sense base": the vajra is Hevajra, and its sense base is the seed of the place of generation, that is, the means. "By this, secret" means in secret, through the union of means and wisdom. "Outer, two by two" means that it does not teach the outer union of means and wisdom. The meaning is that it is not taught by a means that merely points to what is to be accomplished.

Now he states the summary of the four bodies, with "the three bodies." "The full knowledge of the three bodies": the nature in which the three bodies are indivisible is the body of the wheel of great bliss, the result of the three bodies. "Birthplace" means nearness to the birthplace, as in:

"All sentient beings without exception,
the birthplace in which they are born."

"There" means near it.

Now he speaks by setting out the results. Evaṃ is the wheel of emanation, which is the result concordant with its cause. He states the meaning of the results with "experiencing karma." One experiences the result of karma. If one asks how one experiences the result of karma, he says: "As it was done, so it is experienced." The meaning is that the result is experienced in conformity with the action, such as urging and so forth. Maturation is so called because it is the reverse of that: although the wheel of Dharma is a slight action, it bestows a great result of action, and so it is the reverse of that. The wheel of enjoyment is the result produced by personal effort. Because it has become the wheel of great bliss, and in order to attain the result of great bliss, it arises from the especially exalted path. Great bliss is the result of stainlessness, complete perfection itself.

Connecting this with the four schools, he says: "The Sthaviras in the wheel of emanation." Now, to show that buddhahood is not lacking, that sentient beings possess the nature of buddhahood, he says "the schools, the body," and so on. "In the birthplace, free from attachment": because even in the state of the birthplace one does not become attached. "Palms joined at the crown": because at the time of birth one is born with the hands joined at the crown. "The activity of beings" means the activity proper to beings themselves. "Sound is mantra" is the recitation of the mantra whose nature is means and wisdom. Nakedness is being without clothes and not grasping. "By this assembly": earlier one realized buddhahood, and now one also realizes the assembly, so there is no doubt. Having heard that one is a buddha by nature in this way and that, one marvels; and fainting, when filled with wonder, is melting into the form of the awakening mind. Fear comes because the city of things is demolished and gathered in. "They began to tremble" because they are possessed of movement. "If sentient beings too are pure by nature and, being endowed with the assembly, are buddhas, why do I wander in saṃsāra?" The meaning is: "I am falling away from the Dharma body."

Now the Blessed One, in order to raise them up, praises them as well. "Earth, water, fire and wind": the elements are of the nature of earth and the rest. "You" means you all. "Vessel" means the vessel itself. "Goddess" means the goddesses. "Listen" means "listen!" "Elaboration" means by elaboration. "That itself" means suchness. "I" means from me. In "What no one knows," "what" is suchness. "Having heard the Blessed One's speech as in a dream, all of them regained life" means: having heard the Blessed One's speech, which is like an illusion, they regained their bodies by drawing the life wind firmly together.

Then the Blessed One spoke in particular. "Sentient beings" means: therefore, having heard this without distinction, do not turn back from liberating sentient beings.

The yoginīs said: "Blessed One, these things are truly so; they are not false."

The Blessed One said: "By eating a potent poison one faints." "Those who are without mind" means: because they do not know the reality of poison. One who knows that reality:

"Rid of delusion, mind set on that reality,
by that one fully cuts it off."

Its supreme cutting off is its fainting. Therefore it is also a means: by fully knowing that means, sentient beings are of the nature of buddhas, and yet, though endowed with the assembly, they are not awakened, just like one who has fainted from eating poison. The meaning, therefore, is that working for the benefit of sentient beings is not in vain. Having shown the example through that very point, then in order to apply it in the same way to the meaning, he says: "If one knows the means of nirvāṇa." "If each realizes it for himself" means that there is no object at a level beyond the knowledge that is innate by nature; that too is self-awareness itself brought to its culmination.

Seeing that the mudrās were improper, Vajragarbha asked: "Earth is known as Pukkasī." In answer comes "without body": the meaning is that, since body, speech and mind depend on one another and are indivisible in the ultimate, there is no fault.

Now the torma is asked about, with "in union": abiding in the union of means and wisdom and dwelling in great bliss, he asked, and the Blessed One said: "Having summarized the form of Evaṃ." "Taught by one who has become Vajrasattva" refers to giving: it is known through this statement of the benefits of giving torma. The meaning is: "by this torma, in performing all activities."

Asking about the meaning of the mudrās not yet stated, he says: "By which the khecarī." "The mind abiding in the middle" is liberation from both entity and nonentity.

Now he states the sequence of the Tathāgata families, with "the families." "Extensively" refers to placing Vajrasattva. After that, "in three and in five": Vajrasattva having entered into Akṣobhya, one meditates in five aspects. Pride and jealousy are gathered into attachment, so there are three families. Delusion and attachment too are included within aversion, so there is one family.

Moreover, since it enters into the Vajrasattva described earlier, it is inseparable from Vajrasattva. This concludes the explanation of the fourth chapter of the second part, the summary of the meaning of the seals of all the tantras.

So it says. The request for the garland mantra is the passage "Having closely examined the vajra." The Blessed One said: "That which brings women under control," and so on. The extraction of the mantra is taught: "The first of the Vedas" is oṃ. "Adorned with half-moon and drop" states its nature.

Now comes the request about the procedure for drawing the maṇḍala for empowerment into the Hevajra maṇḍala: she addressed him, "Blessed One," and then come the words "Pleased with that." The Blessed One said: "The Teacher there, of great wisdom," which means that the maṇḍala is drawn in the form of Hevajra.

"Heard" means the vessel of the lotus. "The eight vidyās too are great bliss" means they are the very cause of great bliss. The twelve-year-old girl is the purity of mind. The sixteen-year-old is the purity of speech. The twenty-year-old is said to be the purity of body. To the thought, "Who are those eight vidyās?" comes the line "are not taught."

To confer empowerment on them, the entry of the disciple is taught: "afterward." "As has been taught" means as taught earlier in the Compendium of Reality and other works. "By the divisions of the master and so on" means in the order given in the line, "Master, secret, wisdom, and the fourth likewise." Here, by the master empowerment one should take the water, the crown, the garland, the vajra, the bell, the name, the vow, the prediction, the encouragement, and so on.

"Made clear at the end and at the very end" refers to the supreme end, the innate, because it is made clear in this tantra. To make that very point clear, the question is asked: "What is that moment like?" The Blessed One said: "Without beginning or end."

"With one's own right and left hands": the right hand is method, and the left is wisdom. The "elder" is the thumb, and that is the vajra. The ring finger is the source of phenomena. That these two are to be pressed by the yogin is the phrase "on the two channels of enjoyment." "Like the delight of a young maiden" means it produces the great bliss never before experienced. Of that very thing it is said, "It is the nature of Heruka," as in the lines:

The supreme end, and the middle free of extremes,
Empty and not empty, is Heruka.

This is the explanation of the fifth chapter, called the Arising of Heruka, from the Two Examinations.

The Blessed One, holding the nature of the seal of nirvāṇa, taught the sixth chapter, on the rite of the painted scroll mentioned earlier: "Tightly embraced by the goddess." The purification is taught: "With the seal of the five buddhas" means "seal" in the sense of the nature of the five buddhas, Akṣobhya and the rest. The rite of the painted scroll, which this chapter expounds, is requested: "By what rite" refers to the skull cup and so on. "By bodily action" means by conduct, that is, the one who paints and that by which one paints.

Of these, first the action is taught: "The painter who holds the pledge" means that it should be painted by a painter who possesses the pledges to be guarded and the pledges to be eaten. The practitioner himself, having become such a one, should also paint. Those two are taught. The fierce maṇḍala is one endowed with blessing. The rite is taught: "dwelling in the skull." "By whom is the thread to be spun?" means that whichever wisdom consort spins the thread, and whoever weaves it, these too should be women who hold the pledge. To the thought, "How is it to be done by one who possesses the pledge?" it is said, "The yogin blessed with the pledge," meaning that having first performed the yoga, it is done through the pledge and the blessing. Again the painter's action is taught: "Month by month" points to the fourteenth day, and the eighth day is to be understood as well. Therefore one should paint on those two days of the month.

This is the explanation of the sixth chapter, on the rite of the painted scroll of Hevajra, from the Second Part.

Now the rite of the book, to be done first, and the rite of the feast to be performed are taught: "Then the goddess asked him." "Make ink with great honey" means to make a mixture with great ghee and to make the ink with that.

The feast gathering is taught: "Listen, goddess." Because it brings relief, it is a charnel ground; the feast is held there. A mountain valley is an interior covered by rivers, creepers and so on; that is, in the wheel of great bliss. "In a deserted town" means in the abode of the buddhas and the vajraḍākinīs. "At the shore of the ocean": because of its unfathomable depth, its undisturbed calm and its inexhaustibility, the ocean is the Blessed One Hevajra, and the feast is at his shore. Gathering these feasts of the aggregates and so on, one enjoys bliss through great bliss itself.

On "There the seats are to be arranged": "there" means in that assembly. The seat there is the support. "Nine" means the five aggregates and the four elements. To the thought, "How are they to be arranged?" it is said, "in the form of corpses," that is, in the manner of selflessness. "Tiger skin": the tiger, because it grasps distinctions, is conceptual thought. Its skin is the covering by the object of focus set up through error. "Charnel cloth": the charnel ground is the aggregates, and its cloth is what covers them. "Hevajra in the center" means Hevajra should be placed in the center of that circle. "Then place the yoginīs" means then, after him, the yoginīs are placed. "Dwelling" means dwelling after Hevajra. "Enjoy upon the tiger skin" means to enjoy having made it non-appearing. "Pledge" means the pledge substance. "Vegetable": the mālati is selflessness, and its indranaṃ is what makes it blaze. That very thing is to be enjoyed and tasted. "Endowed also with the king's rice" means great bliss. "Enjoying and enjoying" means great bliss. Worship means pleasing it.

"Mātṛkās" means the vajraḍākinīs. "Mother" is wisdom, which gives birth to beings. "Sister" is the sister who discerns and teaches. "Dyer" means one who washes all sentient beings with great bliss. The mother-in-law is so called because she brings about love. "One piece" means non-duality. The skull cup is great bliss. "With good liquor" means with the awakening mind. "To the guru" is because he is the vajra holder. "Give" means to entrust, meaning to make it his nature. "Having bowed" means the mudrā, made by the practitioner of Vajrasattva who becomes such and such. "Oneself too" means in that manner. "Drink" means to enjoy. "To be tasted" means all phenomena. "With the lotus hand" means with the hand which is method itself, which dwells at the center of the lotus and takes up all phenomena. "Give" means generosity, the emission of the awakening mind.

This concludes the explanation of the seventh chapter of the Second Part, the chapter on the rite of the feast gathering.

The chapter that teaches the characteristics of the Great Seal (mahāmudrā), the words of aspiration that are to come first, and the sequence for disciplining those to be disciplined begins with "Then ... asked."

One might wonder: since the Great Seal is inexpressible, how can it be expressed? To this it says, "By a form of conventional aspect." Here "the Great Seal" is Māmakī of the vajra family. As it is said:

The Great Seal is Māmakī,
The action seal is Locanā,
The pledge seal is Tārā,
The Dharma seal is Pāṇḍaravāsinī.

"Not white" means that she is green in color. "Than an ordinary one" refers to what the treatises on desire and other works describe as the padminī. Likewise it is said:

Neither too tall nor short,
Neither too fair nor dark,
Neither stout nor thin,
She conceals faults,
Knows the arts and keeps the mantras secret,
Knows the signals and the mantras of entry,
Truly delights in music and song,
Truly knows song and music,
Supple and ever glowing,
Soft of hand and foot,
Gentle of speech, a lovely padminī,
Green, with a moon-like face, her menstrual blood
Sweet like the fragrance of the lotus,
Shaped like a lotus petal,
With the neck of a deer and the belly of a deer,
Without body hair, full and fleshy,
Her bhaga like a tortoise's back.

"Obtaining her, one has accomplishment" refers to the accomplishment of the Great Seal. Her nature is taught: "Having the nature of innate joy," that is, having the nature of great bliss that is uninterrupted and inexhaustible.

Now the making of the aspiration of the Hevajra yogin is requested: "Then Nairātmyā asked."

Now the means of disciplining beings is requested: "Difficult to discipline." The one who leads those to be disciplined to enter is someone endowed with the good fortune of the tenet system of the Hevajra Tantra. "Teach them the Vaibhāṣika" means teaching the nature of the five aggregates and so forth. "Sautrāntika" refers to the sūtras of the Great Vehicle. "Yogācāra" is the teaching that everything is consciousness. "Madhyamaka" is the emptiness that is empty of the positions of existence, nonexistence and the rest. "All the methods of mantra" means in the manner of the kriyā, caryā, yogottara and yoginī tantras. Thus, if one takes it up, then by having embraced its benefits, the disciple will at that time attain accomplishment.

This concludes the explanation of the eighth chapter of the Second Part, the chapter on those to be disciplined and so forth.

Now, to show that the killing mantras, fire offerings and so forth taught earlier are not to be performed in the slightest exactly as stated, but rather that one who has gained power should, out of great compassion, turn back by other means those persons who are hostile to the guru, who have broken their pledges, and who harm the teaching, the chapter on the characteristics of the casket is taught, its rite having been performed beforehand: "Then," which is to be construed with "of the body of consciousness." Opening that casket means separating it. "By whose characteristic, through consciousness alone, the practitioner": the practitioner is the yogin. The accomplishment is attaining the completion of the opening, or causing that being to be born in the buddhafield of Akṣobhya. The one who harms the teaching is the one to be worked upon, for the sake of the accomplishment of the Great Seal. "The one to be worked upon" means that, placing before oneself the one who is to be punished, one should slay him out of great compassion. Having become wrathful through the yoga of Hevajra, one should draw him out from the channel at the navel with the hand of meditative concentration.

Another rite is taught: "The face should be visualized downward." As for its basis: by the rite, one separates the body from the deity, and with the face turned upside down, one visualizes the target vomiting blood. From the seed syllable raṃ at the heart there blazes something like a terrifying fire, and one should visualize the body burned up to the crown of the head. Imagining in the anal passage a blazing fire in the form of a needle, one recites the mantra oṃ ghuḥ svāhā.

The power of the tantra is taught: "In this tantra."

Now the Blessed One teaches purity. Because all phenomena are pure, it is taught for the time being: "Great secret." "Tangled with garlands of flame" means blazing. In "If pierced, it grants desires," the desires are the aims one longs for and so forth.

Now the extraction of mantras is taught: "The extraction of mantras." Vairocana is oṃ. The fourth of the sibilants (ūṣman) is ha. Pukkasī is u. The empty is the dot, the anusvāra. The lord of letters is oṃ. Khecarī is aṃ. The second of the first is kha. The third of the second is ja. The Water Lady is i. The sovereign of letters is oṃ. The third of the fifth is ba. The ḍākinī is Vajraḍākinī, that is, u. The chief of letters is oṃ. The family of delusion is that same syllable. The first of the first is ka. The second of the semivowels is ra. The third of the semivowels is la. Caurī is a. The third of the fourth is da. The fourth of the semivowels is va. Pukkasī is u. The second of the fifth is pha. The first of the third is ṭa. The fourth of the fifth is bha. The first of the semivowels is ya. Ghasmarī is o. The inner Gaurī is i. The third of the fifth is ba. Fire is ra. The first of the fourth is ta. Vetālī is ai. The Devourer is o. Vajrā is a. The first of the fifth is pa. Nairātmyā is a. The first of the seventh, in their proper order, are a ka ca ṭa ta pa ya śa. "Two kṣa" means two together with ra, that is, rakṣa rakṣa. The third of the sibilants is sa.

This concludes the explanation of the ninth chapter of the Second Part, the chapter on the extraction of mantras.

The division of the rosaries for carrying out the activities of the extracted mantras, and the division of the pledges as well, are taught in the tenth chapter: "Then Vajrasattva spoke." The support for recitation is the rosary. "Lungtang" means the fruit of the lungtang tree.

This concludes the explanation of the tenth chapter of the Second Part, the chapter on recitation.

The request concerning the conventional families of the tathāgatas is the passage "Embracing closely." In "the form of the families," the families are color and shape. "The family of the sattva" is the family of Vajrasattva. "For that reason one should not disparage people" means that beings certainly belong to one or another of those six families. "Hear the worship of the goddess" means how the Perfection of Wisdom is to be worshipped. "With a hand marked with liquor" refers to binding and so forth.

"Action" means through the union whose nature is the nonduality of the natures of skillful means and wisdom. "Possessing the vajra" means with the especially noble vajra. "The litter and the elbow": the man tightly embraces the consort's neck with both his hands, and the placing of the two elbows at the woman's sides is held to be the litter. In it the two legs are extended, but not overly extended. The placing of the two soles of the mother's feet on the thighs of the seated man is the elbow. This is the explanation of the eleventh chapter of the second part, the chapter on the meaning of the coemergent union.

At the time of empowerment, for the empowerment of the master and the others, in their proper order, the guru is to recite verses, and the blessing of the vajra and the lotus is also taught. Then comes "the one with the vajra." For the four empowerments there are four verses. In "You are your own father," the father is Vajradhara. "You are your own" means that one abides as his nature; the meaning is "because one has found that wisdom." "Navel" means A; the meaning is "the center of the lotus of the mouth." This completes the explanation of the twelfth chapter of the second part.

The commentary on the glorious Hevajra called Padminī, composed by the master Saroruhavajra, is complete.

It was translated, edited and finalized by the Indian preceptor, the great scholar Kīrtigarbha, and the chief editor and translator Khutön Ngödrup. Later, at the hermitage of Taklung in Tsé, it was checked once more against the commentary in accordance with the new terminology and finalized.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

Much of the opening glosses plays on the Sanskrit words of the tantra's first line (evaṃ mayā śrutam ekasmin samaye … vijahāra), for example "heard" (śrutam) read as "dripping" and vijahāra split into vi and jahāra. These readings depend on the Sanskrit and are not obvious from the English. The channel names follow the Hevajra Tantra's list of thirty-two channels. "The Embodied" and "the Excellent" are given the Sanskrit forms that these Tibetan words usually render (Rūpiṇī, Bhadrā). Their exact match to the tantra's own list (where Divyā follows Sūkṣmarūpā) is uncertain.  This passage continues the commentary's explanation of the thirty-two channels named in the Hevajra Tantra. The list as given here differs slightly from the familiar one: no names corresponding to Uṣmā or Surūpiṇī appear, and the text is translated as it stands. The channel rendered "the Measure" corresponds to Tibetan tshad ma; it likely stands for the name usually given as Pravaṇā, but because the correspondence is uncertain, the Tibetan has been translated literally. In the verse on the movement of breath, the line mapping the daṇḍa onto "enjoyment and emanation" is compressed in the Tibetan. The final sentence on the three hūṃs is terse and has been rendered literally.  "The mantra for purifying the ground": the Tibetan reads ས་སྤྱོད་པ, but the gloss explains "purifying" (སྦྱོང་བ), so སྦྱོང has been read for སྤྱོད. In the vajra-axe rite, བཟས ("eaten") has been read as བཟླས ("recited"), giving "having first recited ten million times," which matches the parallel instruction that follows in the next piece. "Stone fiber" renders རྡོ་རྒྱུས literally, since its exact substance is uncertain; "axe-cutter plant" likewise renders literally a plant name I cannot identify with confidence. Vairocana, Akṣobhya and Brahmā's seed are coded names for ritual substances, as is usual in this tantra.  The text reads "from hūṃ la sa" in the account of the crossed vajra; this is taken as an evident slip for "from hūṃ" (ཧཱུཾ་ལས). Likewise ར་བཞེས་པ is read as ར་བ་ཞེས་པ, "fence." "Vajra water" is taken as urine, as the following sentence confirms. "The vajra star" renders རྡོ་རྗེ་སྐར་མ literally; the rite described is a prognostication using a young girl as medium. Āli and kāli are the Sanskrit vowel and consonant series.  "A peak of the joys" renders the Tibetan sgang cig literally. It plausibly refers to the culmination of the series of joys, though the reading is uncertain. The doubled "and so forth" after "there is no mind" in the Tibetan appears to be a copyist's repetition and has been rendered once. Vajraśṛṅkhalā is the standard Sanskrit form of the consort's name as given here.  The commentary works through Sanskrit etymologies behind the Tibetan: "ārolika" as "without dispute," mantra from mind (manas), and bhaga as "vanquishing," which is how the Tibetan renders the word. "Śiva" here translates ཞི་བ ("the Peaceful"), the Hevajra Tantra's equivalent of Sanskrit śiva. "All" (sarva), "Reality" (tattva) and "the Fully Awakened" (vibuddha) are the tantra's alternative names for Ratnasambhava, Amitābha and Vajrasattva. The phrase "What is stated here is the reasoning" renders the somewhat elliptical གསུངས་བ་རིགས་པ.  The phrase rendered "so that one may find what is fitting for carrying out the conduct" reads as written "spyod du rdung ba" ("beating to practice"), which makes little sense; it is translated as if it read "rung ba" ("fitting, suitable"). "Sattva and the rest" takes "snying stobs la sogs pa" as the three guṇas (sattva, rajas, tamas). The opening lines refer to the "outcaste woman" (ḍombī/caṇḍālī) of the preceding root verse.  The quoted word rendered "kurukulle" probably stands for the opening of the Apabhraṃśa song in Hevajra Tantra I.6 rather than the goddess Kurukullā; it is kept as spelled. "Pholong" is an obscure word in the sign list, left in phonetic form. The phrase rendered "killed by strangling" (literally "stopped and killed") is uncertain. "Realize" reads རྟོག་པ as རྟོགས་པ.  "The rite of separating the union" renders a terse phrase whose exact sense is unclear. "The wheel of the channels" may alternatively mean "the root circle." "What falls" renders a single cryptic word glossed by the commentary as earth.  The phrase rendered "she is white" reads "white like blang" in the Tibetan; the word blang is obscure here and its comparison has been left out rather than guessed. "Āli" and "kāli" are the technical names for the vowel and consonant series; they are kept as such. The Lower One and the Upper One are literal renderings of the goddess epithets as glossed here. The Upper One is identified in the text as Khecarī. The Lower One is presumably her counterpart Bhūcarī, but the text does not name her.  The word rendered "Now" at the start of the paragraph on the definite place of the innate reads ང ("I") in the text, which makes no sense here; it is taken as a slip for ད (da, "now"). The phrase rendered "the deity that is selflessness (Nairātmyā)" may refer specifically to the goddess Nairātmyā, Hevajra's consort, or simply describe the deity as selflessness. In the gloss on "All is known," the word དབྱེར is read as the evident "indivisible" (དབྱེར་མེད). The phrase ཕུལ་དུ་བྱུང་བས་དང is taken simply as "the excellent."  The commentary glosses lines of the Hevajra Tantra in the order the root text gives them. Several readings are uncertain. "Draws near" renders འདབས, which is obscure here. "Without the continuity of any state being broken" reads འཆིང as འཆད, which fits "uninterrupted." སྤྲས་ས, rendered loosely as "plants," is uncertain. "The Beloved" renders འདོད་མ, possibly standing for the Blessed Lady (Bhagavatī); the preceding མི is not clear and has been left untranslated. "Abandoned" reads སྤགས as སྤངས. "Not equal even to a sixteenth part" reads མེ་ཕོད as the familiar idiom "not worth a sixteenth part."  The lemma "The yogin should rely on them" reads བསྟན་པར་བྱ ("should teach") in the Tibetan; the commentary's own gloss immediately after has བསྟེན་པར་བྱ ("should rely on"), so the latter is translated. ངས་པར is read as ངེས་པར ("certainly"). "Inner tier" renders ནང་གི་བརྩེགས་མ, which is uncertain.  "Contemplate her in the sky as one who knows" renders ནམ་མཁའ་ལ་ནི་ཤེས་པར་བསམ literally; the sense is likely that the goddess is visualized in the sky as aware of, or witnessing, the rite. "Rubble" renders གསེབ་མ, whose exact sense in this list of ground impurities is uncertain. "Vajraratna" renders རྡོ་རྗེ་རིན་ཆེན, which in this series of supplications corresponds to the jewel family (Ratnasambhava).  "Moyoba" (Tibetan mo yo ba) in the list of woods for the tooth stick is unidentified; it is left as a transliterated name. "Size of a cowhide" takes ba ko as cowhide (Skt. gocarma), the usual measure for such a smeared patch of ground. "Joy free of joy" renders dga' bral, the joy of cessation (viramānanda).  "With water twice the measure of the maṇḍala" renders a terse phrase whose exact sense, perhaps the vase's capacity or size in proportion to the maṇḍala, is uncertain. "The quarters of Fire and the Rākṣasa" are the southeast and southwest.  The root-text phrase quoted as "Even where supreme joy is absent" is a brief lemma. Its exact construction in the Tibetan root tantra is uncertain, and it may be meant as "In supreme joy there is no ...". The commentary's gloss, that nothing to be meditated on can be found in the nondual gnosis of joy, is translated as it stands. The glosses on mantra (knowing and protecting) and maṇḍala (taking up the essence) follow the traditional etymologies of the Sanskrit words.  "Evenly means looking downward and to the sides": the Tibetan reads བལྟ་བསམ་ཡིན་ན, which does not parse well; it is taken as a slip for བལྟ་བ་ཡིན་ནོ. "The objects on which the student trains" renders སློབ་མའི་གནས, which may be a slip for སློབ་པའི་གནས, "objects of training"; the root verses describe practicing the gazes on trees and similar objects. "The two fruits" is quoted from the root text and probably refers to the eyeballs. In the fire-rite verses, ཁ་ཁྱེར, rendered "with their ends joined," is uncertain.  The kindling-wood list includes a tree written kasmadya, which I could not identify with confidence; it is given as spelled. "Soba rice" renders a grain named so ba, whose exact identity is uncertain. The text reads mtho ris ("heaven") for the length of the sticks; I have read it as mtho re, "a span," which plainly fits the context. In the washing of the image, a phrase reading lha la bya bas te ba yi lngas has been read as lha la bya ba ste ba yi lngas, "what is to be done to the deity: with the five products of the cow." "So-and-so" stands for the name of the particular deity being consecrated, which the practitioner inserts. The quoted lines on the preparatory rite and purifying the image in the morning come from the root tantra being commented on.  "Water whose covering has been removed" renders ཁ་རྒྱན་ཕྱུང་བའི་ཆུ, whose exact sense (perhaps water from a vase with its lid or ornament removed) is uncertain. In the killing rite, the line རུས་པ་རྡོབ་ནི་མ་པའི་འདབ is obscure; "bones and the leaves of the mapa plant" is a tentative reading, and "mapa" is left unidentified. "Facing Yama" means facing south, the direction of Yama.  The line "Settled in equipoise, with what comes from a courtesan" is terse in the Tibetan. In this rite it most likely refers to fire taken from a courtesan's house, parallel to the "dancer's fire" in the summoning rite that follows. "Bell ... refers to the fruit" may carry an anatomical sense in this sexual-yogic context, but the word is left as the Tibetan gives it. "Howling" (ngu 'bod) is also the name of the Raurava hell; here the commentary glosses it as saṃsāra.  The sentence "Through that very wisdom, what is destroyed here is drawn together, which is to say that all phenomena are made into this and that" is obscure in the Tibetan; the rendering is literal. Root-text lemmas are quoted as the commentary gives them and may differ slightly from canonical versions of the Hevajra Tantra. In "always abiding as the coemergent," the Tibetan reads "skyed pas," taken here as "skyes pas" in line with the surrounding discussion of the coemergent.  The syllable དལ in "དལ་གཅིག་ཏུ་བརྩོན་པ" makes no sense as written; it has been read in line with the root-text phrase "solely intent on vajra yoga" and translated as "solely intent." The comment "སྟོན་པ་ནི་སློབ་མའོ" is terse; it is taken as glossing the root verb "teach" as referring to the disciple, not the epithet "the Teacher." The four moments are rendered Variety, Maturation, Consummation (vimarda) and Freedom from characteristics.  The Apabhraṃśa words of the vajra song are given exactly as the Tibetan spells them. "The practice of cold" renders the commentary's lemma literally; its exact referent in the root verse is unclear. The clause on expelling the uninitiated ("so that they themselves may be protected from the lower realms") could also be read as guarding against the lower rebirths that they would cause.  The Apabhraṃśa words of the song are transcribed as the Tibetan spells them. "Drinking it in" renders འཐུབ, read as འཐུང "drink," which matches the root text's sense; as written it would mean "be able." The commentary takes "Lord of the Gathering" (gaṇapati) here as Heruka himself, not the god Gaṇeśa. The final remark on the word "certainly" is obscure in the Tibetan; the rendering is tentative.  "Sky ... bird-faced one" likely plays on Sanskrit kha (sky) and khaga or khagamukhā (bird, bird-faced); the exact lemma of the root text is not certain. "Fair-faced one" renders the quoted lemma བཞིན་མཛེས, probably an address to the consort in the root tantra. Saṃvara is glossed here, as the commentary itself does, as "that which gathers" or "binds together," not as a vow.  "Urging" renders བསྐུལ་བ in the gloss on concordant results; its exact reference here is unclear. "The middle" in "the mind abiding in the middle" refers in this tantric context to the central channel (avadhūtī), not the Madhyamaka school.  "The former wheel" renders འཁོར་ལོ་སྔོན་པོ, which literally reads "blue wheel." The commentator's gloss ("just as your wheel, so also mine") suggests the sense "former" or "as before," and that reading has been followed. The name written བྷ་སྨ་རི has been rendered as the standard Ghasmarī, the goddess holding medicine in the Hevajra retinue. "The devouring disease" translates ཟ་བྱེད literally; the precise illness intended is uncertain. "Unobstructed" (ཐོགས་མེད) is taken as the opening word of the verse to be recollected.  "Month by month ... the eighth": the text reads "eight dharmas" (chos brgyad); in context of lunar days this is read as "eighth day" (tshes brgyad). Likewise "me zad" is read as "mi zad" (inexhaustible). The Sanskrit indranaṃ, as spelled, is glossed by the commentary as "that which makes it blaze," suggesting fuel (indhana).
