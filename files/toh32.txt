The Story of the Birth Tales (Jātakanidāna)

This text is the *Jātakanidāna*, known in Tibetan as *Skyes pa rabs kyi gleng gzhi*, or "The Introduction to the Birth Stories" — a foundational narrative frame that precedes and introduces the collected jātaka tales of the Buddha's former lives. Composed in a rich literary style, it opens with a series of similes illustrating the causes of suffering and the nature of the path to liberation, comparing the plight of unawakened beings to a man fallen into filth who fails to reach a lotus-filled lake, or one surrounded by bandits who does not flee to safety. As such, it serves both as a doctrinal preamble on karma, affliction, and the possibility of liberation, and as a narrative setting for the birth-story genre that follows. This work is catalogued as Toh 32 in the Kangyur, situated among the sūtra and narrative literature dedicated to the life and past lives of the Buddha.

Just as, when a man who has fallen into a heap of excrement sees from afar a lake endowed with five colors of lotus flowers, along with a great lake, it is fitting that he make his way there by whatever path leads to it and enter that lake. If he does not go by that path, this is not the fault of the great lake. In the same way, when washing away the stains of affliction, if there exists a great lake of deathless nirvāṇa and one does not enter it, this is not the fault of the great lake of deathless nirvāṇa.

Likewise, when a man surrounded on all sides by bandits has a path of escape available but does not flee quickly, this is not the fault of the path but the fault of the man. In the same way, when a person surrounded and seized by afflictions has available a path to peaceful nirvāṇa but does not seek that path, this is not the fault of the path but the fault of the individual.

Just as, when a man tormented by illness has a physician who can cure that illness, if he does not seek out that physician there for the curing of his illness, this is not the fault of the physician but the fault of the sick man. In the same way, when a person tormented by the sickness of affliction has available a supremely skilled one who can pacify affliction, if he does not seek out that master, this is the fault of the person, not the fault of the master who could destroy the afflictions.

Further, one should reflect thus: just as when something unclean is tied around the neck of a man adorned with ornaments, he happily casts it off and departs, so too should I abandon this unclean body without any regard and enter the city of nirvāṇa.

Further, just as when a man or woman has gone to a place of defecation and, having discarded the excrement, does not pour it into a vessel and carry it away, but rather, finding it revolting, abandons it without any regard and departs — so too should I, without any regard, abandon this unclean body and so forth, and enter the city of deathless nirvāṇa.

Further, just as sailors abandon a leaking boat without any regard and depart, so too should I abandon this body — which leaks from these nine wounds, the nine orifices — without any regard, and enter the city of nirvāṇa.

Further, just as a man who has taken various jewels and joined company with bandits, fearing that his jewels might be lost if he travels that road, abandons the bandits and takes to a good path instead — so too, I, fearing that if I indulge this craving, like a bandit plundering the jewel of the wholesome Dharma of the noble path, might lose that jewel because of this body with its many impurities, should for that reason abandon this bandit-like body and enter the city of nirvāṇa.
