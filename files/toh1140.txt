Praise of the Marks of the Perfectly Awakened Buddha (Samyaksaṃbuddhalakṣaṇastotra)

The Praise of the Marks of the Perfectly Awakened Buddha, known in Sanskrit as the Samyaksambuddhalakṣaṇastotra, is a devotional hymn that celebrates the physical perfection of the Buddha. After an homage to the Three Jewels, it honors the Blessed One as the jewel of Śuddhodana's lineage, worshipped by gods and asuras, and then describes his body feature by feature in rich verse. Soft black curling hair is likened to the cuckoo and the dark-blue bee, the brow to a golden mountainside, and the white curl between the eyebrows to snow, pearls, and moonlight. In this way the poem dwells on the traditional major and minor marks of a fully awakened being, turning each one into an object of praise and contemplation. The work is Toh 1140 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the collection of praises, the hymns of devotion addressed to buddhas and other sacred figures.

In the language of India: saṃmyag saṃbuddhālakṣaṇastotraṃ. In the language of Tibet: Praise of the Marks of the Perfectly Awakened Buddha.

Homage to the Three Jewels.

Worshipped on a vast scale by gods and asuras,
Blessed One endowed with endless, lovely beauty,
Supreme ornament of the lineage of Śuddhodana,
Vastly beautiful like a snow mountain, to you I bow.

You whose beautiful head has hair soft, black and curling, like the cuckoo and the dark-blue bee,
Whose stainless brow is like a golden mountainside, like campaka flowers and realgar,
Whose white ūrṇā hair surpasses snow, silver, kumuda lilies, lotus-root fibre, pearls and moonlight,
Whose ears hang long and are supremely beautiful, the color of refined gold,
Whose eyebrows are stainless and soft, like sapphire, bees and dark clouds,
Whose nose is high and most beautiful, whose cheeks are flushed red as if fully ripened,
Whose lips are like the young bimba fruit, whose jaw is like a lion's, whose rounded chin is beautiful,
Who have teeth even and closely set, a tongue long and supple like the petal of a blooming utpala,
A voice deep and pleasing like the goose, the cuckoo and the kalaviṅka bird,
Speech melodious and stainless like the supreme drums of the gods, like thunder and the voice of Brahmā,
A neck round and full like a conch, a chest broad, full and high,
Stainless shoulders like refined gold, like a stainless golden mountainside,
Arms like the trunk of the elephant of the Heaven of the Thirty-Three, the color of refined gold,
Palms marked with beautifully curling signs: auspicious emblems, hooks, fish and wheels,
Body hairs, each one curling neatly to the right, colored like the body of the bee and the cuckoo,
Like a golden mountain peak covered all over with sprouting rubies,
A navel that curls to the right from its center, like the finest of all rubies,
Private parts withdrawn into a sheath, kneecaps round, even and without bulges,
Thighs like golden plantain trees of jambūnada gold and like golden palm trees,
Calves well formed, gradually tapering, most beautiful and round, like those of the lion and the antelope,
Feet even, joined by webs and supremely beautiful, like those of the king of geese,
A gait slow, firm and unwavering, moving beautifully like the stride of the lion and the elephant,
Light rays streaming forth like a surface suffused with the red of the twilight sun,
A body beautiful and lovely like gold, jewels and crystal,
The body of the Blessed One that moves to the right, like the stainless sun and the rainbow,
Feet firm on the bases of miraculous power, flanks beautiful with right diligence,
Blessed Lion, worshipped, whose feet are honored by the lords of gods, of nāgas and of men,
You who were unmoved by the sidelong glances of Māra's daughters, firm as a mountain in samādhi,
Not felled by the weapons of heroes, before whom the great lords press their crowns: to you I bow my head.

Thus I have offered praise to the Blessed One.
By whatever virtue I have gathered through this,
May all beings swiftly attain buddhahood.

This completes the Praise of the Marks of the Perfectly Awakened Buddha, composed by the great poet, the master Mātṛceṭa.

Two descriptive terms are uncertain. In "cheeks flushed red as if fully ripened," the Tibetan phrase is ambiguous: it could also be read as "like full eyebrows," which fits the context poorly. "Chin" renders an uncommon word whose exact sense is uncertain. The line about the body that "moves to the right" is translated literally; it probably alludes to the auspicious rightward turning or circling of the Buddha's form and radiance.
