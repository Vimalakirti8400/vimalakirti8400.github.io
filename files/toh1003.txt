The Noble Great Queen of Vidyā-Mantras Called Jayavatī (Āryajayavatīnāmamahāvidyārājñī)

The Noble Great Queen of Vidyā-Mantras Called Jayavatī, known in Sanskrit as Āryajayavatīnāmamahāvidyārājā and in Tibetan as Phakma Rikngak kyi Gyalmo Chenmo Gyalwa Chen, is a short scripture centered on a protective incantation personified as the great queen of spells, Jayavatī, "the Victorious One." It opens with the Buddha dwelling on Mount Videha at a time when faithless gods, nāgas, yakṣas, rākṣasas, and many other spirits have grown agitated and are harming monks, nuns, and lay followers devoted to the Three Jewels. Moved by great compassion, the Buddha surveys the four directions, sees lightning, hail, torrential rain, and falling meteors bearing down, and responds by teaching a powerful vidyā-mantra to pacify these threats and protect his followers. It is Toh 1003 in the Kangyur, where it belongs to the class of dhāraṇī texts, the collection of incantations and protective formulas valued for warding off harm and calamity.

The location ལུས་འཕགས་རི is rendered "Mount Videha" on the basis of ལུས་འཕགས་པོ = Videha; the identification of this mountain is uncertain. In the speech of Vajrakrodha, ལུས་སྤྲུལ་པོ is read as ལུས་སྲུལ་པོ (kaṭapūtana), as in the surrounding lists. The speaker called རྡོ་རྗེ་ཁྲོ་བོ ("Vajra Wrathful One") is given as Vajrakrodha. རྡོ་རྗེ་ཕོ་ཉ་མོ is given as Vajradūtī, consistent with the vocative "vajradūti" in her mantra. རྡོ་རྗེ་ལུ་གུ་རྒྱུད is Vajraśṛṅkhalā, matching "vajraśaṃkale" in her mantra.  The spirit classes rendered "speaking spirits" and "killing spirits" are literal translations of Tibetan terms whose Sanskrit originals could not be identified with confidence. "Shadow spirits" likewise renders a term probably reflecting Skt. chāyā. The Tibetan term rendered "Skanda grahas" is the usual Tibetan rendering of Skanda, the child-seizing spirit.  In the lists of protections, ཆད་པ is rendered "punishment" (royal penalties) rather than the glossary's doctrinal sense "annihilation," which does not fit this apotropaic context. The yakṣa count བྱེ་བ་ཁྲག་ཁྲིག་འབུམ་ཕྲག་དུ་མ combines several large Indian numerals and is rendered loosely as "many hundreds of thousands of millions of billions." The number of buddhas, "ninety-nine koṭis," is given as nine hundred and ninety million.  The speaker of the passage from "Whoever transgresses" to "I will turn them back" is not named in the Tibetan. Its humble verbs and the Buddha's reply praising Vaiśravaṇa show it is Vaiśravaṇa addressing the Buddha, so it is rendered in the first person. "Siddhaka" is kept as written. It is evidently a plant whose blossom or head splits into pieces, a simile that other texts usually make with the arjaka.
