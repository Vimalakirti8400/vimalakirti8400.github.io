The Noble Perfection of Wisdom in Five Hundred Lines (Āryapañcaśatikāprajñāpāramitā)

Toh 15 in the Kangyur is the Noble Perfection of Wisdom in Five Hundred Lines, known in Sanskrit as the Āryapañcaśatikāprajñāpāramitā and in Tibetan as the Phags pa shes rab kyi pha rol tu phyin pa lnga brgya pa. It belongs to the Perfection of Wisdom class of sūtras, a body of canonical Buddhist scripture that explores the nature of emptiness and the transcendent wisdom cultivated on the bodhisattva path. Like others in this genre, it takes the form of a discourse delivered by the Buddha — here on Vulture Peak Mountain at Rājagṛha — in which he instructs the Venerable Subhūti on the insubstantial nature of the aggregates, sense bases, and all conditioned phenomena. As one of the shorter Prajñāpāramitā sūtras, it distills these profound themes concisely, sitting within the broader collection of Perfection of Wisdom texts that forms a central section of the Kangyur.

In the language of India: Āryapañcaśatikāprajñāpāramitā. In the language of Tibet: The Noble Perfection of Wisdom in Five Hundred Lines.

Homage to all buddhas and bodhisattvas.

Thus did I hear. At one time the Blessed One was dwelling at Rājagṛha, on Vulture Peak Mountain, together with a great community of monks and a great assembly of bodhisattvas.

Then the Blessed One declared to the Venerable Subhūti: "Subhūti, form is without substance, of base substance, and yet of some substance. Subhūti, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness are without substance, of base substance, and yet of some substance. Subhūti, the eye, forms, and eye-consciousness; the ear, sounds, and ear-consciousness; the nose, odors, and nose-consciousness; the tongue, tastes, and tongue-consciousness; the body, tactile objects, and body-consciousness; the mind, phenomena, and mind-consciousness — all of these are without substance, of base substance, and yet of some substance.

"In this way, because ordinary childish persons do not fully know form as it truly and completely is — as set out in these three chapters — they cling to that form, they affirm it, and they obscure themselves with it. Because they cling to form, affirm it, and obscure themselves with it, they will not attain definite emergence even by way of the vehicle of the śrāvakas or the vehicle of the pratyekabuddhas — let alone by way of the Great Vehicle. Clinging to it, affirming it, and obscuring themselves with it in this way, they experience for a long time the aging and death found among hell-beings, animals, hungry ghosts, gods, and humans.

"Subhūti, likewise, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness — being without substance, of base substance, and yet of some substance — and the eye, forms, and eye-consciousness; the ear, sounds, and ear-consciousness; the nose, odors, and nose-consciousness; the tongue, tastes, and tongue-consciousness; the body, tactile objects, and body-consciousness; the mind, phenomena, and mind-consciousness — being without substance, of base substance, and yet of some substance — and thus this mind-consciousness as set out in these three chapters: because ordinary childish persons do not fully know that consciousness as it truly and completely is, they cling to that consciousness, they affirm it, and they obscure themselves with it. Because they cling to that consciousness, affirm it, and obscure themselves with it, they will not attain definite emergence even by way of the vehicle of the śrāvakas or the vehicle of the pratyekabuddhas — let alone by way of the Great Vehicle. Clinging to it, affirming it, and obscuring themselves with it in this way, they experience for a long time the aging and death found among hell-beings, animals, hungry ghosts, gods, and humans.

"But the wise ones, fully knowing form as it truly and completely is, as set out in those three chapters, do not cling to it, do not affirm it, but rather illuminate it. Not clinging, not affirming, but illuminating — because of that, they will attain definite emergence even by way of the Great Vehicle; let alone by way of the vehicle of the śrāvakas or the vehicle of the pratyekabuddhas."

Not clinging to it, not affirming it, but illuminating it — by so doing, one does not experience aging and death among hell-beings, animals, hungry ghosts, gods, and humans.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The phrase rendered here as "without substance, of base substance, and yet of some substance" translates the triad དངོས་པོ་མེད་པ, དངོས་པོ་ངན་པ, དངོས་པོ་ཡོད་པ, which is technically and doctrinally challenging. The three terms map roughly onto: lacking inherent entity, having a defective or inferior entity, and having a conventional entity. This triad is distinctive to this particular Perfection of Wisdom text and does not have a straightforward parallel in other Prajñāpāramitā literature. The rendering adopted here reflects the most defensible reading, but scholars differ on the precise doctrinal import.

The phrase གསལ་བར་བྱེད་དོ, rendered "illuminate it," could also mean "make it clear" or "elucidate it." In context it appears to denote a positive cognitive act that stands in contrast to clinging and affirming — namely, seeing the object clearly without superimposition.  The phrase rendered "lord of creation" translates Tibetan dbang phyug, which corresponds to Sanskrit Īśvara, the theistic creator-deity.
