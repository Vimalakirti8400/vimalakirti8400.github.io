The Means of Accomplishment of Vajravārāhī (Vajravārāhīsādhana)

The Means of Accomplishment of Vajravārāhī, known in Sanskrit as the Vajravārāhī Sādhana and in Tibetan as Dorjé Phakmö Drubthab, is a brief tantric practice text devoted to the goddess Vajravārāhī, the "Vajra Sow," a principal female deity of the Cakrasaṃvara cycle. It guides the practitioner through the visualization of the cosmos arising from seed syllables, the elemental maṇḍalas of wind, fire, water and earth, and Mount Meru, then the deity herself, red, naked and fierce, holding a skull cup of blood and a vajra, surrounded by four goddesses, and it closes with her heart and near-heart mantras for recitation. Its colophon says it came from the abode of Vajrayoginī in Uḍḍiyāna and was translated into Tibetan by the great translator Marpa Chökyi Wangchuk. It is Toh 1572 in the Tengyur, where it belongs among the tantra commentaries as one of the many sādhanas, or deity practice manuals, of the Cakrasaṃvara tradition.

In the language of India: vajravārāhīsādhanaṃ. In the language of Tibet: The Means of Accomplishment of Vajravārāhī.

Homage to the Blessed Lady Vajravārāhī.

From the transformation of the syllable paṃ comes a red lotus. Upon it, from the transformation of the syllable yaṃ, comes the maṇḍala of wind, black in color, shaped like a bow and adorned with a banner. From the transformation of the syllable raṃ comes the maṇḍala of fire, red and triangular, marked with blazing flames. From the transformation of the syllable vaṃ comes the maṇḍala of water, round and white in color, marked with a vase. From the transformation of the syllable laṃ comes the maṇḍala of earth, square and green, marked with a vajra. From the transformation of the syllable suṃ comes Mount Meru, square, made of the four precious substances, and beautified by eight peaks.

In its center, from the transformation of the syllable vaṃ, comes a variegated lotus. In its center, from the transformation of the syllable hūṃ, comes a crossed vajra. In its center is the union of the vowels and consonants. In its center, from the transformation of the syllable vaṃ, comes a vajra, and at its hub is the syllable vaṃ.

From its transformation arises the Venerable Lady Vajravārāhī, red in body color, her hair loose, naked, adorned below the waist with a girdle of fragments, and hung with a garland of fifty freshly severed human heads. In the skull cup in her left hand she holds the blood of hostile ones such as the māras. With her right hand, which holds a vajra menacing those who are hostile in every direction, she makes the threatening gesture. She blazes fiercely like the fire at the end of the eon. Delighting in blood and dripping with blood, she is seated upon her seat with the right leg extended.

The Ḍākinī, and likewise Lāmā,
Khaṇḍarohā, and Rūpiṇī.

Her recitation mantra is oṃ vajra vairocanīye hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ phaṭ svāhā. This is the heart mantra.

oṃ sarva buddha ḍākinīye vajra varṇānīye hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ phaṭ phaṭ svāhā. This is the near-heart mantra.

The Means of Accomplishment of Vajravārāhī, which came forth from the abode of the glorious Vajrayoginī of Uḍḍiyāna, is complete. It was translated by the Tibetan translator, the monk Marpa Chökyi Wangchuk himself.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The phrase rendered "adorned below the waist with a girdle of fragments" is obscure in the Tibetan; it seems to describe a lower garment or apron made of pieces, probably bone ornaments, but the exact sense is uncertain. The earth maṇḍala is described as green, as the text has it, though it is more often yellow in such sequences. The four goddesses Ḍākinī, Lāmā, Khaṇḍarohā and Rūpiṇī are listed without further description; they are the retinue goddesses of the Cakrasaṃvara and Vajravārāhī cycle.
