The Treatment of Words Ending in si and the Rest (Syādyantaprakriyā)

The Treatment of Words Ending in si and the Rest, known in Sanskrit as the Syādyantaprakriyā and in Tibetan as Si la sogs pa'i mtha'i bya ba, is a treatise on Sanskrit grammar that explains, step by step, how nouns, pronouns and numerals take their case endings, beginning with si, the ending of the nominative singular. Opening with homage to Mañjunātha and a verse to Tārā, the author promises a concise and clear digest of the longer works on the subject, and then works through stems of every kind, quoting grammatical rules, mostly in the tradition of the Kātantra grammar, and giving full sets of forms in all three genders, with discussion of the finer points and of the views of other grammarians. Its colophon names the author as the learned Mañjukīrti of Vikramaśīla monastery and the translator as the Tibetan monk Palden Lodrö Tenpa. It is Toh 4287 in the Tengyur, where it belongs among the treatises on grammar and language.

In the language of India: syādyanta prakriya. In the language of Tibet: The Treatment of Words Ending in si and the Rest.

She through the vajra of mindfulness of whom
The rocky crags of misfortune born of fear
Plunge headlong into the ocean of destruction:
May that Tārā be pleased with you.

Treasuries of the endings si and the rest, set out at length by the excellent ones,
Are many indeed; and yet, even so,
Having drawn out their essence, I shall make this concise
And clear work for those who earnestly seek its meaning.

O learned ones, cakora birds who hold the night lotus of understanding and are sure of delight,
Whose own minds' darkness has swiftly been dispelled and who have found contentment:
Here this moon, born from the ocean whose very nature is speech, with its white rays,
Free of every turbidity, rises and shines forth in full.

The well-known words shall be explained,
In masculine, neuter and feminine too;
Those with three genders, through connection with quality, action or substance,

Of these, to begin with, the word vipra receives the designation "stem" (liṅga) by the rule "What has meaning, leaving aside root and case ending, is a stem." After it come the case endings. By the rule beginning "si, au, jas," all of si and the rest become applicable; and when one wishes to express the first meaning, it takes the first case ending, by the rule "The first case ending is for the word expressing the meaning of the stem." There, when one wishes to express one thing, the singular ending is si. The i of si is for the sake of pronunciation; it is not a distinguishing marker. In rules such as "si after what ends in ī," when what has meaning is taken, the s of sup is not taken; and when what lacks a marker is taken, what has a marker is not, so others say. The s that stands at the end becomes visarga by the rule "Visarga is to be made for r and s." vipraḥ.

When one wishes to express two things, the dual ending is au. By the rule "One should separate without departing from the order," combined letters are to be separated without departing from their order and made distinct. By the rule "au before o, and before au too," the letter a, before the letters o and au, becomes au, and the following letter is elided. By the rule "A consonant without a vowel is to be carried over to the following letter," whatever consonant lacks a vowel is to be joined to the following letter. viprau.

When one wishes to express many things, the plural ending is jas. The j serves as the marker for the rule "Every jas becomes ī." Now someone may object: "The words paṭu, śuci and the rest are not said to be feminine merely because they stand near a feminine word. Otherwise, when one says 'paṭu śuci,' one would not know whether this is feminine, masculine or neuter, because they are general words. Words that express qualities apply, through their connection with a quality, to what possesses the quality; and it is not only women who possess qualities such as skill, but men too. Therefore, like grāmaṇī and the rest, paṭi and the rest would not take the operation of nadī either." That is not so. Some words are made to apply to another meaning through the nearness of another word: as, for example, in "this ox is a strong one," some words exclude, among words that apply both to that and to other things, their application to the other things. Take "blue utpala": the word utpala expresses the class of utpala, and though it also applies to substances that are white, blue, red and so on, its application to the red and the rest is excluded by the word "blue," which has the same reference; it does not, however, apply to the blue afresh, because it already applies there of itself, and because in that application of its own the reason, the class of utpala, is present. In the same way here too, paṭī and the rest, which through possessing skill apply to the feminine, the masculine and the neuter, have their application to the masculine and the neuter excluded by the feminine word that has the same reference, and they do not apply to the feminine afresh.

Some say that grāmaṇī and the rest have masculine gender of themselves, and that by the rule "from another word it applies to another gender," they become like nadī optionally. The Ṭīkā says that in that case it is simply not something that has the mention of "expressing." For us, the difference is that, since the mention of "expressing" always has the feminine as its object, there is no becoming like nadī by "also of a short vowel before the endings with ṅ."

Now the meaning of the sūtras within the list is to be explained, beginning with "in the sense of relative position." Relative position is a fixed boundary that depends on the word's own referent. A boundary is a limit, and its fixity is that it necessarily occurs, a boundary that has not been destroyed. If one asks what this amounts to: it depends on its own referent. The own referent of words like pūrva is a thing of direction, place or time, and the word depends on that; hence it is "dependent on its own referent." The meaning is that pūrva and the rest, when they are not names and apply only to their own referent in the sense of relative position, are to be understood as words bearing the sarva name. pūrvasmāt is to be understood as "from the later boundary," and parsmad is known from the mantras.

If one asks why it says "in the sense of relative position": dakṣiṇaya gayathakāya dehi, which means "for the skilful one" (prābri). Taking mere brābriṇya as its ground, the word dakṣaṇa applies to the gathaka without depending on anything as a boundary. If one asks why it says "when not a name": uttairāya kurave dehi; even though there is relative position, here it is their name. For the word uttara applies in dependence on the boundary of Jambudvīpa or Mount Meru, and from this the relative position is understood.

"sra, when not denoting a kinsman or wealth": if the word sra does not apply as a name for a kinsman or for wealth, it bears the sarva name. svasmai sutraya svamegabe means "for one's own." Because the words sutra and go are close by here, even kinsman and wealth are understood from the word sva merely as "one's own." If one asks why it says "when not denoting a kinsman or wealth": svayājñātaye, in the sense of "one who is known"; and sayādhanaya, in the corresponding sense. Only when it does not depend on another word does the word sra denote a kinsman or wealth. If it is objected, "In that case the words for kinsman and wealth would not be used alongside it, since they are synonyms," it is not so. Where a word may be suspected of having many meanings, a synonym is added in order to make that meaning clear, as in "hari is the lion" or "pik is the cuckoo."

"antara, in the senses of connection with the exterior and of covering." The exterior is to be described as an unobstructed domain, and connection with it is "connection with the exterior." That too becomes an exterior thing, because it is unobstructed. From "it is put on above, it covers that," samviyate, there is yuṭa in the sense of the object; from "one covers with this," it is optionally in the sense of the instrument. What is close to covering is an under-covering; it may even be without connection with the exterior, and it denotes one thing being covered by another thing. antarasmai grihāya nagrabahāya means "for the house of the outcastes (caṇḍālas) and the like." antarasmai śātrakāya means "for a garment that is covered by another garment, or that is worn beneath." If one asks why it says "in the senses of connection with the exterior and of under-covering": anayur grāmayorantate tapas pratipasti; asminantare śigtani udkani. The word antarī expresses the middle.

If one asks why in antarāyām puri pasati there is not, by "After a sarvanāma" and so on, yam, the su āgam and the shortening of the preceding vowel, arguing that the pūrva that lie outside the wall have connection with the exterior, that this word yantara applies to them too, and that therefore it would follow from the interpretive rule on the mention of a stem: that is true, but one should state "when not pūrva," and therefore yam and the rest do not occur.
