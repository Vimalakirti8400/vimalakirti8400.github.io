Homage to the Three Jewels.

Whatever precious jewel exists in this world, or in those beyond, or in the heavens above, there is none that equals the Tathāgata, who has become the God of gods, the supreme among humans. Therefore this is declared to be the supreme jewel. By this truth, may there be well-being here.

The exhausted, the free of attachment, the immortal, the unconditioned: this the Śākya Sage realized with knowledge and clearly set forth. The deathless, peaceful, not compounded: nothing at all is equal to this Dharma. Therefore this is declared to be the supreme jewel. By this truth, may there be well-being here.

The supreme goal, which the Teacher properly taught, the unsurpassed yoga of the Teacher, constantly accomplished, the concentration that sees the path with two eyes like vajras: nothing exists that equals that absorption. Therefore this is declared to be the supreme jewel. By this truth, may there be well-being here.

The eight great persons, who are highly praised and spoken of as four pairs of individuals, the great sages, persons without equal: the Sugata declared them to be fields worthy of offerings. A gift given to them becomes a great fruit, like sowing seed in a well-prepared field. These are declared to be the Saṅgha, the supreme jewel. By this truth, may there be well-being here.

Those who, with steadfast mind, strive diligently and enter the teaching of Gautama attain the deathless, having reached their goal. Having dispelled the darkness, they attain nirvāṇa. Therefore the Saṅgha is declared to be the supreme jewel. By this truth, may there be well-being here.

At the very moment of joining this view, three afflictive fetters are abandoned all at once: the view of a real self in the aggregates, clinging to moral rules and observances, and doubt. They see the truths of the noble ones. Therefore the Saṅgha is declared to be the supreme jewel. By this truth, may there be well-being here.

Whether by body, speech, or mind, they never commit the three kinds of harmful deeds, and if they do so inadvertently, they do not conceal it; and so, being such, they do not cling to their views. Therefore the Saṅgha is declared to be the supreme jewel. By this truth, may there be well-being here.

Just as a pillar of Indra planted in the earth is not shaken by the winds of the four directions, so too are the noble Saṅgha, those who behold the sublime path of the noble ones. Therefore the Saṅgha is declared to be the supreme jewel. By this truth, may there be well-being here.

Those who meditate on the noble truths, taught so well through profound wisdom, who are intent even on giving their bodies, do not meet the eight fears. Therefore the Saṅgha is declared to be the supreme jewel. By this truth, may there be well-being here.

As fire, with the wind, scatters the fence and cannot be counted when taken in the hand, so the sons of the Buddha, having abandoned all binding attachments, pass beyond all that can be pointed out. Therefore the Saṅgha is declared to be the supreme jewel. By this truth, may there be well-being here.

This is the Tibetan version of the Ratana Sutta (Jewel Discourse), here with a colophon-like opening on a mantra collection. Several lines are corrupt or obscure in the Tibetan, and I rendered them as the verse most plausibly intends. The line on "joining this view" refers to attaining the path of seeing, where the three fetters fall away together. The phrase "the eight fears" is as in the text. The fire simile and the line "pass beyond all that can be pointed out" are difficult and follow the sense of the parallel Pāli verse on the extinguished lamp. The final line breaks off, matching the Tibetan, which ends mid-stanza.  The passage is a protective verse composition. Several names are rendered by identification and are somewhat uncertain. "The Great Lord" (དབང་ཕྱུག་ཆེན་པོ) is Maheśvara, usually Śiva, here taken as the powerful deity. "The conqueror of foes" renders the epithet of the Victor, with the Tibetan "dgra rgyal" read as an honorific. "The Snatcher" is read as Hārītī, the child-snatching yakṣiṇī, though the Tibetan "'phrog ma" is only a descriptive epithet. The generals (sde dpon) are likely the yakṣa commanders. The mantra-holders may refer to vidyādharas. "May my poison become free of poison" follows the Tibetan literally. The closing phrase begins an epithet of a protective figure whose sentence continues in the next passage.
