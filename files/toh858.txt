His discourse turns to a distant world-realm called Boundless Flowers, where a buddha named Supreme Lamp dwells, and the dhāraṇī takes its name and authority from this buddha. In the canon it is Toh 858 in the Kangyur, belonging to the Tantra division, in the class of dhāraṇī and related ritual scriptures.

The Tibetan "mi mjed" renders Sahā, the world of Śākyamuni, rendered here as "Endurance" in the traditional etymology.

The title's Sanskrit is given as in the text. The list of harmful beings includes some obscure terms: sbrang bu lcags phye is rendered as "scorpion" on the likely reading of "iron-powder bee," which is uncertain. The bodhisattva's name Mi pham pa is rendered "Invincible" (Aparājita), though in the Tibetan text the form given is "ma pham pa." The Tibetan text breaks off mid-sentence, and the translation ends at the same point. The Tibetan reads "ཆོངས་ཤིག" and "ཆོས་ཤིག" at different points. I took both as the same verb, "master," on the sense of the context.  The numerals in the Tibetan are given as written: the first set is "eight million times ten thousand (བྱེ་བ་ཕྲག་བརྒྱད་ཅུ་ར་གཉིས)," which I have rendered as eighty-two million to match the preceding passage's rendering for continuity. By the same convention, the later "eighty-four" is rendered here as eighty-four million to remain consistent. Strictly, the Tibetan phrase could be read in larger multiples, so the figures are conventional. The Sanskrit mantra syllables are given in a plain transliteration of the Tibetan spellings, and their original Indic forms are uncertain. In the opening lines, the preceding passage's speaker is Mañjuśrī, so I kept the first-person recitation his, followed by the Buddha's own declaration to the monks.  The passage breaks off mid-sentence, as in the source. I rendered ལྷོག་པ་ and བྱད་ as skin diseases and spells, and རོ་ལངས་ as corpse-raising spirits (vetāḍa). The mantra syllables are given in plain transliteration, and the count of buddhas here (nine hundred and ninety million) differs from the preceding passage's eight hundred and forty million, as the Tibetan states.  Several sentences in the Tibetan are obscure or possibly corrupt. The sequence of counter-rites (killing, cutting by punishment, beating, "the eighth," threatening, the gaze) has been rendered by sense; the term brgyad pa, literally "eighth," is probably a corruption of a word for binding, and I have rendered it as "binding" to fit the escalating sequence. The mantra syllables are given in the Sanskritic transcription as they stand in the Tibetan, with the first element read as daṇḍe, deṇaḍe following the text's da ṇaḍe, de naḍe. The Tibetan sentence about "any other fate" is a negative construction (it is impossible that such a person should come to another fate), which I have kept. In the verses, gru bum is read as kumbhāṇḍa, and srin po as rākṣasa.  In the verse on the four Great Kings, the Tibetan names seem garbled or displaced. "Evil Body" (ལུས་ངན) translates Virūḍhaka, "Ugly Eyes" (མིག་མི་བཟང) translates Virūpākṣa, and "Noble Born" (འཕགས་སྐྱེས་པོ) translates Dhṛtarāṣṭra. "Colorful and Renowned" (ཁ་དོག་ལྡན་ཞིང་གྲགས་དང་ལྡན) is a loose rendering of Vaiśravaṇa. The line "ལུས་ངན་དང་ནི་ཡུལ་འཁོར་སྲུང" is syntactically odd, probably a corruption of the names.
