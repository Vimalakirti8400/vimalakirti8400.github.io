The Guru's Instruction on the Rites of Kurukullā

This brief untitled work, which names itself in its colophon simply as a guru's instruction, is a short ritual supplement on the practice of the red goddess Kurukullā; no separate Sanskrit or Tibetan title is given for it. It teaches how, after reciting Kurukullā's mantra a hundred thousand times for each of its syllables while abiding in her meditative absorption, the practitioner can use that mantra in rites of magnetizing and bringing others under control: consecrating flowers, incense, food and drink, performing fire offerings, and drawing a sixteen-spoked wheel diagram with the name of the person to be won over, which is placed in a wax effigy and warmed over a fire. It closes by urging faithful worship of the goddess and recitation of the king of mantras, and states that it was translated by the Indian paṇḍita Amoghavajra and the Tibetan translator Bari Rinchen Drakpa. It is catalogued as Toh 1314a in the Tengyur, an appendix to the Kurukullā sādhana of Toh 1314, among the tantra commentaries.

The mantra of Kurukullā, oṃ kurukulle hrīḥ svāhā, is renowned, since its power is undiminished, for bringing the three realms under control. Abiding in the meditative absorption described earlier, one should recite it a hundred thousand times for each of its syllables. Then whoever is given flowers, incense, ointment, perfume, powder, clothing or the like, after the mantra has been spoken over them, will be brought under control. If one perfumes oneself with fragrant incense, all people will be delighted. If one performs a fire offering with red flowers and the like in someone's name, that person will come under control. If one consecrates food and drink with the mantra and gives them, the one who receives them will come under control.

When bringing a great man under control, one should first perform a fire offering of white mustard seed. Then, on a favorable date and lunar mansion, having made offerings to the Blessed Lady, one should draw with saffron, bezoar, camphor and the like, on cloth or on birch bark, a wheel diagram with sixteen spokes shaped like utpala petals. In the center of the wheel one should write the name of the man or woman to be accomplished, together with the mantra, and on the petals one should write the name of the one to be accomplished alone, surrounded by the four syllables ku ru ku lle. On the outside, the wheel is encircled three times with the syllable oṃ. It should be wound with red thread and inserted into the middle of the heart of an effigy made of wax. If it is then heated over a fire of acacia wood, it draws the person's feet toward one. When bringing a man or a woman under control, if one pierces both feet, or the private parts, with a copper needle and heats the effigy, there is no doubt that the person will come under control.

Worshipping the Blessed Lady with great faith, one should recite the king of mantras. By such a sequence, everything will be accomplished.

The guru's instruction is complete. It was translated by the paṇḍita Amoghavajra and the translator Bari Rinchen Drakpa.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The mantra in the Tibetan carries a genitive ending on "kurukulle", which is taken here as Tibetan grammar ("the mantra of Kurukullā") rather than part of the mantra. "Draws the person's feet toward one" renders rkang pa 'gugs par byed, which is taken to mean that the person is summoned and comes on foot.
