The Instruction on Nairātmyā (Nairātmakopadeśa)

The Instruction on Nairātmyā, called in Sanskrit the Nairātmakopadeśa and in Tibetan Dakmema'i Mengak, is a short tantric meditation manual devoted to Nairātmyā, the goddess of selflessness who is the consort of Hevajra. Arranged as a series of fifteen brief rites, it guides the practitioner through visualizations of the three channels of the subtle body, the syllables and the bindu, the blazing of the inner fire, union with Heruka, the signs of the dissolving elements, an outer worship and the acceptance of a student, presenting each practice as a way to make the ultimate directly manifest. Its colophon says it came from the coemergent yoginīs of Oḍiyāna and was written down by the paṇḍita Samādhivajra so that he would not forget it, and that it was translated into Tibetan by Devākaracandra and Sertsa Sönam Gyaltsen. It is Toh 1312 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the commentaries and practice texts of the tantra section.

In the language of India: nairātmakopadeśanāma. In the language of Tibet: The Instruction of Nairātmyā.

Homage to glorious Vajrasattva.

Dwelling on the left, named lalanā, flowing with water, the aspect of wisdom that is emptiness;
dwelling on the right, named rasanā, flowing with blood, compassion, whose nature is that of things;
dwelling in the center, the avadhūtī, flowing with the melted essence, of indivisible form, nondual:
in that form of the three channels, let the Blessed Lady protect you!
Nairātmyā pervades to the farthest limits of space.
Having paid homage to glorious Nairātmyā,
in the abode of true bliss I
shall explain the instruction of Nairātmyā.

Here, as regards recitation and meditation: one whose mind is adorned and anointed with the flavor of compassion, who has firmly taken up the precious awakening mind, and who has served and honored the guru and Nairātmyā as inseparable, enters a secluded, undisturbed house of meditation and recites oṃ rakṣa rakṣa hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā. With this he utters the mantra of wrathful speech that dispels obstacles; it protects the place and so forth.

Then, sitting upon a special seat, he anoints it with the five nectars and the like, as far as he is able, and should strew flowers there. He worships the Blessed Lady, present in a painted scroll or the like, with the five offerings, perfumes the place thoroughly with incense made from mahābala, recites the mantra of purification, and should imagine himself to be Nairātmyā.

After that, he should behold Nairātmyā in this way. Her seat is the petals of an emanated wheel, the limb of a chariot, and she has come to rest on the anthers of a four-petaled lotus. She is born from sugandhe upon the disk of the cool-rayed moon at the heart of a corpse. Her hair blazes with light like a blazing fire that has taken on the rays of a ruby. She wears a crown made of a garland of stainless skulls. She is slightly wrathful, with three red, rolling, round eyes. Her body is adorned with a garland of fifty human heads still wet with blood, and adorned with the five lovely mudrās. With her left hand she holds a skull cup full of blood and a banner, and her right hand, holding a curved knife, is like a palāśa branch in full leaf bowed down beneath the weight of its milky sap. At her middle are three folds; she is naked, full-bodied, with her hips set apart, possessed of the nine moods of the dance. She presses down in the dance of the half cross-legged posture, graceful and playful, of lovely youth. From the place where the mind delights, masses of light rays dawn. She has arisen from the gate of Dharma and has the form of true wisdom.

Then, according to the rite, he causes the wisdom being to enter the commitment being, and imagines her eyes, body and the rest to be purified.

Her spoked wheel is the ground of the essence of bliss, the very nature of Vajradhara. Red like a heap of vermilion or like the dawn twilight, it flows down from the wheel with the spokes of great bliss in the essence of the first and supreme. It is born having the shape of a stream of melted essence flowing down from the central channel. Upon the limbs of the vajra stūpa there is dependent origination, the natures of the sense sources, gone to the right, to the left and toward the base below. In twelve places, shaped like a lattice of windows, he should meditate on twelve drops, like clusters of round, clear, red pearls, which show themselves as the moments, the joys and the empowerments. He should meditate on the four places, the very natures of the four doors of liberation, as having the nature of the four vajras, the form of the four letters, and as the four bodies.

Of these, the upper place, in the form of a vajra jewel, giving birth to the body vajra, is like the hare-marked moon in autumn. The left place, in the form of a half moon, causing the sprout of the speech vajra to grow, is like a ruby gem. The right abode, also in the form of a half moon, causing the shoot of the mind vajra to grow, is like a sapphire. The central place, in the form of the source of phenomena, the cause from which the commitment vajra is born, is like the vajra essence of an excellent family; endowed with five rays of light, it should be thought of as the ground of all phenomena.

Then he should meditate that the drops of the moments enter the drops of the joys, the drops of the joys enter the drops of the empowerments, and the drops of the empowerments enter the bodies. After that, with his mind in equipoise, he thinks that the four bodies too, entering one into the next, higher and higher in sequence like a conjunction of the constellations, melt from the central drop and dissolve within.

After that, that very thing is a drop like a rain of nectar, the form of the dharmadhātu, radiating garlands of light. Drawn by the thread of nāda from the syllable of Dharma that has entered the drop, it rises upward along the path of the channel as described, and Dharma and drop together become one taste, like water and milk. He should meditate that the innate, which has the form of the inseparability of emptiness and compassion, transcends the world and is perfect, revolves with the momentum of a spinning potter's wheel.

This, from the Instruction of Nairātmyā, is the rite for meditating on the various drops as one: the first.

Then the mantra practitioner with the characteristics described before enters the house of meditation mentioned before and sits upon a comfortable seat. He recites the protection mantra and also performs the worship of the maṇḍala and so forth, and with oṃ svabhāva śuddhaḥ sarva dharmāḥ svabhāva śuddho'haṃ he should intensely imagine himself to be Nairātmyā.

Then, abiding upon a lotus whose base has the limb of a chariot, upon the disk of the hare-marked moon at the heart of a corpse, he should meditate on himself in the form of Nairātmyā with the characteristics as described, blue, or dark red, or red, whichever he wishes, and should see the wisdom being, the eyes, the body and the rest being blessed.

Then, on her lower spoked wheel, he should meditate on the essence of the first and supreme, whose characteristic is the nature of the space element: a triangular wheel like vermilion or a pomegranate flower, upon which is a powerless fragment of a skull, having the nature of the earth element. Within it he imagines a white ball born from milk, having the nature of the water element. Then in its center he imagines a weapon flashing like lightning, having the nature of the fire element, and at the hub, together with the fire maṇḍala, he imagines a blazing half moon having the nature of the wind element. Above that he beholds the seed syllable, the very nature of the five elements, adorned with a half moon and a drop, possessed of a mass of joys, luminous.

Then, upon that fire maṇḍala, he should meditate on three letters, clearly blazing, which display great passion. Of these, the one in the upper corner is clear as mere mind, empty of the aspect of objects. The one in the left corner produces the fruit that is truly desired. The one in the right corner produces delight. These three letters, moreover, should be understood as established as words that are definitely the causes of the bliss of the preparation, of the main part and of the conclusion.

Then, by the power of being touched by their light rays, stirred by the wind of great passion, just as a woman in love shows her longing, the seed letter with its drop, like a very fine thread at the door of the house it has entered, in the form of nāda in the rasanā, goes upward, or else goes like a mātṛkā; and again, going crookedly, where the cavity of the opening in the throat is the house, he should see Nairātmyā touch the letter haṃ, the very nature of glorious Heruka, which abides at the tip of the hanging uvula.

Then, through the empowerment of the essence of a rain of stainless nectar, the water of joy, dripping from the indestructible drop, whose bliss is the bliss increased by that touch, he should meditate on that Blessed Lady as spontaneously accomplished: fully satisfied, joyful, intoxicated with supreme joy, like a turning spinning wheel.

Further, her three inseparable vajras, her nature as the three bodies, and the inseparability of method and wisdom should be learned from the instructions of the holy guru.

This, from the Instruction of Nairātmyā, is the rite for meditating on revolving in the form of the ultimate letter, which has the nature of the five great elements, the three vajras and the three bodies: the second.

Then the yogin should dispel obstructors through things being just as they are. The rite is this: in a secluded house or the like, he sits comfortably on a soft seat, imagines himself to be Nairātmyā, and recites the mantra of purification. The lower spoked wheel, like the aśoka, the lac tree and the kusuma flower, is a triangle having the limb of a chariot; upon a crossed vajra, on the anthers of a lotus, is a moon. There stands the khaṭvāṅga. Its lower part is adorned with a sharp, single-pointed vajra. Its middle part is marked with a crossed vajra. Its neck part has garlands of tinkling bells hanging down and various other garlands. Its upper part is marked with an excellent five-pointed vajra. That khaṭvāṅga, standing upright, which has the nature of the dharmadhātu, he should see as arisen from the transformation of a seed syllable into the very essence of Nairātmyā.

Then the natures of karma, Dharma, commitment and the great mudrā, in its lower part, its middle, its throat and its upper part, are the gate of Dharma, the fierce one, the ocean of wisdom and the headless one. These he meditates upon in their colors: multicolored, black, red and white. He should meditate on them as blazing fiercely like the mass of fire at the time of the world's destruction, stirred by the karmic wind born from the cessation of sun and moon.

Then, from the tip of the letter ra at the center of the vajra above it, a blaze rises like the fire at the time of destruction, and by its blazing he should cause a turmoil in the manner of burning. He should meditate properly that, upon the wheel of bliss, whose defining characteristic is the gathering of the three wheels into the nature of the body, speech, mind and wisdom of all the tathāgatas of the ten directions, the hosts of buddhas and their consorts who dwell in the wheel, there is a melting like butter melted by a blazing fire.

Then the holders of the dripping nectar are revived; and the hosts of buddhas and consorts, who were burned, take on the form of nirvāṇa and are moistened, satisfied by the drops, just as water falls from a mass of clouds, like an intoxicated peacock. Through extreme joy, satisfied by the desire of the other, having entered great bliss, from the nature of wisdom it spreads out into all the realms of space: that dance, inflamed by the mass of joys. The mantra practitioner whose mind has gone there should meditate on it as spontaneously accomplished for as long as he wishes.

This, from the Instruction of Nairātmyā, is the rite for meditating on the play of the khaṭvāṅga, which has the nature of supreme wisdom: the third.

Then, in accordance with what has been taught about the characteristics of the yoginī, he performs the appropriate protection mantra. In an undisturbed house or the like, on a comfortable seat, sitting locked in the vajra cross-legged posture, he imagines himself to be Nairātmyā and recites the mantra of purification. Then, in the center of an emanated wheel, upon a moon maṇḍala at the ground of the mind of a corpse, he meditates on Nairātmyā with her own characteristics as described before. At her base he should imagine a triangular wheel of fire having the limb of a chariot, like a ripe kimpaka fruit, and within it a four-petaled lotus.

Then on its petals and anthers he should meditate on drops of bliss, fine as mustard seeds, which have the nature of the five ḍākinīs. Of these, on the eastern petal is the drop of the bliss of full union, which has the nature of mirror-like wisdom and of the buddha ground, the nature of Vajrā. On the southern petal is the drop of the root bliss, which has the nature of the wisdom of equality and of the buddha ground, the nature of Gaurī. On the western petal is the drop of the bliss of full delimitation, which has the nature of discriminating wisdom and of the buddha ground, the very nature of Vāriyoginī. On the northern petal is the drop of the bliss of the aim, which has the nature of all-accomplishing wisdom and of the buddha ground, the nature of Vajraḍākinī. On the anthers is the fully purified wisdom of the dharmadhātu and the nature of the buddha ground, the nature of Nairātmyā: on this drop of the supreme, ultimate bliss of all phenomena, the mantra practitioner, his mind in equipoise, should contemplate for a long time.

After that, radiating out from the central drop, he should see the drops of the directions drawn in by the stainless hooks of light rays, four in number, and see them too enter the central drop. Then he should meditate, for as long as he wishes, on the chief drop blazing in the nature of ultimate wisdom, which has the nature of the five wisdoms. Then the red light rays that arise, like the branches of a wish-fulfilling tree, produce whatever aims are desired, and like a fire that kindles, they kindle bliss. Then he carries it to the uṣṇīṣa at the tip of the nose and should imagine that it abides there blissfully for a long time.

Then that drop, like a rope, by a stream of thread-like lines that issue from itself, goes along the path of the rungs of a ladder. Like a precious wish-fulfilling jewel it fulfills every wish; like the disk of the hare-marked moon it pervades the whole of space with masses of light rays whose nature is nectar. It comes to abide at the tip of the nose as the nature of supreme bliss, the very nature of the dharmakāya and the nature of Nairātmyā, and it blazes. The mantra practitioner who has become free of the stain of the faults of conceptualizing an object of meditation, a meditator and a meditation should meditate on it for as long as he is able to remain stable.

This, from the Instruction of Nairātmyā, is the rite for meditating on the drop of supreme bliss: the fourth.

Then the mantra practitioner who possesses empowerment and commitments and has such qualities as compassion gathers the accumulations, and sits upon a seat in a place agreeable to the mind. By the rites of protection and of worshipping the maṇḍala he imagines himself to be Nairātmyā, and on his own emanated spoked wheel he generates Nairātmyā just as her generation was taught before. At its hub, like an opened mandāra flower, is a triangle, the supreme abode of the lord of sages, especially together with the mother. And he should clearly behold, in union, the one whose nature is the first and supreme, luminous, the one of sixteen digits and the lord of the day, like the peg of the mountain of the seven-horsed sun.

What arises from that, displaying itself as initiating, discriminating and enjoying, like a needle, extremely sharp, fine as a ray of the sun, intensely red, he should imagine releasing the union three times and going inside there.

Then he should contemplate the victors born from that union, as if their birth had been brought forth from blazing and joy, as drops of ultimate wisdom.

Then what arises from that, whose nature is that of an emanated line, which is also the lord of the gods, the supreme one who, focused on the bliss of the lord, acts for the benefit of others, moves playfully and gracefully like a she-elephant. Long like a fine, slender rod of red wish-fulfilling jewel, blazing beautifully like a branch of the sāla tree, by means of the image of Nairātmyā he should imagine it touching the body of the Blessed One in headless form, the very nature of glorious Hevajra, who gathers all the tathāgatas and abides in the center of the wheel of great bliss.

Then, stirred by joy, they melt through the passion arising from the touch of the image, and he should enjoy the desire of joy, which has the form of drops that descend and follow in a manner in keeping with joy. Further, he tastes the nectar of bliss that has truly arisen from that union, the very essence, and, from the increase brought by its ripening into strength, gladdened and undiminished, he gathers together the host of excellent and supreme joys. The mantra practitioner whose mind is fixed upon it should meditate, as far as he is able, on all that moves and does not move, dwelling in every direction of space.

This, from the Instruction of Nairātmyā, is the rite for meditating on the drop of wisdom: the fifth.

Then, sitting comfortably in the house of meditation and having done what is to be done, the one who possesses the yoga of Nairātmyā, with the characteristics of the yoginī as they were described, should clearly behold, upon the emanation wheel described before, the blazing letter oṃ, Nairātmyā in form: it has the form whose nature is the gathering of the āli, the vowels; it has the nature of the dharmakāya, the essence of wisdom, the mode of emptiness; it is the mode in which all the yoginīs without exception are gathered. Abiding in the wheel of Dharma, he should meditate on the blazing letter hūṃ, which has the form of the gathering of the kāli, the consonants, the nature of the sambhogakāya, the essence of method, the mode of compassion, the nature in which all the tathāgatas are gathered, the essence of glorious Heruka.

Then, by garlands of light rays arising from them, which like messenger women accomplish the aim desired by both, like the sun, they are drawn together, and right there he meditates on passion. Then again he should meditate for a long time on the passion of union there and the intense attachment, with the precious anther inseparable from it reaching the tip and remaining there.

Then, after that, there is the wind arising from the power of the great shining of the chariot of the seven-horsed sage, the sun, in the place of the mānasa, and there is the melting brought about by its union with fire. The true emptiness of all things, emptiness, and the awakening mind inseparable from compassion, the yogin's own defining nature, like an excellent vase that accomplishes every desired aim, radiates out. By making it appear evenly as a stream of bliss, appearance and what appears, all sentient beings without exception, are made into vessels. The mantra practitioner whose mind has gone into that form of the drop should contemplate this.

This, from the Instruction of Nairātmyā, is the rite for meditating on the innate, unchanging letter as a drop: the sixth.

Then the yogin of Nairātmyā generates himself mentally as Nairātmyā upon the emanation wheel, as before. On that emanation wheel he meditates on a lotus with four petals inside and ten outside. Then on its anthers he should meditate on the first vowel together with a drop, the essence of Nairātmyā, which has the nature of consciousness, mind and aversion, of the buddha ground, and of fully purified wisdom; and on the petals he should meditate on the fourteen vowels radiating from it, which have the nature of the fourteen yoginīs.

Of these, on the inner eastern petal is the second vowel, the nature of Vajrā, whose nature is form, the eye and delusion, and, through the buddha of delusion, the mirror-like. To the south is the third vowel, of Gaurī, whose nature is feeling, the nose and pride, and, through the buddha of pride, equality. To the west is the fourth vowel, the essence of Vāriyoginī, whose nature is perception, the tongue and desire, and, through the buddha of desire, discrimination. To the north is the fifth vowel, the essence of Vajraḍākinī, whose nature is formations, the body and envy, and, through the buddha of envy, the accomplishment of activity. In the outer northeast, the quarter of Īśāna, is the sixth vowel, of the nature of Pukkasī, which has the nature of the three grounds beginning with Joy. In the southeast, the quarter of fire, is the seventh vowel, the nature of Śavarī, which has the nature of the three beginning with the Radiant. In the southwest, the quarter of Nairṛti, is the eighth vowel, the essence of Caṇḍālī, which has the nature of the Far Gone and the Immovable.

In the northwest, the quarter of Vāyu, is the ninth vowel, the essence of Ḍombī, which has the nature of the Good Intelligence and the Cloud of Dharma. On the petals of the east and the other directions are the tenth, eleventh, twelfth and thirteenth vowels, the essence of the gatekeepers, which have the nature of the four faculties beginning with faith. In the intermediate quarter between the west and Nairṛti is the fourteenth vowel, the nature of Bhūcarī, which has the nature of touch and of wisdom as a faculty. In the intermediate quarter between Īśāna and the east is the fifteenth vowel, the essence of Khecarī, which has the nature of mental phenomena and of the five powers beginning with faith. One should imagine these vowels, too, as luminous, raining down a shower of nectar.

Then one should gaze intently upon the first vowel, pervaded by garlands of light rays, as it is reached by abundant rays in the form of shooting stars. Then, as the red light of the sun radiates forth, they are seen clearly; and one meditates on those fourteen vowels as dissolving like the stars at daybreak. One imagines that the first vowel too, as though frightened by the day, enters into the hollow of the bindu. When it has entered the inner recess of the hollow of the bindu, it is to be burned, scorched by the light of the sun; and one should imagine the vowel with its fine, long nāda. Then, impelled by its nāda and struck by the mantra and by the horn of the bull, the swan (haṃsa), melting, pours forth like a hanging thread. Stirred by the coemergent wind, like the soothing of one tormented by heat, it streams forth in the ultimate sense from the supreme form, and with a stream of great bliss pervades the whole of existence. The mantrin whose mind has gone into that should meditate for as long as he is able. This is the seventh, the rite of meditating on the bindu through the joining of the parts of the letters, from the Instruction on Nairātmyā.

Next, the mantrin with the characteristics as described should perform all the activities taught earlier. Conceiving himself as Nairātmyā, in the emanation wheel, arisen from the union of moon and sun, which are of the nature of supreme method and wisdom, blazing upward like an unmoving lamp in a windless place, she quells the whole mass of darkness and burns the city of objects and faculties. She also prevents the entry of other yoginīs, and exhausts conceptual thoughts, which are not few. Blazing as a mass of fire, the supreme wisdom, upon a heap of dry branches, she is to be beheld as Caṇḍālī, the nature of Nairātmyā, untouchable like an outcaste woman, fierce and exceedingly fierce. Then, as she becomes enamored, one should imagine the swan that dwells in the sky of the wheel of bliss, like a moth gazing with craving at a fire of melted butter, glistening and richly fed, melting with passion and dissolving into it. Then, like butter poured into that fire, it blazes up fiercely, dispelling all the torment of evil deeds and afflictions; radiating outward, it spreads great bliss abundantly, and all objects of focus subside, as a stabbing pain subsides. It becomes beyond objects of focus, inconceivable and inexpressible, and in that one meditates for as long as one can. This is the eighth, the rite of the Fierce and Exceedingly Fierce, from the Instruction on Nairātmyā.

Next, the empowered mantrin, seated on a comfortable seat in the meditation house described earlier, performs the rites of protection, worship of the maṇḍala, and so on. Gathering together the sacred sites and the rest, he takes his own body to have the nature that comprises the grounds and the perfections, and conceives his own body as the celestial palace, the house of great liberation, of the nature of a jewel blazing in manifold forms. The three channels dwelling there, on which all the channels rely, he conceives as Nairātmyā.

Among them, the channel called lalanā begins at the throat, lies on the left, faces downward, goes to the navel wheel, and carries water. One should conceive it as having the nature of the enjoyment body (sambhogakāya), its nature comprising body, the moon, āli, emptiness, entry into the path, night, setting, moving in the north, dream, the grasped object, the knowable, the ultimate, the subtle, the syllable e, and the absence of entities.

The channel called rasanā, starting from the navel, faces upward, runs on the right, goes as far as the end of the throat, and carries blood. One should conceive it as the essence of the emanation body (nirmāṇakāya), embodying all whose nature is comprised in entities: speech, the sun, kāli, compassion, emergence from the path, rising by day, moving from the south, waking, the grasping subject, the knower, the conventional, the coarse, and the syllable vaṃ.

Then the channel called avadhūtī runs through the middle, faces downward, hangs like a plantain flower, and carries the awakening mind like a lamp supplied with oil. One should conceive it as having the nature of mere consciousness, nondual, the essence of the Dharma body, embodying the awakening mind in which emptiness and compassion are indivisible, the essence that comprises everything.

The three channels reaching to the emanation wheel have the nature of one. With an opening like a hollow tube in three sections, like horns bound together, like the form of a vajra, or else like a crow's face, the chief of channels has the nature of the unity of body, speech and mind; like space it is free of conceptual elaboration and unstained. As such, one should meditate on Nairātmyā as having the nature of the body of great bliss. Through the power of this meditation one directly realizes the ultimate. This is the ninth, the rite of meditating on the chief of channels, from the Instruction on Nairātmyā.

Next, the empowered mantrin enters the place of meditation described earlier and sits on a comfortable seat. Having performed the rites of protection, the maṇḍala, and so on, with a mind in meditative equipoise he should conceive himself as Nairātmyā. Then he meditates on the great queen called Nairātmyā, who dwells constantly in the house of emanation, encircled by a halo of her own light, tormented by separation from her beloved, and, in union with her beloved, of the nature of the supreme syllable of desire. And the great king called the venerable glorious Heruka, who abides in the place where the house of Dharma constantly stands, blazing like the sun, eager to embrace like desire itself, the supreme object of desire, sends out messengers of light rays and looks upon her. Then the messengers of light rays are drawn back and carry her off; with the joy of passionate attachment, as though in love, she finds him and attains him in an embrace. It is like a poor woman finding a treasure, or a starving man eating a feast of the finest things, or one tormented by summer heat being bathed with cool water. Having attained the supreme bliss, through the power of meditation one should make the ultimate manifest.

This is the tenth, the rite of meditating on the coemergent queen, from the Instruction on Nairātmyā.

Next, the mantrin with the characteristics described earlier, seated on a comfortable seat in the place of meditation as taught, performs the rites of the protection wheel and the rest. Conceiving himself as Nairātmyā, he should awaken the wisdom that lies at the navel coiled like a snake, unawakened as though asleep, the essence of Nairātmyā, embodying the Dharma body. The essence of glorious Heruka, embodying the enjoyment body, the nature of all, enters by way of the nose, and through the firm pressure of the wind she is to be awakened. Then, like a female snake touched by a stick, she rises up in the form of a staff, and one should see her as fire blazing from the opening of her mouth. Then one imagines that she blazes even more fiercely and burns even the three worlds. By such power of hers the hare-marked moon, whose nature is nectar, at once melts, and one meditates on it pouring down in the form of a stream. Then, through the power of meditation, one makes the ultimate directly manifest and radiates it into all the directions of space. This is the eleventh, the rite of meditating on the wisdom of the wind, from the Instruction on Nairātmyā.

Next, the mantrin who has gained mastery enters the place of meditation and performs the rite of protecting the place, himself and the yoga, and also performs the rite of worshiping the maṇḍala. Reciting, "All phenomena are completely pure by nature: oṃ prakṛtipariśuddho'haṃ," he contemplates selflessness. Then that Nairātmyā dissolves within the breast; and, arisen from the pistil of one's own lotus, the vajra, whose two sides are the bell, with passion and looked upon with a passionate gaze, any woman at all is to be seen as Ḍombinī. She, moreover, is the essence of the paths of preparation, seeing, meditation, distinction and culmination, by way of the distinctions of the moments, the Variegated and the rest. Embracing glorious Heruka, who is like the full moon, having the nature of the enjoyment body, the nature of all that is coemergent, Nairātmyā, the essence of coemergent wisdom, having the nature of the Dharma body like the sky, is to be made directly manifest. Having made her manifest, one does so in all the limbs and lesser limbs. From there one radiates into all the things of the ten directions, and makes all that is radiated one's own nature. Having made it oneself, one imagines it as having the nature of mind only. Having imagined that, one should be free of conceptual thought. In this way, too, one meditates by this rite in all meditations. This is the twelfth, the rite of meditating on the two coemergents, from the Instruction on Nairātmyā.

Next, the mantrin who has gathered the accumulations and who desires the accomplishment of the Great Seal (mahāmudrā) should focus on the practice of any of the yogas as taught, and make the mind stable. When it has become stable, one should examine the signs of the mind. The sign is this: when, by the force of the joy that has arisen spontaneously, the stream appears vividly and directly. It is also that no awareness arises at the loud sounds of drums, great drums, round drums, copper trumpets and the like; that no sensation arises from whips, slaps, fists and the like; and that no harm arises even from flies, bees and the like.
