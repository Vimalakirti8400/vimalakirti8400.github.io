An Auspicious Praise of the Three Jewels (Triratnamaṅgalastotra)

An Auspicious Praise of the Three Jewels, known in Sanskrit as the Triratnamaṅgalastotra and in Tibetan by a title of the same meaning that begins with dkon mchog, the word for the Jewels, is a devotional hymn honoring the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha. Opening with homage to the Three Jewels, it praises the Buddha in rich, lyrical verse as a sage with a golden hue, lotus-like eyes, and a moon-like face, whose feet are revered by Brahmā, Indra, Kubera, and even Māra, and it likens him to a stainless sun. From that sun stream the rays of the true Dharma, which awaken the sleeping world and lead the wise to peace. The work is Toh 1139 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the collection of praises, the class of devotional hymns that open that portion of the canon.

In the language of India: tri ratna maṃ ga la sto traṃ
In the language of Tibet: An Auspicious Praise of the Three Jewels.

Homage to the Three Jewels.

The Sage, whose hue is like utterly purified molten gold, with a maṇḍala of light a fathom wide,
Possessing eyes like a full-blown lotus, a face like the moon, and hair the color of bees,
Whose lotus feet are touched by the crowns of Brahmā, Śakra (Indra), Kubera, and Māra:
To the Buddha, the utterly stainless sun, source of the rays of the true Dharma, I pay homage.

From that very sun of the supremely great Buddha, radiating the light of reason,
Whatever rays of the true Dharma stream forth and illuminate every direction,
By which these worlds, sunk ever in sleep, are truly awakened,
And by realizing which the sages have gone to peace: to that true Dharma I pay homage.

Whatever renown the very rays of the sun of the Tathāgata have caused to bloom,
With its pond of learning, the sweet fragrance of moral discipline, the lovely stalks of the limbs of awakening,
The cool, pure waters of the meditative concentrations and the formless absorptions, and the excellent heart of nirvāṇa:
To the flourishing lotus groves of the Saṅgha upon the earth I pay homage, worshipping them well with reverence.

This completes An Auspicious Praise of the Three Jewels, composed by the master Mātṛceṭa. It was translated, edited, and finalized by the Indian preceptor Vidyākarasiṃhaprabha and the chief editor and translator, the monk Paltsek Rakṣita.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

In the third verse, "the formless" is taken as the formless absorptions paired with the meditative concentrations (dhyānas). The whole verse is read as the image of a lotus grove: learning is the pond, discipline the fragrance, the limbs of awakening the stalks, the absorptions the water, and nirvāṇa the heart of the flower.
