The Rite of the Maṇḍala of Glorious Kālacakra (Śrīkālacakramaṇḍalavidhi)

The Rite of the Maṇḍala of Glorious Kālacakra, known in Sanskrit as the Śrī Kālacakra Maṇḍalavidhi and in Tibetan as Pal Dükyi Khorlö Kyilkhor gyi Choga, is a ritual manual in verse for establishing and worshipping the maṇḍala of the Kālacakra, or Wheel of Time, tantra. Opening with homage to Kālacakra and to Avalokiteśvara, it sets out the maṇḍala rite in its two aspects of preparation and worship, each performed for one's own sake and for others, beginning with the qualities required of the presiding master and moving through the preliminary, main and concluding stages, including consecration, the conferral of empowerments, the offering of fees to the master, the dedication of merit and the final dismissal of the deities. Its colophon names it The Glorious Delightful Garland and attributes it to the great Kālapāda, one of the early Indian masters of the Kālacakra tradition, and it was rendered into Tibetan by an Indian paṇḍita together with a Tibetan translator. It is Toh 1360 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the commentaries on the tantras, among the ritual works of the Kālacakra cycle.

In the language of India: śrī kālacakra maṇḍal bhidhiḥ. In the language of Tibet: The Rite of the Maṇḍala of Glorious Kālacakra.

Homage to glorious Kālacakra.

Having paid homage to Avalokiteśvara,
The light rays of whose speech
Open the lotus of the heart,
I shall set out the maṇḍala rite.
Having first performed the approach
By means of the four-limbed sādhana,

The summary:
The maṇḍala, moreover, is of two kinds:
The preparatory rite and the worship.
The preparatory rite too is of two kinds:
For one's own sake and for the sake of others.
The worship too is of two kinds:
Accomplishing oneself and accomplishing others.
The preliminaries, the main part and the conclusion,
The limbs and so forth, are common to both.

First, the examination of the master.
The master, moreover, keeps the sacred commitments,
Has first mounted the Vajra Vehicle,
Is pervaded by reality, free of attachment,
And entirely free of stains;
He engages in patience, moral discipline and the aim,
Bestows happiness on his disciples,
And dispels the fear of hell;
He keeps the pure conduct of reality itself,
And is a vajra staff against the māras.
Such a one upon the earth
Is renowned as Vajrasattva.
One who is wrathful and proud,
Devoid of the sacred commitments,
Greedy for possessions and unlearned,
Who acts to deceive disciples,
Whose mind has fallen from the state of supreme bliss, and who is without empowerment,
Attached to and intoxicated with enjoyments,
Who speaks harsh and cutting words,
And indulges his desires for his own ends:
The disciple, for the sake of complete awakening,
Recognizing him as like a hell,
Should shun such a one.

One whose mind is profound and vast,
Who delights in pleasing the guru,
Who has a generous mind and great qualities,
Who longs for liberation and has faith in the tantra,
Whose mind is unwavering,
Who has found reality and keeps the secret,
And who stays apart from the company of the wicked:
Such a disciple should be accepted
By a guru who is highly learned.

The Glorious Delightful Garland, a maṇḍala rite of glorious Kālacakra, composed by the great Kālapāda, who was endowed with sublime vision, is complete. It was translated by the great paṇḍita Baniśrī and the translator Ching Yönten Bar.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

The text counts with symbolic number words in the Indian manner: "the sun" for twelve, "the Victors plus two" for twenty-six (the Victors standing for twenty-four), and "the times" for four. The seat's measure, "cubits of the times and the hands," is left as written because the intended figure is unclear. "The sowing" renders spel ba in the list of ground rites, a reading that is not certain; "up to the full moon" interprets nyam thar thug. "Bedrock" renders the unclear rngo. "Stretch the canopy" reads bre for the written bri, "draw." "Free of stains" takes dri mdag as dri ma. The torma in the departure verse reads gtor ma for the written gtong ma. "The six families" are the six buddha families of the Kālacakra system, not the six classes of beings. The cry "O glorious Hevajra" is as the text has it; it may instead be read as an address to the glorious vajra.  The text counts with symbolic number words: "eye" stands for two and "moon" for one. The measure of the vase's breadth ("twice eight, three finger-widths each") is uncertain. The twenty-five names beginning with Brahmāṇī label the twenty-five medicines; "the Powerful Lady," "the Great Powerful Lady," "the Witness," "the Aged One" and "the Self-Bewildered One" render names that could not be identified with confidence. Several grains and gems are given in Sanskrit forms spelled in Tibetan letters that could not be identified, and the gem called ba is left as written. "Bell metal" renders khro chu. "Hearth" reads thab khung for the written thag khung. "A hundred, of the length of an arrow" may instead intend "five hundred," since "arrow" can stand for five. The syllables "be ram" at the start of the generation of the elements are as written and may be corrupt. The objects held in the fire god's left hand, "a hearth and a lotus," are as the text has them; the reading is doubtful.  The three fires correspond to the Vedic triad: the āhavanīya (here "all-consuming"), the gārhapatya ("householder's"), and the dakṣiṇāgni ("fire of offerings"). Several apparent copyist slips are translated by their evident reading: "ma go" read as "mgo" (an ox head); "chig" as "tshig" (words); "bsod nams mi bya" read as "bsad" (not to be slain), which fits the Kālacakra list of five forbidden killings; "sring pa'i bdag po" as "srid pa'i bdag po" (Lord of Existence). "So ba" is rendered "millet" tentatively; its exact identity is uncertain. "Starka" is left as written; it may be a corruption of arka, a plant often used in fire rites. The line "the fire melts secretly into light" renders "gsang," which may be a slip for "gsal" (clearly). "The hare-bearer" is the moon, used here in the Kālacakra convention of symbolic numbers for "one", hence "the first". "The Lord of the Manifold" translates "sna tshogs bdag po" literally; the Sanskrit is uncertain (perhaps Viśvapati, an epithet of Kālacakra). "The Thirty" (tridaśa) is a standard term for the gods; its appearance here in the lists of the forbidden killings and the forbidden hostilities is unusual but translated as it stands.  The passage uses the Kālacakra convention of number-words (the sun for twelve, Rudra for eleven, the directions for ten, Kāma for thirteen, and so on). Where the count of root downfalls makes the value clear, it is given in parentheses; elsewhere, in the maṇḍala measurements (wealth, time, taste, the crested one, the lord of men), the words are translated as they stand, since their exact values in this text are not certain. Apparent copyist slips read by evident sense: "rgyu'i byams pa" as "sgyu'i" (deceitful loving-kindness); "byar du" as "byang du" (to the north); "ma la sar" as "mal sar" (on one's bed), which also restores the seven-syllable line; "rgya la ba" as "rgyal ba" (Victors); "ri bod can" as "ri bong can"; "ne khā" as "re khā" (bands); "dbung" as "dbus" and "rga la de sbang" as "rgal ba spang" (avoid stepping over). The seventh downfall, "goes further than the others" (gzhan bas ring), may conceal a number-word, but is translated as written. "The empowerments for sleeping" (nyal dbang) and "green, like āli" are uncertain.
