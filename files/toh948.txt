The Dhāraṇī Called the Rite of Noble Vajrapāṇi Clad in Blue (Āryanīlāmbaradharavajrapāṇikalpanāmadhāraṇī)

The Dhāraṇī Called the Rite of Noble Vajrapāṇi Clad in Blue, known in Sanskrit as the Āryanīlāmbaradharavajrapāṇikalpanāmadhāraṇī and in Tibetan as 'Phags pa lag na rdo rje gos sngon po can gyi cho ga zhes bya ba'i gzungs, is a ritual and incantation text devoted to the wrathful form of the bodhisattva Vajrapāṇi. It opens with homage to the Three Jewels and then to Vajrapāṇi as a great yakṣa general, empowered by countless buddhas and vividly described with bared fangs, blazing eyes and breath, a terrifying roar, and ornaments of powerful nāgas such as Vāsuki, Nanda, and Upananda. As a kalpa, or rite, it presents the dhāraṇī together with the ritual practices associated with this fierce protector, whose blue-clad form is invoked especially for subduing harmful forces. The work is Toh 948 in the Kangyur, where it belongs to the class of dhāraṇī texts, the collection of incantations and their accompanying rites.

In the language of Tibet: The Dhāraṇī Called the Rite of Noble Vajrapāṇi Clad in Blue.

Homage to the Three Jewels.

Homage to the great yakṣa general Vajrapāṇi Clad in Blue, who has been authorized by as many buddhas as there are grains of sand in the river Ganges; whose bared fangs befit him; whose eyes are like heaps of glowing embers; whose breath blazes like tongues of flame; who has a terrifying roar; whose hands and feet are adorned with the sons of the nāga Vāsuki; who holds the black nāga Sera in his mouth; who wears the blazing nāgas Nanda and Upananda as earrings; who wears a jeweled crown of blazing snakes; who devours blazing venomous snakes; whose face is broad; whose brows and forehead are knitted; whose upper teeth press down on his lower lip; who has the guise of Yama; whose tongue flickers like lightning; whose great belly devours snakes; who strikes terror with the sound of his clapping hands; who has bound the nāga king Suparṇa about him as a belt; who cries out "alala"; who, though the ocean is eighty thousand yojanas deep, finds that it reaches only to his knees; who carries Indra and the Sun upon his shoulders; who destroys all nāgas; who throws everything into turmoil with his great peals of laughter; who has the form of Yama; who terrifies the three realms; and who binds the nāgas.

Then one should recite this vidyā-mantra, spoken by as many buddhas, blessed ones, as there are grains of sand in the river Ganges:

As for its rite: for one seized by a yakṣa, if one enchants water with the mantra and sprinkles it, the yakṣa will let go.

If one wishes to turn back nāgas, one should use a bamboo switch or a shoot of wild pomegranate.

For pretas and apasmāras, the spirits that cause fits, one should use a thread.

To ward off epidemic disease, for paralysis of half the body, and for confusion of letters, one should use a blue thread.

If one desires wealth, one should keep a fast for one day; then, if one fumigates with resin incense, he will reveal in a dream all matters, virtuous and nonvirtuous, and will bestow seven thousand gold coins.

If one wishes to undo a sealing, one should use ashes.

If one wishes to open a bolted door, one should use white mustard seed.

To protect against all fears, one should use old butter, ashes, or white mustard seed.

For every fear of disease, if one ties protective knots in a blue thread and fastens it at the neck, it becomes the supreme protection.

For a child afflicted by grahas, if one ties protective knots in a thread of five colors and fastens it at the neck, it becomes the supreme protection.

If one wishes to repel bandits and thieves, one should use earth.

For poison, one should sweep with kuśa grass.

To protect oneself, one should do it with the mind or with water.

To protect others, one should use a blue thread.

For all these actions, the mantra should be recited three, seven, fourteen, twenty-one, or one hundred and eight times. If one does so, it will succeed.

Before a stūpa containing relics, one should make offerings of blue flowers; if one then recites the mantra a thousand times, it will succeed.

The Dhāraṇī Called the Rite of Noble Vajrapāṇi Clad in Blue is complete.

It was translated at the gate of glorious Nālandā by the Indian preceptor Dīpaṃkaraśrījñāna and the Tibetan translator Gya Tsöndrü Sengé.

This concludes the translation. The following are translator's notes.

"Sera" (the black nāga held in the mouth) could not be identified and is kept as the Tibetan sound. The text calls Suparṇa, normally a name of the garuḍa, a "nāga king," and this is translated as written. The plants used to repel nāgas are uncertain: "bamboo switch" and "wild pomegranate" are tentative. "Confusion of letters" is a literal rendering of an unclear ailment, perhaps a speech or writing disorder. In the phrase about bandits, the text reads "wishes from, earth"; it is taken as "if one wishes, use earth," though "barley" is also a possible reading.
