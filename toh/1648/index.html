About this text

The Means of Accomplishment of Mahāmāyā, in Sanskrit Mahāmāyāsādhana and in Tibetan Gyutrul Chenmö Druptap, is a short verse manual for the meditative practice of Mahāmāyā, a wrathful, four-faced and four-armed deity of the Mahāmāyā Tantra, one of the yoginī tantras of Highest Yoga Tantra. Opening with homage to Vajraḍākinī, it leads the practitioner through the preliminaries of purification, offering, confession, refuge and the mind of awakening, the contemplation of emptiness through the seed syllables, the building of a protective vajra enclosure ringed by the elements and the eight charnel grounds, and the generation of the central deity in union with his consort, surrounded by four ḍākinīs on the petals of a lotus. It closes with brief completion-stage instructions, mantra recitation, and the counsel to regard saṃsāra and nirvāṇa as nondual, and its colophon names its author as an Indian preceptor called Bodiless Vajra, probably Anaṅgavajra. It is Toh 1648 in the Tengyur, where it belongs among the tantra commentaries as one of the sādhanas, or deity-practice texts, connected with the Mahāmāyā cycle.

Translation

The Means of Accomplishment of Mahāmāyā.

Homage to Vajraḍākinī.

First, the mantra practitioner
purifies his own face;
that is the custom of the world.
The ultimate yoga is as follows:
upon a moon maṇḍala at the heart
one should imagine a black hūṃ.
Rays of five kinds stream out, pervading everything;
having illuminated the world-realms,
they gather back, and one purifies the face.

Then the ground for meditation
should be sprinkled with the five nectars.
Upon it, sit on a comfortable seat.
At the heart imagine a moon disk and hūṃ.
From it rays stream forth abundantly,
invite the maṇḍala of intrinsic nature,
and one should imagine it in the space in front.
From the hūṃ at one's own heart
four ḍākinīs are sent forth,
and they make outer and inner offerings, praises, and so on.

Then one should confess wrongdoing:
the wrongdoing done with body, speech and mind,
all the wrongdoing one has done oneself and the rest,
confess it, purified in the maṇḍala of intrinsic nature.
Rejoice in the merit of the buddhas and others;
having rejoiced,
by this merit of rejoicing
dedicate it for the sake of all sentient beings.
I go for refuge to the Three Jewels.
With the twofold mind of awakening,
this body, speech and mind of one's own
offer up to the buddhas and the rest.
Having made the sevenfold offering,
upon one's own body, speech and mind
contemplate these three syllables, their intrinsic nature.

The three syllables are these:
he, ru and kā, the three.
he: phenomena are without cause.
ha: phenomena are without arising and ceasing.
e: phenomena are free of speech and expression.
ru: phenomena are like an echo.
ra: the body of perfect great bliss.
u: phenomena rest on nonconceptuality.
kā: phenomena are without any abode.
ka: phenomena are endowed with bliss.
ā shows phenomena to be unborn.
These three syllables, the intrinsic nature
of one's own body, speech and mind,
meditate on in this way with diligence.
oṃ śūnyatā jñāna vajra svabhava ātmako'haṃ

Having made the mind of emptiness stable,
that very mind becomes a sun maṇḍala.
Upon it one should imagine a black hūṃ;
it transforms, and one should meditate on it as a vajra.
In the vajra's central bulb imagine a black hūṃ.
From it vajra rays stream out;
they build a square vajra wall,
above, imagine a vajra lattice,
and below is the vajra ground.
Outside the vajra fence,
laṃ transforms into a square ground of earth
marked with three-pronged vajras.
Outside the earth maṇḍala,
vaṃ transforms into a round water maṇḍala;
in its center imagine vases.
Outside the water maṇḍala,
yaṃ transforms into a wind maṇḍala shaped like a bow,
which should be marked with banners on right and left.
Or else, outside that,
raṃ transforms; imagine a triangular fire maṇḍala.
Inside the vajra fence,
in the four directions and the four intermediate directions,
one should imagine the eight charnel grounds.
paṃ transforms; imagine a red lotus.
Upon the anthers of the lotus
one should imagine a sun maṇḍala.
In the center imagine a dark blue hūṃ.
From the hūṃ rays stream forth abundantly,
summoning the sugatas of the ten directions;
they dissolve into the hūṃ, and it melts.
On the four petals of the lotus
one should generate the four goddesses.
Arising from the nature of the four immeasurables,
the four, Vajraḍākinī and the rest,
with the vajra song of these four
should rouse the one who abides in sameness:

O glorious one, who fully brings to accomplishment the sublime state of the vajra song,
ah, supreme friend, with vajra light open wide the lotus!
a la la la la ho
Perform the dance of great bliss, of utmost delight!
Just as the light of the sun opens wide the lotus's mouth,
a la la la la ho
perform the dance of great bliss, of utmost delight!

When the vajra song has been sung,
that very melted globe becomes hūṃ.
From it rays stream forth abundantly,
summoning all the sugatas of the ten directions;
imagine them dissolving into the hūṃ itself.
Through the power of compassion it becomes the Vajra:
his body dark blue, with four faces,
beautiful with four arms.
The central face and the body are dark blue,
the right face yellow, the left face green,
and the remaining face white.
His two right hands hold a skull cup and an arrow,
his two left hold a khaṭvāṅga staff and a bow.
He sits on a seat of a sun maṇḍala.
His four faces each have three eyes.
He wears a necklace of fresh human heads
strung on a cord of moist entrails,
his head is adorned with a garland of dry skulls,
and he is fully endowed with the great bone ornaments.
Laughing with joy, his mouth open, fangs bared,
his reddish-yellow hair blazes upward.

Then bless the six places.
At the point between the brows imagine a yellow ci.
On the two eyes a white oṃ;
on the two ears imagine a red ā.
On the nostrils a white khe.
At the throat one should imagine a red āḥ.
At the heart one should imagine a red re.
When the six places have been properly blessed,
the bodies of father and mother are complete in a single instant;
he embraces emptiness closely.
From the nondual body rays stream out;
having illuminated the world-realms,
the head is adorned with Vajradhara,
and body, speech and mind are blessed.

She whose very essence is
the completely pure wisdom of the dharmadhātu:
on the eastern lotus petal
imagine Vajraḍākinī.
Her body dark blue, with four faces,
the right face yellow, the left face green,
the remaining face white.
Her two left hands hold a khaṭvāṅga and a bell,
her two right a skull cup and a vajra.
She is the earth element, the nature of mirrorlike wisdom.

Yellow in color, Ratnaḍākinī
one should imagine on the southern lotus petal.
Her right face black, her left face green,
the remaining face white.
Her two left hands hold a banner and a jackal,
her two right hold a trident and a jewel.
She is the water element, the nature of the wisdom of sameness.

White in color, Padmaḍākinī
one should imagine on the western lotus petal.
Her right face yellow, her left face green,
the remaining face black.
Her two left hands hold a bow and a skull cup,
her two right an arrow and a multicolored lotus.
She is the fire element, the nature of discerning wisdom.

Green in color, Viśvaḍākinī
one should imagine on the northern lotus petal.
Her right face yellow, her left face blue,
the remaining face white.
Her two left hands hold a noose and a skull cup,
her two right hold a sword and a small drum.
She is the wind element, the nature of diligent accomplishment.

The four, Vajraḍākinī and the rest,
are firmly seated on sun seats.
Their skull cups and other ornaments
are said to match the ornaments of the principal deity.
Their eyes match those of the principal himself.
Their mouths slightly open, fangs bared,
their reddish-yellow hair blazes upward.
From the bodies of them all
stream rays of a blazing mass of fire.
The Vajra Lady of compassion's power,
beautifully encircled by these four goddesses, meditate on her thus.

How the completion stage is done
should be asked of the masters.
At the crown of the head imagine the wind maṇḍala;
at the heart, the earth maṇḍala;
at the navel imagine the water maṇḍala;
at the secret place, the fire maṇḍala;
one should meditate by way of the neuter.
oṃ āḥ hūṃ
For the yogin to take rest,
one should recite the mantra repetition:
oṃ āḥ hrīḥ hūṃ phaṭ
Having arisen from meditative absorption,
one should meditate on saṃsāra and nirvāṇa
as nondual.

The Means of Accomplishment of Mahāmāyā, composed by the Indian preceptor Anaṅgavajra, is complete.

Translator's notes

"Through the power of compassion it becomes the Vajra" (rdo rjer gyur): the melted seed becomes the principal deity, Mahāmāyā in his Heruka form, described in the following lines. The first line of the vajra song (dpal rdo rje glu'i dam pa'i srid pa shin tu sgrub byed pa) is obscure; it is rendered as an address to the deity being roused. "Jackal" (ce spyang) as an implement of Ratnaḍākinī is translated as written. The wisdoms named only by one word in the Tibetan (mirror, sameness, discerning, diligence) are the four wisdoms (mirrorlike, equality, discriminating, and all-accomplishing); "diligent accomplishment" renders nan tan, the all-accomplishing wisdom. The throat syllable's color is written dmar, read as dmar "red". "The Vajra Lady of compassion's power" (snying rje'i stobs kyi rdo rje ma) is read as the deity at the center, now named in the feminine, encircled by the four goddesses; the line could also be taken as a further epithet of the four goddesses themselves. "By way of the neuter" (ma ning gis): in completion-stage usage the neuter is the central channel, as distinct from the male and female side channels. The author's name sku med rdo rje, "Bodiless Vajra," is identified as Anaṅgavajra, whose name is usually rendered yan lag med pa'i rdo rje in Tibetan; this identification is likely but not certain.
