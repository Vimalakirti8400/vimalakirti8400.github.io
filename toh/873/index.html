About this text

The Hundred and Eight Names of the Buddha, the Blessed One, together with the Dhāraṇī-Mantra (Tibetan: Sangs rgyas bcom ldan 'das kyi mtshan brgya rtsa brgyad pa gzungs sngags dang bcas pa) is a short devotional text that praises the Buddha through a series of epithets. It opens with homage to the Omniscient One and then celebrates the Blessed One's fragrant, compassionate body, his marks and signs, and his radiant smile and features. The listing of a hundred and eight names is combined with a dhāraṇī-mantra, a formula of protective and merit-bringing recitation. The work is Toh 873 in the Kangyur, the collection of translated words of the Buddha, where it is classed among the dhāraṇī and related ritual texts of the canon. Its Sanskrit title is not given in the lines above, so it is best described simply as a litany of the Buddha's names intended for praise, recitation, and blessing.

The Hundred and Eight Names of the Buddha, the Blessed One, together with the Dhāraṇī-Mantra.

Homage to the Omniscient One.

Homage to the Blessed One, whose body is anointed with the fragrant incense of the sandalwood of compassion; whose lotus feet are touched by gods and asuras wearing jeweled crowns; the self-arisen, the dispeller, the guru, who brings benefit to the whole world; the Teacher, the Tathāgata; the Sage free of faults, whose nature is peace; beautiful of form, splendid with clear major marks, his body adorned with the minor marks, perfectly complete in every quality.

His face smiles like a blossoming lotus. His teeth are white, even, and well set, fully revealed and shining, luminous and lovely like a stainless jewel, bright and clear. He outshines the disk of light of many suns blazing with a thousand rays. Homage to the supreme Sage endowed with the ten powers. I bow. I bow down deeply. I offer praise.

Oṃ bho hūṃ hūṃ, ā ā, hī hī, śiva śiva, śānti śānti, śama śama, śamāpaya, tathāgatānugata, svabhāva, abhāva, samuttāya, asamabala, aviralabala, amitabala, atibala, hūṃ ghuṃ ghuṃ, dhau dhau, bhrau bhrau, bhvedu bhvedu, hī hī, bhagavāna, bhavānataka surata bhoga mahāśāntikara, pāpakilbiṣa nāśaka svāhā.

He is radiant like the sun,
Bearing the ornament of a blazing halo.
Renowned, he has cast off the fence of stains;
Stainless, faultless, his mind is the Dharma;
The great all-pervading lord, endowed with the ten powers,
Free of dust and possessed of glory.

Translator's notes

The mantra is given in a standardized romanization of the transliterated Sanskrit as it stands in the Tibetan. Several syllables, such as "samuttāya" and "bhvedu," look irregular but are kept as transmitted. "Asamabala" reads the Tibetan "asamapala" as "bala" to match the series of "-bala" terms.

The line rendered "will flourish before my very eyes" literally says "by me directly will prosper or come into presence." It may instead mean that such a person will come into the speaker's own presence.

The speaker in the verses is the wrathful vajra-holder who has just risen to speak. His Tibetan name, rdo rje 'jigs byed, is the standard rendering of Vajrabhairava.

"Unmoving Vajra-Holder" (mi g.yo rdo rje can) and "Vajra-Holder of Great Bliss" (bde chen rdo rje can) are kept as descriptive translations. Their Sanskrit forms, perhaps Acalavajrin and Mahāsukhavajrin, are not securely identified.

The final line breaks off mid-sentence and continues in the next piece. The term rendered "place of refuge" (Tibetan 'khra ba'i sa, 'khra sa, occurring twice) is unclear, and the rendering is conjectural from context. "Vajra, Sword, and the rest" may refer to named deities or to deities identified by their emblems. The Tibetan could also be read as a single "Vajra Sword." The phrase rendered "on Mount Kailāsa and on Meru" is literally "on the Meru of Mount Kailāsa." It may instead mean Kailāsa as the king of mountains. The epithet "with lashes like a cow's" reads rdzi ma as eyelashes, matching the major mark of cow-like eyelashes. In "a thousand ten-millions of eons," bye ba is a koṭi, that is, ten million. The figure who rises to speak, "the one who wore a vajra headband," is described rather than named in the Tibetan. It may stand for a bodhisattva name, possibly something like Vajroṣṇīṣa, but this cannot be confirmed, so it is rendered descriptively.
