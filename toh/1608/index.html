About this text

An Explanation of the Noble Four Seats, known in Sanskrit as the Āryacatuṣpīṭhaṭīkā and in Tibetan as Phakpa Den Shyipai Nampar Shepa, is Toh 1608 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the section of commentaries on the tantras. It is a commentary on the Catuṣpīṭha, the Four Seats Tantra, a yoginī tantra of the Unexcelled Yoga class, and its author describes it as an explanation that unties the knots of the tantra's secret teachings. Opening with homage to all the ḍākinīs, it works through the tantra's verses, explaining its seed syllables and mantras, its visualizations of deities and of the stupa as an image of the three realms, the union of the expanse and wisdom, the signs of progress on the path, and practices such as mental recitation and rejuvenating elixirs. According to its colophon, the text was composed by the Indian master Smṛtijñānakīrti on the basis of instructions received from his guru, and was also translated and finalized by him.

Translation

In the language of India: ārya catura pīṭhi ṭīkā. In the language of Tibet: An Explanation of the Noble Four Seats.

Homage to all the ḍākinīs.

Adorned with a buddha who holds the drop of the sun above the young moon,
of beautiful form, the head of loving-kindness, the supreme body, and in the gentle body too;
the staff-form born of the lotus, the summoning body, the terrifying roar, the vajra-bearer;
the form of consciousness and wisdom, destroyer of the terrors of existence: to the crown of the gods I bow.

On the great tantra of the glorious Four Seats,
this explanation that unties the knots of secrecy
has been written,
because Armor of Virtue requested it.

Beginning first with "Thus it was explained," up to the words "he praised," one should apply, as far as one is able, the analysis, the signs, the words, the summaries and so forth, the heavy and light syllables, and the explanation of the meaning of the words, letters and so forth.

The passage beginning "Thus it was explained by the Omniscient One" teaches four points: the teacher, the manner of teaching, the matter taught, and the place. Here "the Omniscient One" is the teacher. "Thus" is the manner of teaching. "Wisdom" is the matter taught. "In the Pure Abode" is the place. As for the meaning of the words, "of the sages" means "of the buddhas." In some versions "of the sages" also has just this meaning.

On "the supreme bliss of the net of yoginīs": here the yoginīs are Prajñāpāramitā and the others; "net" means a gathering; "supreme bliss" means enjoyment.

If one asks in what assembly this took place, it says, "with the One of Trained Qualities," and so on. "With the One of Trained Qualities" is, in both words, Maitreya. "The Terrifier" is Vajrapāṇi. "The Pendant One" is Samantabhadra. "The Three-Necked One" is Gaganagañja. "The One of Meru" is Sarvanīvaraṇaviṣkambhin. "The One with the Meru Crown" is Kṣitigarbha. "The Lotus One" is Lokeśvara. "The Lotus Holder" is Keen Wisdom. These, and so forth, means together with all the gods, humans and others.

"Secret union" means the secret time. "Ground" means the earth. "The breaths that come out and go in" are the wind that comes upward and the wind that goes downward. On "the objects, the body and so forth, the deities," one should take this as the five aggregates, the five faculties, their objects, the elements and the sense sources. On "the domain of wisdom and consciousness": here "wisdom" is the male form, that is, method; "consciousness" is the goddess form, that is, the wisdom consort. "The lord of wisdom" means whoever is master of these.

Wishing to teach first of all the root wheel, which is the method that is the basis of sentient beings, he says "Listen!" and speaks of the divisions of the breath in which those two have become one, saying "Twenty thousand," and so forth. Here a "ten-thousand" is ten thousands; "twenty thousand" is two ten-thousands; "one thousand" is a single thousand; "six hundred" is six hundreds. "The breaths of sixty water-clock periods" means sixty water-clock periods. These two together give the number of breaths there are in a day and a night. "Coming out" is the breath that rises upward; "going in" is the breath that enters downward.

On "To be set outside for counting, likewise twelve on the ground": this is the wheel of the ground. "Twelve" is twelve squares. "Outside" means drawn on the outside. "To be set for counting" means that one should set down the letters for the calculation.

"Just as the days of the month are counted" means that one should place, in the squares on the outside, in order through the directions and the intermediate directions, the twelve names, the Dripper and the rest.

If one asks from which point in the order of days one should begin placing them, he says, "From the east, set down the middle winter month and the rest," and so forth. "The middle winter month" is the middle month of winter; the sense is, starting from that, up to the first month of winter. It means that from Fear and the rest at the beginning to Desire at the end, they should be placed in the twelve square cells. That the twelve links beginning with ignorance are, in another enumeration, called the Dripper and so forth, is the oral instruction of the guru.

"Worship Rohita at the extra time; likewise the sage for seventeen years": this should be explained by reversing the root text.

"Subhūti, the ground is first aged, and the yoga ground until it arises": this means what is born in part through joining the two, the natural progression and the reversal. The characteristics of their bodies should be understood in every case.

On "The ones with r succeed in preśapa": here the ones with r are the Dripper, the Makara, the Thick One and the Scorpion, because in the Indian words there is that letter. Likewise for the Bull, the meaning is that whatever is done succeeds.

"The wise should calculate at morning" means that, starting from the time of dawn, one should count the breaths and so on.

By "A son of Vāsuki is the Dripper," and so forth, it means that when someone has been struck by poison, in examining whether he will recover, in the fields of the Dripper and the rest one should apply it to the son of Vāsuki.

On "Here the sequence of poison applications should be known in detail": here "poison" is wherever the wheel of the nāgas is; "sequence of applications" means that one should know in detail the sequence of recovery and non-recovery.

On "Under the horned ones and the bulls it succeeds; nectar will not arise": here again the horned ones are Fear, the Makara, the Thick One and the Scorpion, because in the Indian words there is the letter r. The word "one with r" is a synonym for what is nonvirtuous. The meaning is this: "If one is bitten under the four with r and the Bull, one will not survive." For that very reason it says, "nectar will not arise," and "one will be devoured by the Black One."

On "As for the application of gathering up the poison, having applied all the rites of others": the meaning is that if someone has been bitten in the measure of any one of the Bull and the four with r, then it should be investigated.

On "In order, in the middle winter month and the rest, the northern direction traversed by time": beginning from the middle month of winter, the sun travels in succession according to its motion, that is, the southern course and the northern course. "Knowing the northern direction" means the northern course. It means that the southern course makes the day increase, and the northern course makes the night increase.

Now he speaks of the subtraction from and addition to the sixteen water-clock periods in the divisions of night and day in the twelve months, the middle winter month and the rest, saying "Twelve multiplied and four," and so forth; this means thirty-six water-clock periods.

"Just as day and day" means "as many as complete the month": just so many, in each day and each night, the divisions of the water-clock periods should be well understood.

"Of the twelfth of the squares" is the last month of winter. "Twelve-fold and six" means twenty-six water-clock periods in the day. "Thirteen multiplied and four" means thirty-four water-clock periods in that same night.

"Twelve multiplied, eight" is twenty-eight water-clock periods in the day of the first month of spring. "Thirteen multiplied, two" is thirty-two water-clock periods in the night.

"Joining two up to the bhiśu" means that the middle month of spring is marked by thirty water-clock periods; the sense is that its day and its night each have thirty water-clock periods.

"Thirteen and two" is thirty-two water-clock periods in the day of the last month of spring. "Twelve-fold and eight" is twenty-eight water-clock periods in that same night.

"Thirteen set out, and the four should be added" is thirty-four water-clock periods in the day of the first month of summer. "Twelve multiplied and six" is twenty-six water-clock periods in that same night.

"Thirteen multiplied, six as well" is thirty-six water-clock periods in the day of the middle month of summer. "Twelve multiplied and four" means that in that same night there come to be twenty-four water-clock periods.

He then says that what is called the southern course, which is the cause of the day's increase, is completed by just these, in the words "It shows the course in the southern direction," and so forth.

Again, wishing to describe the northern course, which is the cause of the night's increase, he says "Thirteen multiplied," and so forth. Here this means thirty-four water-clock periods in the day of the last month of summer. "Twelve multiplied, six" is twenty-six water-clock periods in that same night.

"Thirteen multiplied, two as well" is thirty-two water-clock periods in the day of the first month of autumn. "Twelve multiplied and eight" is twenty-eight water-clock periods in that same night.

"Shown as thirteen-fold" means that the middle month of autumn, called bhiśu, has thirty water-clock periods in the day and thirty in the night.

"Twelve-fold and eight" is twenty-eight water-clock periods in the day of the last month of autumn. "Thirteen-fold, two" is thirty-two water-clock periods in that same night.

"Twelve multiplied, six" is twenty-six water-clock periods in the day of the first month of winter. "Thirteen multiplied, four" means that there are thirty-four water-clock periods in that same night.

Again, he speaks of the examination of how many breaths there are in a single water-clock period of the months, nights and days as divided by the courses, saying "Three hundred and likewise sixty," and so forth. Its meaning is this: of the sixty water-clock periods of a day and night, in each single water-clock period there are three hundred and sixty breaths.

"According to the sequence of the months" means that the breaths of the water-clock periods of the middle month of summer and the rest are likewise.

On "The breaths of the water-clock periods, all complete, and in sequence revolving, cut off": here the middle month of spring and the middle month of autumn are the bhiśu, and in these two the day and the night revolve equally. In the remaining months, what is "cut off," that is, subtracted, and what is added revolve.

Having stated the breaths of the divisions of the courses and the months, and the divisions of the water-clock periods, now, wishing to divide the portions of the days of a single year, he says "kha and the rest," and so forth. Here "kha and the rest" means taking kha as the first of the twenty-four letters and going up to the middle letters, because grammar (byākaraṇa) states a ka ra śa ma ti ka la ya va ra la. It says that if one knows the time, one should make only six.

Gathering all of these together, it says, "In the reckoning of the two courses": the meaning is that from the two courses one should make two cognitions. The intention is this: having gathered the number thirty-six, one should divide it into equal portions. As soon as this is done, there are eighteen reckonings in one portion.

"By making the two ten-fold" refers to the two portions. Each one is multiplied by ten, and whatever number results from adding the two together, the full count of that number is "thus the water-clock periods of the year"; the meaning is that the breaths of the water-clock periods are converted.

"The breaths of day and night should be applied by its measure": "its measure" means that the understanding of the water-clock periods and the full extent of the breaths make the measure clear, and one should understand this.

Having thus set forth the turning of the root wheel, namely the years and months, the water-clock periods of day and night, and the measure of the breaths, he next wished to teach the wheel of the yogin, so that, once the outer maṇḍala has been made, the signs of virtue and non-virtue may be examined through the distinctions of the vowels placed in the twelve places. For this he spoke the passage beginning "Having taken all the lords of speech."

The instruction for this is as follows. In a secluded spot smeared with cow dung that has not fallen to the ground, on a maṇḍala spread with sandalwood, saffron and so forth, one draws a wheel of twelve cells with chalk over which mantra has been recited with the seed syllables of the five ḍākinīs. In it, beginning from the eastern cell, one writes the letters starting with a and ending with the last of the a-series, leaving out the neuter letters ṛ ṝ ḷ ḹ. Then oneself, having brought one's chosen deity to mind, takes the measure of the questioner's lords of speech and states them as a number. One also takes the letter of the wheel and triples its number, then multiplies that count six-fold, adds sixty-five to it, and subtracts twelves from it. Looking at the quality of whatever count remains, one should declare virtue or non-virtue.

"The two neuters are clearly excluded" means the vowels with ṛ ṝ and ḷ ḹ left out. "If in order with what is called a" means taking it from the first. Up to "Just as the first is a, so the last should be taken," the qualities of the signs are taught.

Now, to teach the instruction on "If all the lords of speech are taken," the Blessed One himself, speaking, said the passage beginning "First it is to be tripled." Here, "First it is to be tripled" states the meaning that the questioner's lords of speech are first tripled and afterwards multiplied six-fold. "The count is set down six-fold": the meaning is this. Whatever number of vowels the questioner's lords of speech come to once tripled, that very measure should in turn be set down six-fold.

He also said, "Having added sixty-five." This means that to the very measure that was multiplied six-fold one adds sixty-five, making a single sum. "Having divided it by the places" means dividing it by twelves and subtracting. "Taking the remaining letter" means: by the count of the letter that remains after subtracting twelves, one should declare the quality, virtuous or non-virtuous, of the letter arrived at by counting through the letters.

With the passage beginning "Or else draw a zigzag line," yet another kind of sign of virtue and non-virtue is taught. Here "zigzag" means a line drawn like the trail of an ox's urine. On ground made pure by the preparatory procedures described before, one draws such a line evenly, drawing it toward oneself, with chalk over which the ḍākinī mantra has been recited. Placing the twelve vowels on its peaks above, one counts, and one should declare the quality of the vowel at which the count ends.

With the passage beginning "Or else drop a flower," still another method of declaring signs is taught. The instruction for this is as follows. The wheel of twelve cells described above is drawn in the order already explained, and in it the twelve vowels, with the neuter letters left out, are placed. Then, with one's mind first set on one's chosen deity, one should drop a flower down onto it. Whatever cell the flower falls on, one should declare to the questioner the quality, virtuous or non-virtuous, of the vowel placed in that cell.

This is the explanation of the first chapter of the Glorious Four Seats.

Now, wishing to teach the signs of untimely death that arise, the method for protecting the person so marked, the position of the questioner, the aspects of the breath, living and not living, the method for bringing rain, and the signs of messengers, he began the second chapter with the passage beginning "The particulars of the suchness of wisdom."

Here, "How the signs of the body" means: what are the signs of untimely death present in people's bodies like, and where are they located? With the passage beginning "Listen, great vajra king," the Blessed One answered that very question.

"Through agitation of the breath, an embodied being dies within six months" means that the characteristics of the incoming and outgoing breath proceed in reverse, decreasing and increasing contrary to the count that was stated. Increase and decrease also occur in the body, and "the body of embodied beings dies" means that they die and pass on.

With the passage beginning "Likewise, if one sees slight fright," further signs of untimely death are taught. The meaning is this: "slight fright" means being frightened on seeing an untimely death.

"The cheeks will tremble." "If the time has come, the breath is cut off and keeps being cut off": "if the time has come" means that if one reckons by calculation at the point of sudden death, there is cutting off in the measure of the breath, and "keeps being cut off" refers to increase and decrease. At that time, the cutting off is death.

Furthermore, the thorough knowledge of the stages of this cutting off is the guru's instruction. One makes a wheel of twelve cells furnished with twelvefold rings, drawing the central cell first. In it, the outer measures are these. In the eastern cell, five measures, that is, five numerals. In the second cell, ten. In the third cell, fifteen. In the fourth cell, twenty. In the fifth cell, twenty-five. In the sixth cell, twenty-six. In the eighth cell, twenty-eight. In the ninth cell, twenty-nine. In the tenth cell, thirty. In the eleventh cell, thirty-one. In the twelfth cell, thirty-two. In the central cell, thirty-three. These should be understood as the sequence up to the center.

Again, in the intermediate ring: in the eastern cell, three years. In the second, two years. In the third, one year. In the fourth, six months. In the fifth, three months. In the sixth, two months. In the seventh, one month. In the eighth, half a month.

In the ninth, ten days. In the tenth, five days. In the eleventh, three days. In the twelfth, two days. In the center, one day. These are the numbers of days of remaining life.

It is further said: if one asks at what time the yogin should examine this cutting off and increase of the breath, he said:

"At the time of the morning Dripper,
one should examine the signs then."

The thought is this: one should examine it on the occasion of the limb, the Dripper, at dawn.

Now he teaches the measure of the number of days of remaining life when what resides in the eight limbs is cut off, with the passage beginning:

"When the inner part of the nose is cut off,
one will surely die in seven days."

The meaning is this: as soon as the tip of the nose collapses, one dies in seven days.

"If the āṃbara of the eye is cut off,
the trota likewise, five days."

This means that if the channel of the eye called trota is cut off, so that it does not throb, one dies in five days. Its throbbing should be examined at the end of the eye channel on the side of the bridge of the nose.

"If the flesh between the cheeks is cut off" means that if the channel at the side of the cheek is cut off, one dies in one day.

"If the udum of the ear is cut off" means that if the channel joined to the outside of the ear opening is cut off, one does not last beyond five water-clock periods.

Here, "the tongue, a black line" means that as soon as one suddenly sees on the tongue a black line like a thread, one dies in two days.

"If the upper and lower teeth clench" means that as soon as the teeth clench against the teeth, one dies in just three days.

"The throat, the side, or the outer channel" means that if what is called the small point on the outside of the throat is cut off, one dies in half a month.

"If the breastbone collapses" means that if the chest suddenly sinks downward, it is only half a month.

"If the nails lose their luster" means that as soon as the nails of the hands and feet have suddenly lost their redness, one dies in six days.

For the person in whom the signs of untimely death with the characteristics thus taught have been observed, he teaches the rite of protection, with the passage beginning "When the inner part of the nose is cut off." For all of these the instruction is this: at whatever place the channel has been cut off, at that very place one meditates on the seed syllable as taught, or on the deities generated from it.

For that very reason he said, "with the seed of wisdom." The seed of wisdom is the letter hūṃ. The seed of the vajra is the letter suṃ. The seed of the Fierce One is the letter kṣuṃ. The seed of Vetālī is the letter yuṃ. The seed of Caṇḍālī is the letter hūṃ. The seed of the Lioness is the letter smryuṃ. The seed of the Tigress is hmryuṃ. The seed of the Jackal is ymryuṃ. The seed of the Owl is kṣmryuṃ.

Again, for the protection of that same person, he teaches the rite of placing in the outer circle, with the passage beginning "Further, I shall explain protection." There, "Taking two firing vessels" means taking two unfired earthen vessels. "Draw with saffron and gorocanā" is understood as saying that one should draw with bezoar or with saffron.

Now he teaches the manner of drawing the hearth: "Further, I shall explain: nine cells and so on, with portions." The instruction is this: on the lower earthen vessel one should draw a wheel of nine cells encircled by one ring. "The upper, with twelve portions" means that on the upper vessel too one draws a wheel of twelve cells encircled by one ring.

Now, wishing to teach the mantra to be set within, he spoke the passage beginning "Having written the name in the center." On both earthen vessels, in the center, one should write the name of the person for whom the rite is done. If one asks how it is written, he said, "Surround it likewise with seed syllables," the meaning being that it should be written amid the letters of the mantra.

Wishing to make just this clear, he said, "The sound of homage comes first," and so on. The sound of homage is the letter o, and it should be written below the name of the person for whom the rite is done. "Place it before the words of protection" means that one should write that person's name before rakṣa rakṣa. "The seed placed at the end" means that at the end of what was given above comes "richly adorned by the five buddhas," that is, one writes the letter hūṃ. "Place the burning letters at the end" means that at the end of what is richly adorned by the five buddhas one should set the burning letters svāhā.

Having thus set the letters in the center, he teaches what is set on the encircling ring: "The vowels on the upper ring and so on." That is, on the ring encircling the upper, the second earthen vessel, one should set the twelve vowels with the neuters left out.

Now come the three seeds placed in the nine cells of the lower earthen vessel: "ka, pha, and the doubled seed." Here ka is earth, and its seed is the letter hūṃ. pha is wind, and its seed is the letter yuṃ. "And the doubled seed" is the letter kṣuṃ, because ka and śa are stacked. "The name of the goddess Vajrī" is the letter sruṃ. "And the seed of the One Endowed with Five" is the letter hūṃ.

Wishing to explain how the mantra is set out, in exactly the order in which all of these are placed in the central cell, he said, "Homage, with burning at the end." The application is this: one should set it out thus, oṃ so-and-so rakṣa rakṣa sruṃ sruṃ. One should apply it in the same way to the letter kṣuṃ and the rest.

Since "the seed of the One Endowed with Five" was explained as "richly adorned by the five buddhas," the verse explanation beginning "The staff is Lokeśvara" explains the letter hūṃ alone. The staff is the straight stroke beneath the letter ha, and that is Lokeśvara. "The vajra hand on the side that is reached in examination" is the foot stroke of the letter hūṃ, and that is Vajrapāṇi. "The body" is the belly of ha, and that is Mañjunātha. "The supreme bell" is Prajñāpāramitā, because the two are of a single nature. It is understood that the letter ha is the nature of Mañjuśrī and also the nature of the Perfection of Wisdom.

"Likewise the head is Maitreya" means that the head stroke of the letter hūṃ, its top line, is Maitreya. "What is called ma, blazing above": here ma is the stroke shaped like a half-moon, and "blazing above" is the dot of its circle. "Vairocana liberates from saṃsāra" means that those two are Vairocana; they are just what was described above.

Then he taught the seeds kha, pha and the rest according to the sequence of the cells, with the passage beginning "What is placed in the eastern direction." There, in the eastern cell is sruṃ, the Vajra Ḍākinī. In the north is kṣuṃ, the ḍākinī of the Fierce One. In the west is yuṃ, the seed of Vetālī. In the south is hūṃ, the Caṇḍālī ḍākinī.

Having thus arranged the directions, he now teaches the arrangement in the intermediate directions, with the passage beginning "In the direction of Īśāna, the Lioness." In the cell of Īśāna is smryuṃ, the Lioness. In the cell of Fire is hmryuṃ, the Tigress. In the cell of Nairṛti is ymryuṃ, the Jackal. In the cell of Wind is kṣmryuṃ, the Owl. These are what is arranged there.

Wishing to explain the forms of the seeds of just those in the corners, he said, "Six letters for the four." The meaning is this. The four letters sa, kṣa, ya and hā are set down. Beneath them is the letter ma. Beneath that is the letter ra. Beneath that again is the letter ya. Beneath that again is the foot stroke of the letter u. Above all of them are the half-moon and the dot. As soon as this is done, each of the four letters sa and the rest comes to have six letters, as examined. All of this is the instruction for the maṇḍala of the lower earthen bowl.

Now, wishing to set out the arrangement of the seeds on the wheel of the upper earthen bowl, he spoke the passage beginning "In those twelve places above." The meaning is this: in the directions of the twelve-celled wheel described above, one should place the seeds of the vowels. "Placing homage and burning at the end" means that, as before, one should place the neuter letters with the letter oṃ first and the letters svāhā at the end. The neuter letters are the vowels ṛ ṝ and ḷ ḹ that are placed.

He taught that these very neuter letters are called the natures of the ḍākinīs and others, with the passage beginning "The Blazing One and the Sucking One." There, the Blazing One is the letter ṛ. The Sucking One is the letter ḷ. The Robbing One is the letter ḹ. The Ḍākinī is the letter ṝ.

Now he teaches how these letters are arranged in order, with the passage beginning "In the east is the place of the Ḍākinī." In the eastern cell, ṝ is the Ḍākinī. In the north, ṛ is the Blazing One. In the west, ḷ is the Sucking One. In the south, ḹ, the Robbing One, is placed.

He also teaches the arrangement in the corners, by the guru's instruction. In the two cells of the Īśāna corner are i ī. In the two cells of the Fire corner are u ū. In the two cells of Nairṛti are e and e. In the two cells of the Wind corner are o and au. "The name is written in the center" serves as a reminder that, as with the lower wheel, something is set in the central cell and the vowels are set on the encircling outer ring.

If one asks how the protection wheel drawn in this way is to be meditated on, he said, "Blessed by the five ḍākinīs." The meaning is this: one should have it blessed by the five ḍākinīs, and meditate on that very maṇḍala.

If one asks what nature the vajra master too should meditate on, he said, "By the union of oneself as the vajra holder." Setting out the instruction for this as well: one should perform the yoga of Vajrasattva in the sequence taught in the Nopaki of the maṇḍala. "Saying, I am Vajrasattva" means that one should meditate with the pride of the deity, saying vajrasatva ahaṃ.

"With the fingertips of the vajra hand" means that, having performed the yoga of Vajrasattva in the sequence taught, and having visualized the palm of one's own hand, at the fingertips, as a five-pronged vajra, one should touch the earthen bowl with that hand. "The firm seed in the center" is the letter hūṃ. "It should be recited one hundred and eight times" means that, having worshipped with scents and the like, one also offers a torma to the bhūtas, places the bowls at the head of the person for whom the rite is done, and recites the mantra over them. These are the rites of outer protection.

Now, wishing to teach virtue and non-virtue through the sameness or non-sameness of the position of the one who asks about the signs and of oneself, he spoke the passage beginning "Further, the nature of the breath."

"By abiding in accord with the breath,
all is said to be accomplished."

Here, breath in accord means this: if the one asking about the signs approaches and asks from the right side of the vajra master, and at that time the breath is flowing in the right channel, one should judge it virtuous. If he comes to the left and asks, and the breath is flowing in that channel, it is virtuous.

Wishing to make just this clear, he spoke the passage beginning "The right abides with the right." When the breath is on the right and the questioner stands on the right, it is virtuous; when the breath is on the left and the questioner stands on the left, it is virtuous. That is all.

When this is reversed, he teaches non-virtue: "Words not in accord with the breath." "Not in accord" means flowing in reverse. In that case, if the breath is definitely flowing on the right and the questioner stands on the left, or if the breath is flowing on the left and he stands on the right, it is non-virtuous.

Even when the questioner stands in accord with the breath, there are the aspects of outflow and inflow. Wishing to teach, by these portions, the distinction between what is desirable and what is undesirable, he spoke the passage beginning "Through the union of outflow and inflow." Here, inflow is the breath flowing in, and outflow is the breath going out. Even when the breath and the position are both at one in the asking, if the question is asked while the breath flows in, then it is virtuous; if it is asked while the breath goes out, it is otherwise, non-virtuous.

Making just this clear, he said:

"All that is done upon the inflow
is renowned as flourishing."

"Flourishing" carries the thought that every aim is accomplished.

Now, wishing to teach protection for one who has been bitten and the methods to be applied against poison, he spoke the passage beginning "For one bitten by a venomous creature." There, "One should give treatment for it" means that one should treat it. Further, the passage beginning "Now, of those bitten at the four times and so on": the four times are the four letters with ra, and it means that those bitten at these should not be treated. This connects with the statement that others who are bitten should be treated. To make just this clear he said, "Other treatment for all," and so on.

Having thus set forth the full examination of the cases of those bitten, he wishes to make protection of the afflicted person manifest and then to explain the treatment, so he first explains protection, with the passage beginning "Having arranged the letters." There, the meaning of the words

"The mantra of eight letters
should be placed on the eight limbs"

is this. The eight letters that are the mantra are the seeds of the eight ḍākinīs, beginning with the letter suṃ. "Should be placed on the eight limbs" means this: on the crown a yellow letter kṣuṃ; on the two eyes a blue letter hūṃ; on the two ears and the two nostrils a red letter yuṃ; on the tongue a white letter sruṃ; at the throat a yellowish-white letter smryuṃ; on the two shoulders a whitish-black letter hmryuṃ; on the upper back a blackish-red ymryuṃ; and at the navel a reddish-yellow kṣmryuṃ. Each is first set in place in the order of the places with its own color alone; then, in the specific phase of removing the poison, all of them become white and one meditates on them. There is no doubt about this. He said just this:

"Imagining them all white in color,
one should remove the poison with the heart mantra."

Having thus fully protected the limbs, he teaches the proper protection of consciousness:

"Placing it in the center of the maṇḍala,
it is blocked by knowing and consciousness."

"Placing it in the center of the maṇḍala" means this. At the heart, in the region of space, one generates, with the mantra oṃ kamala be svāhā and with the lotus mudrā whose characteristics are taught for the fire offering, an eight-petalled lotus, and imagines it at the heart. On it is a moon disc generated from the letter a, and on that one should imagine the all-base consciousness, like a star.

"It is blocked by knowing and consciousness": consciousness and wisdom are the letter hūṃ, and so is consciousness, for that reason. The meaning is this: the letter hūṃ, which is method and wisdom as a pair, having become red light, rests beside consciousness, and with it one should press down upon the consciousness that has become white. This is the instruction.

Now he teaches the treatment, with the passage beginning "As soon as the body has been protected." The instruction for this is as follows. Take an earthen vessel or a copper basin filled with milk and water, and place the foot of the bitten person in front of oneself. In its center, visualize an eight-petalled lotus set out with the mantra and mudrā described above. On it is a moon disc from the letter a. On that is a vajra generated from the letter suṃ, and on its hub again the letter suṃ. From the face of its light rays the circle of all the ḍākinīs radiates out and is gathered back again. One should say oṃ vajra śuddhaḥ sarva dharmā vajra śuddho'haṃ.

Generated from that vajra is a goddess, white in color, with two arms, seated in the cross-legged posture of a sattva. Her black hair hangs loose. She is adorned with ornaments of snakes. White garments cover her upper and lower body, and a white parasol shades her from above. With her right hand she holds a khaṭvāṅga, and a bhandha. She rests on the eight petals, having gathered together the heads of the eight nāgas, Vāsuki and the rest, and made them her seat. So one should meditate.

The nāgas are set out as follows. On the eastern petal is Vāsuki; on the northern petal, Takṣaka; on the western petal, Karkoṭa; on the southern petal, Padma; in the Īśāna quarter, Mahāpadma; in the Fire quarter, Śaṅkhapāla; in the Nairṛti quarter, Kulika; and in the Wind quarter, the one called Lindaka. Their colors are like those of the eight seeds placed on the limbs of the bitten person described above. He said just this, in the passage beginning "Having filled the vessel with water."

Showing that all of this is to be undertaken by the vajra master himself, having first performed the yoga of Vajrasattva, he said:

"My own body is the vajra;
one should imagine Vajrasattva."

The instruction for this as well is as follows. One should meditate on oneself as Vajrasattva, either in the sequence taught in the extensive sādhana or else in the momentary manner. "The momentary manner" means that the vajra generated from the letter a, resting on a moon at one's own heart, is transformed and visualized as the Great Seal. Or else "momentary" is merely visualizing the seal of the Great Seal.

If one asks with what the yogin who has thus brought the deity to mind should remove the poison, he spoke the passage beginning "With the feathers of a peacock." The sequence is this. One imagines the peacock feather held in one's own hand as held in the hand of the wisdom ḍākinī. With it one draws out the poison from every limb of the patient, from the forehead and so on down to the soles of the feet, and then, below, in the water maṇḍala, one should imagine a torma for the wisdom ḍākinī. That is all.

All of this is to be done by a vajra master who has done the recitation of the vidyā. He gives the mantra for recitation: oṃ hridadanadhara hūṃ phaṭ. This is the heart mantra of these eight nāga kings, Vāsuki and the rest; the meaning is that at the time of recitation one recites just this much, without inserting a name.

If one asks with what practice and how many times this heart mantra is recited, he spoke the passage beginning "Of the manifest union of Vajrasattva." The meaning is this: first, at the time of approach, having performed the yoga of Vajrasattva, one recites it one hundred thousand times.

Again, making clear that same mantra with a name inserted, he spoke the passage beginning oṃ vasuki hridadanadhara svāhā: oṃ hridadanadhara hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ phaṭ phaṭ make so-and-so free of poison! hūṃ phaṭ svāhā. It is applied in the same way to Vāsuki and the rest.

"With white mustard, flowers and sandalwood, by burning": here the word "white" should be applied to each of them individually.

"As soon as the heart is shaken,
bind and hold that consciousness"

means that as soon as one strikes at the heart as described above, one should bind and hold the life faculty at the throat with the seed syllable as taught.

"As soon as the breath is agitated,
press it with one's own breath"

means that with one's own breath one should draw up the breath of the person being treated. The thought is this: one should press the life faculty of that person with the seed syllable of wind. That is all.

"When all the hands and feet move,
one should take up the melody oneself"

clearly means that with the melody of the letter hūṃ one should cause the wisdom to descend into the person being treated.

"Then, at a dwelling place of the nāgas,
one should pour the milk there"

means that one should cast the milk and the other implements into a dwelling place of the nāgas or into a great river.

For the examination of those who have been bitten, he teaches the kinds of cases that should not be treated, with the passage beginning "The face, the dot, the name."

Having fully understood with his own mind the thought in the mind of Vajrapāṇi concerning those bound by grahas sent by others, and knowing it, the Blessed One himself, wishing to explain, spoke the passage beginning:

"The supreme rite for bringing down rain,
great king, on that very day."

"In a pleasant spot
near the bank of water"

means that one draws the maṇḍala for bringing down rain, spoken of next, only near water and not anywhere else. That is all.

"Square, with four doors,
adorned with eight nāgas"

clearly means that one should lay out the eight nāgas, Vāsuki and the rest, in the five colored powders. "Forming the crest with two heads" means that two nāgas rising upward should have their heads set at the crest. "One should wind the tails at the corners" means that the two tails are wound and set at the corners. "The eight nāgas form the fence" means that the eight nāgas, Excellent Victor and the rest, form the fence and so on.

With the passage beginning "In the directions are the eight families," he teaches the arrangement in its center. Setting this out: in the center of the maṇḍala one draws an eight-petalled lotus. On its eastern petal is Vāsuki, white in color. In the north is Takṣaka, yellow. In the west is Karkoṭa, red. In the south is Padma, black. In Īśāna is Mahāpadma, whitish-yellow in color. In the Fire corner is Śaṅkhapāla, whitish-black. In Nairṛti is Kulika, reddish-black. In the Wind quarter is Hulunta, yellowish-red. In the same way, for the fence and the rest too, one should know the distinctions of color according to the order of the families.

"The great kings, the lords of the clouds,
should be summoned from the eight directions"

means that one should summon the great gods of the clouds from the eight directions of the nāgas.

"The one called Agatsar and the Roaring One,
the Fierce One and the Massing One,
the Hard One and the Wrathful One,
the Rain-Pourer and the Brimming One":

these cloud kings should be known, in that order, in the outer maṇḍala. Wishing to make just this clear, he said:

"As they are placed in the east and so on,
know them by the color of each direction."

He taught that all of this is to be done by the yogin who holds the pride of the Vajra Ḍākinī, with the passage beginning:

"In the center the Vajra Ḍākinī
sits upon a lotus seat."

"With a lotus encircled by snakes,
joined to a seat of snakes"

means this: she is seated on a lotus seat entwined by snakes, and at the command of the Vajra Ḍākinī the eight nāgas give command to the great kings of the clouds. That is all. Making just this clear:

"Making the wisdom of yoga clear,
one is the very self of the wisdom ḍākinī."

oṃ bhur taḥ svāhā is the mantra for the offerings and the rest. As with oṃ vasuki hūṃ svāhā, one should apply it in the same way to Takṣaka and the others. oṃ gili gili varśa variśapaya hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ ho ho ho tha tha tha svāhā is the mantra of the eight cloud kings. oṃ vajra takrini varśa hūṃ bhur taḥ svāhā is the root mantra for recitation. These are the mantras of Vāsuki and the rest. He taught that all of this is to be done only by a yogin who has obtained the signs.

Further, the passage beginning "I shall also teach the signs of messengers" belongs here. "Squint-eyed" means that the eye is slightly crooked.

"Just as with my messenger,
one should examine the causes of both"

means that, just as with the messenger, one should examine oneself thoroughly as well.

This is the second chapter of the Explanation of the Glorious Four Seats.

With the intent that if the awakening mind has not arisen there will be no accomplishment of mantra and the rest, the Blessed One began the third chapter, on the awakening mind, which concerns the particulars of striving in yoga, with the passage beginning "Whoever seeks out mantras."

With the passage beginning "First, contemplating emptiness," he teaches that all phenomena lack intrinsic nature.

"Form is empty, and so on, from the first,
up to the eighteenth, mind":

this means the mind element, the dharma element, and the mind-consciousness element. One should apply this in the same way to the eye and the rest. "The form element itself is empty" means that form is empty of intrinsic nature. "Likewise, sound too" means that one should understand it for sound and the rest as well. That is all.

"If one imagines the mind dissolving into space" means that one should meditate on the mind as like space. It is said:

"In the midst of space there is no self,
and in the midst of the self, space is made.
Whoever sees and knows
these two as indivisible is liberated."

"The three realms" are the desire realm, the form realm and the formless realm. In "Seeing in its midst," "its" means emptiness. "The cause examined there" means that one should examine the signs taught as desirable and undesirable.

With the passage beginning "By blazing, pacification," he teaches the signs taught for pacification.

"Swelling, thick with specks": here "specks" means the most minute particles.

With the passage beginning

"The arising of the later application
is the mind of sprouts and so on,"

he teaches the enjoyment of the fruit of the signs. "One should examine the movement of the knowable" is to be learned from the guru's oral instruction.

Wishing in this chapter to make suchness alone clear, he spoke the passage beginning "Killing father and mother." Here the father is delusion, and the mother is craving. The king is pride. The pure brahmin is the ear faculty and the rest. The region is the five aggregates. The one who moves about is conceptual consciousness. "Killing" means that one should kill them with the sword of wisdom alone; that is the whole of it.

This completes the explanation of the third chapter, the Seat of the Self, from the glorious Four Seats.

Thus, since a yogin who is intent mainly on suchness does not attain the accomplishment of the rites without the divine pride of the deity, he wished to state deity yoga and the rites of paralyzing, killing and so on, and so with the passage beginning "Listen to how reality is the path," he begins the fourth chapter.

"Fully abandoning duality and nonduality" clearly means abandoning both oneness and multiplicity. "Of the sound of the river" refers to attachment and the like.

Teaching the means of drawing out those sunk in the ocean of saṃsāra, he says: "Having the character of the characterized and the characterizer." The characterized is the person; the characterizer is the method; "character" means "clearly marked."

"Consciousness is to be contemplated by wisdom": here, wisdom having been set upon consciousness, it is said that karma is gathered in the mind that characterizes the wisdom to be characterized. As it is said in the noble Descent into Laṅkā:

"Karma is gathered by the mind;
by wisdom it is discerned;
by consciousness it is cognized."

"Embodied beings sunk in the middle:
their liberation is called the sign."
This is another way of saying that they are to be liberated by the method given above.

Wishing to make just that clear, he spoke the passage beginning:

"With wisdom examine the knowable;
one should examine the movement of the knowable."

"Realization completed in the fifth lifetime, the yogin delights in giving." Here "realization completed in the fifth lifetime" teaches the path of the Tathāgata. The intent is this: among a hundred thousand yogins, will even a few go or not go? That is the whole of it.

To say that, after the body has been freed of attachment and the like by meditating on emptiness, one should plant the seed that is to arise, he says:

"In the field of emptiness, in the body and the rest,
the intelligent one plants the seed."

Wishing to elaborate just that, he spoke the passage beginning "For example, having burned an outcaste's house with fire." There, "making it a great dwelling for the gods and others": "great" means made abundant. The meaning is this: just as one burns an outcaste's dwelling and makes it a dwelling for the supreme gods and others, so one burns with the fire of wisdom the body governed by attachment and the like, and then, solely in order to generate a body empowered by what is called compassion, which becomes the cause of benefit for sentient beings, one should plant the seed of the dharmadhātu.

"The virtuous, those of good family, the wise, dwell everywhere": here "the virtuous, those of good family" are the compassionate, and "the wise" is the yogin. So it is clear that the yogin should generate compassion and the like.

With the passage beginning "Setting out the syllables to be set out," he teaches that one should set out the seeds.

"At the crown one should imagine fire" means setting the syllable kṣuṃ at the crown of the head.

"With the seed of the two earths
one should apply it to the eyes"
means setting the syllable hūṃ on the two eyes.

"With the seed of empty wind
imagine it at the nose and ears"
means that one should imagine the syllable yuṃ at the ears and the nose.

"From the seed of nectar water,
the tongue faculty, chief lord"
means setting the syllable hūṃ on the tongue.

"Those which" refers to smryuṃ and the rest: they alone are to be taken in stacked form; that is the whole of it. "One should apply them at four places" means smryuṃ at the throat, hmryuṃ at the two shoulders, ymryuṃ at the breast, and kṣmryuṃ at the navel. "One should set them on the eight limbs" means in that same way. "Of the body endowed with seeds" means setting out their seeds.

"Oneself as the wisdom ḍākinī" means that one should meditate on oneself as having the nature of the wisdom ḍākinī.

"Fully applying āli kāli": here āli means ā and the rest, the vowels, and kāli means the letter kā and the rest, the consonants.

"At the place of the drop, at the brows": here the place of the drop is the syllable hūṃ; the meaning is that one meditates on it between the eyebrows. "Adorned by the five buddhas" refers to that very syllable.

Wishing to explain its very nature, he says "On the seventh object of fire and wind." Here fire is the letter ra, and wind is the letter ya. The two made into one are called "fire and wind." The seventh object from that is the letter ha. The vajra seed is the u written beneath, on the authority of the scripture that says, "The summoning, the side that has gone, the vajra hand."

"Fully endowed through mingling with the drop" means placing above it, in the middle of the shape of a crescent moon, the drawn dot of the drop riding upon it. "It is called the stream of nectar rain" because its very nature is the dripping of nectar.

"First the eight rites and so on": here the eight rites are to be understood as pacifying, enriching, subjugating, fierce rites, filling, paralyzing, impelling, bringing down, and so on. The words "and so on" also include the sword, the underworld, the vetāla, the eye ointment that makes one invisible, the aim of accomplishing bezoar (gorocanā), the stars, red ochre, the cloth, and other desired things.

"First perform the approach and so on": "first the approach" means mantra recitation and meditation. Here too the words "and so on" mean observances, vows, regulating one's food, and the like.

With the passage beginning "Listen! The vajra is reality itself," he teaches the extraction of the hidden mantra.

"First the vajra speech": here the vajra speech is the word bajra, and the word "first" means that it is the opening word. "And the letter of the highest class" means the letter ka. "As of the fourth class": taking the ka class as the first, the fourth class is the ta class. "The first letter" means that one should take the letter ta.

"Doubled, and with the letter ra above": here "doubled" means that very letter ta stacked twice, and "with the letter ra above" means that the letter is given at the head of that very ta. "Join i and the letter ra" is said because it bears the vowel sign i.

"As of the second class, take the second letter, with the drop": this is the letter cha with the drop above it. "Making its tip with a hook" means that the vowel sign i is to be given to that very cha. "The third letter of the fourth" is the letter ḍa. "Both are to be turned over twice" means that chin and da, these two, are to be said twice. "The fifth, and the giving of speech" means that one should furnish it with the syllable hūṃ, which has the fifth; that is the whole of it. "With the homage syllable above" makes clear that one should set the syllable oṃ in the first place. "Set the fierce syllable last" sets forth that one should set the syllable phaṭ at the end. Combined in this way it is: oṃ bajra karttari cchinda cchinda hūṃ phaṭ.

"Both hands are to be bound": having bound the vajra bond, "bringing it to the mouth" means that it should be placed right in front of the mouth. "The wrathful and the middle are extended" means that the index finger and the middle finger are extended. "The two are to touch and touch" means that one should cut many times, in the manner of their meeting.

With the passage beginning "Then I shall explain the cutting off," he teaches the cutting off of rain and the like. There too is the extraction of a hidden mantra:

"As of the last class, the third letter is to be taken": this is the letter ba. "So too, of the third class, likewise the third letter": this is the letter ḍa. "The portion of the three seeds of which is to be furnished with the letter e": this means that one should give the preceding letter bā the vowel stroke that carries e. "Whichever, at the first of the fifth, has its tip made with a hook": this is the letter bā furnished with the vowel sign i. "The water seed of the third, and in between, the fierce dots": this means placing the side drops on the letter ba. "Of the last of the vajra seed": the seed of the Vajra Ḍākinī is the letter sa, and its last is the letter ha; that is what is to be taken. "The letter ra, the hook and the noose": the letter ra is attached beneath that ha, and at the head the vowel sign i is drawn turned back toward the reverse side. "Setting the destroying syllable at the end" means that one should set the syllable phaṭ at the end. "The homage is to be set above" means that one should set the syllable oṃ above.

Combined, they are: oṃ ba ḍa be biḥ hṛ phaṭ.

Here too the further instruction is this: the yogin should undertake it with the pride of the noble Paramāśva. From another tantra one should know that bi bā bi bā is said twice.

"Joining the two hands palm to palm" means making the two hands into the vajra palms. "The ring fingers one with the other; the wrathful one presses all down." "With the two ring fingers" means that the two middle fingers are to be wound around on the far side by the two ring fingers. "There the thumbs are joined: this is the mudrā of the Mare." By joining both the two middle fingers and the two thumbs, it is the mudrā of the Mare's Mouth.

"A garland blazing with even light, blazing in the places of the ten directions, will burn the clouds and the rest": this means that with the pride of glorious Paramāśva one should see the gods of the clouds dwelling in the ten directions as devoured by the light of a blazing garland of rays.

This completes the Seat of the Self, from the Explanation of the Glorious Four Seats composed by the vajra master Armor of Virtue.

Now Vajrapāṇi, wishing to know how effigies are set up, the fire offerings of pacifying and the rest, the nature of the torma, and how disciples are taken on, asked with the passage beginning "What form, and of what likeness?" This is treated at length in the Seat of Others, which begins here.

Distinguishing the kinds of fire pit: "Beginning from about one cubit, up to as many as a thousand cubits." According to the rite, beginning from eight finger-widths, it should be increased up to twenty finger-widths. Here "rim" refers to the fire pit in general.

"Of three parts of the measure, two parts are to be dug": the measure of the fire pit as stated is made into three parts, and the lower two parts are to be dug. "And of what is dug, an eighth part is made the lip of the pit": the measure of the dug fire pit is made into eight parts, and the measure of one part is called the lip of the fire pit. "And outside, half a part of the lip, a rim is to be made": this means that a rim half a part of the lip is to be dug outside it. "Furnished with eight rims, an eight-petaled lotus": this means extending the measure of the surrounding rim into eight parts, making the measure of the lotus petals. "Outside the lotuses a wall of one finger-width is made": outside the lotus a wall of one finger-width is made. "In the center of the pit a vajra is to be placed within a lotus": this means that in the center of the fire pit, as is fitting, one should set a vajra upon a lotus.

"Three rims are for fierce rites": for the rite of fierce sorcery the fire pit is triangular. "For pacifying, four-cornered": for the rite of pacifying the fire pit is made four-cornered. "The fully round goes with subjugating, and likewise for enriching": for subjugating and enriching the fire pit is round. "Crescents for expelling": for expelling and similar rites the fire pit is bow-shaped. "Eight-cornered for summoning and bewildering": for these rites the fire pit is eight-cornered.

"They are to be applied in order, one by one:
pacifying, enriching, subjugating and the rest."
This means that pacifying and the rest are to be done just as stated, and not otherwise.

"With the emblems of the five buddhas, set out direction by direction": this means that, circling to the left, one should set out Vajrasattvī and the others in the directions. From the east there is Sattvavajrī; in the north, Ratnavajrī; in the west, Dharmavajrī; in the south, Karmavajrī. Saying that those hand emblems are to be set on the lip and not elsewhere, he says:

"On the lip at the four places,
adorned with the emblems of the buddhas."

"Crescents and most beautiful vajras
are to be set at each corner":
this means that, as is fitting, one should set moons and vajras at each of the corners.

"At the place of the seat, as is suitable, by measures of twelve thumb-widths, reaching to half a man, heaped like snowy Kailāsa: auspicious." This means that in front of one's own seat, within the rim, one should set a snowy Kailāsa. "Half a man" means half the measure of a man's cubit, that is, one span. Combined further with the measure of twelve thumb-widths, it comes to twenty-four finger-widths, and that measure is the measure of the snowy Kailāsa; that is the whole of it.

"In the places of the eight petals and the rest, invite the deities to be seated in order": this means that one should set the Vajra Ḍākinī and the others on the eight petals in their sequence. Saying that the measurements are to be kept without fail, he says: "The yogin should apply that measure in particular as well."

These form the first chapter of the explanation of the Seat of Others, in the light of the glorious Four Seats.

With the passage beginning

"How are the water offerings and the rest done?
The water offerings and the rest, there too,"

Vajrapāṇi, wishing to hear about the water fire offering and the like, fully requested the second chapter of the Seat of Others. "The water fire offering" is so called because the fire offering is made with water. Here the words "and the rest" take in the torma, the offering substances and so on. "How is its character to be characterized?" means "What is the nature of the water fire offering?"

"In the center, the water vessel and the rest" means a water vessel made of gold or the like in the shape of a lotus, in addition to the eight vases spoken of below. "It is to be anointed with white substances" means that it is to be anointed with white fragrant scents and the like.

"Then, placed outside, the water rites and the rest": this means that, having meditated for a moment on oneself as the Vajra Ḍākinī, and then having sent her forth with one's own breath by the stages of arrangement taught above, one generates the wisdom ḍākinī in the outer place, in the middle of the water, according to the rite, with her body color, hand emblems and the rest, and then performs the fire offerings of pacifying and the rest.

"The Discerning One, the steadfast, colored with her own form; the mudrā of all the pledges: the mantras, mudrās and the rest are shown." This means that, just like the pledge mudrā of the wisdom ḍākinī, they are to be shown to the Vajra Ḍākinī and the others.

"If the mantrin always sprinkles water, the particular results will increase": this means that by sprinkling water every day on the vajra tips of the crown ornaments of the Vajra Ḍākinī and the others visualized in front, and offering it, great increase arises.

These form the second chapter of the Seat of Others, from the Explanation of the Glorious Four Seats.

Since a yogin who meditates in this way does not attain the supreme accomplishment without drawing the maṇḍala, he begins the chapter on the maṇḍala with the passage beginning:

"The characteristics that pass beyond saṃsāra:
listen, great Vajra King!"

The lack of intrinsic nature of the five aggregates and the rest is not elaborated here, since the chapter on emptiness has already explained it. The passage beginning "How one views with equality" is easy to understand. The passage beginning

"The characteristics of nondual wisdom:
listen, Vajra, to reality,"

teaches the purity of things.

"In order to make it unsurpassed,
these three palm-joinings are to be recited":
the meaning is that, having brought forth the bodhisattva, one should make offering to the unsurpassed Buddha.

"Joining what is called hūṃ, the homage and the destroying are set at the ends": here "fully joining what is called hūṃ" means that one should join the syllable hūṃ between the homage and the destroying; that is the whole of it. As soon as that is done, it becomes the mantra oṃ hūṃ phaṭ. With that mantra one imagines one's own hand as a vajra and regards the ground as the abode of the five tathāgatas, and so the ground is purified.

Then one makes a square maṇḍala with exquisitely fragrant scent and draws an eight-petaled lotus with powders of five colors. In its center one draws the vase of all accomplishments; on the eastern petal a dot of five colors; on the southern petal a vajra; on the northern petal a bell; and on the western petal a great conch filled with nectar. Having drawn the maṇḍala of these vessels and so on, one winds a red thread all the way around their sides and sets them in place.

After that, the vajra master, with the pride of the wisdom ḍākinī, should make offerings with flowers, scents, incense, garlands of flowers, food offerings, torma, articles of enjoyment and the like.

After that, again binding vajra fists with both hands, one places them crossed in reverse at the tip of the nose, extends them toward the directions, and should destroy the obstructors with this mantra: "For now, whoever you may be, lords of the earth, gods, nāgas, yakṣas, gandharvas, bhūtas, bhūtīs, ḍākinīs and the rest: since I am beginning the maṇḍala here for the sake of sentient beings, go away at once! hūṃ phaṭ phaṭ phaṭ svāhā." This is the mantra and mudrā for expelling the obstructors.

Then, binding the mantra and mudrā of Lāsyā: oṃ pra sā dha ku ru maṅ me ti sun dhā rī da ya ci ta taṃ hūṃ jūṃ jūṃ svāhā. That is the mantra and mudrā for supplicating the earth goddess Pṛthivī.

After that, one should hold the vajra as suchness and ring the bell as the Dharma. Then, visualizing the vase that stands in the center of the lotus as the wisdom ḍākinī, white in color and holding vajra and bell, one should then make offerings.

Having bound the vajra bond, one extends the two index fingers, bends them, joins them tip to tip, and places them at the heart. oṃ āḥ svāhā: that is the heart mantra and mudrā. With this vajra one touches the vase with the right hand and recites it one hundred and eight times.

Then one consecrates the five colors with oṃ hūṃ hri svāhā, the seed that is the nature of the five buddhas, and sets them down. Over the five colored powders one should recite the seed of the Lioness, oṃ smryuṃ svāhā.

Then, having given torma to the bhūtas, one makes offerings with as many of the five articles of enjoyment as there are, for the sake of taking possession of the ground, and sets them at the right-hand boundaries, the shoulder and the rest. Those entrusted with the work of drawing the maṇḍala and so on are to be engaged in the path of the ten virtuous actions. Again, having anointed the ground with fragrant water, one should lay the lines. These are the rites for protecting the ground.

Then the laying of the lines. The thread to be used is made of five strands and dyed with the five colors. After that, in the center of the maṇḍala ground, one makes dots at the eight directions and intermediate directions, generates the pride of the wisdom ḍākinī, holds the five-colored lines with both palms, and consecrates them with mantra, using the seeds of the five ḍākinīs. The mantra is oṃ a a a oṃ āḥ. The master holds one end and the disciple holds the other end, and so they lay the line.

The order here is as follows. When the master sits at the corner of Nairṛti, the disciple should stand at the corner of Īśāna. Again, when the master, coming round to the right, sits at the corner of the wind, the disciple likewise stands at the corner of fire. When the master stands in the west, the disciple stands in the east. Then when the master stands in the south, the disciple likewise stands in the north. One should then lay the lines of the outer directions.

Then the square maṇḍala is made in five parts, and it is to be enclosed from the central square by half the portion of three squares. The heart of the maṇḍala, the nine squares, is to be adorned with vajra pillars. All the corners are to be adorned with crossed vajras alone. By the portion of the retinue come the doors, that is, the four corners. Half of that portion gives the middle band; half of the middle band gives the rim; half of the rim gives the ground of the five colors.

On the inner eastern, northern, western and southern squares one should lay white, yellow, red and green colored powders. In the center is blue. Outside the vajra garland, on the seats of the outer yoginīs, is a deep red color. Outside the goddess Pukkasī and the others, one should encircle it with five walls. The place of black color outside that is like the blue sky. On the deity ledge one should lay the color of refined gold. Outside it is furnished with four archways (toraṇas). So it is said:

"Square and with four doors,
adorned with four archways,
draped with bells, hanging lattices and blazing garlands,
with nets, half-nets and the rest;
on every part of the corners,
on the courts of the doors and on the junctures,
are set vajras and jewels:
this is the design of the outer maṇḍala."

At the sides of the four doors, above the vases, in the east, north, west and south, one sets up banners marked in colored powder with a lion, a peacock, a goose and a makara. At their foot eight vases are placed. At the four corners too, four vases are to be set. Such a maṇḍala is to be drawn from one cubit up to as much as a thousand. For the maṇḍala too, as for the fire pit, the prescribed measurements are to be kept without fail.

Then he teaches the rite of casting out. With the pride of the Vajra Ḍākinī, making it resound with hūṃ, one should expel the obstructors with vajra and bell. Standing at the eastern door with the circling movement, the casting movement, the even movement, the dividing movement, the one-footed stance and the cutting to the left, and saying "I am the Vajra Ḍākinī," one should destroy the obstructors with this mantra: oṃ hūṃ hana māraya duṣṭāṃ ghātaya ghātaya a hrīḥ phaṭ.

In exactly the same way, in the north, with the pride of the Fierce Ḍākinī, one binds the vajra bond and places it at the heart, stands in the right-extended stance, the left-extended stance, the vaiśākha stance and the circular stance, and should destroy the obstructors with this mantra: oṃ hūṃḥ aḥ phaṭ.

Then, at the western door, one binds the mudrā of Lāsyā, places the left foot joined to the knee, stands in the posture of the circling lion, and should destroy the obstructors with this mantra: oṃ hūṃ āṃ phaṭ.

After that, in the south, with the pride of Caṇḍālī, one places the bound vajra at the crown of the head, stands in the kneeling posture, the squatting posture, the circling movement and the turning-back movement, and should destroy the obstructors with this mantra: oṃ hūṃḥ i phaṭ.

Again, standing in the place of the dance, with the pride of the wisdom ḍākinī alone, one binds the great mudrā of glorious Vajrasattva, and, standing with the right leg extended, seated, drawn together and upright, one should utter the syllable hūṃ in many ways.

Then, with just the mantra and mudrā for purifying the ground, one should drive in the pegs. Opening the vajra palms at the crown of the head, one should set the vajra net with this mantra: oṃ hūṃ phaṭ. Binding the vajra bond, raising the little fingers and the thumbs, and circling toward the directions, one should complete the fence with this mantra: oṃ je pa ni hūṃ phaṭ.

Setting down the vase adorned as before, one touches it with the left hand, the little finger pressing the thumb, and should recite this mantra over the vase one hundred and eight times: oṃ tapte tapte mahā tapte svāhā. This is the mantra and mudrā for filling it with the water of the lake. With that same mudrā and the mantra given below, one puts the five nectars into it, and should recite this mantra once, as stated: oṃ sruṃ kṣuṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā. These are the mantras for the vase abiding in the seed syllable.

Then, adorned with clothing and ornaments as described, and endowed with loving-kindness and the rest, one is surrounded by armor alone.

"Opening both hands wide,
and winding the two thumbs together,
one forms the shape of a goose's wings,
turns them back and places them at the heart."

Binding that mudrā, one should put on the armor with this mantra: oṃ hūṃ hoḥ phaṭ.

Here too, having begun the meditation and generated each deity in turn, one first carries out the arrangement on the eight limbs, makes offering with the five articles of enjoyment, flowers, incense, lamps, scents and flower garlands, and then makes oneself the nature of the three bodies with this mantra: oṃ vajra kāya ho oṃ vajra vākka āḥ oṃ vajra citta ya hūṃ. With svabhāva śuddhaḥ sarva dharmāḥ svabhāva śuddho'haṃ vajra śuddhaḥ sarva dharmā vajra śuddho'haṃ yoga śuddha sarva dharmā yoga śuddho'haṃ one should purify the three bodies and generate the pride of the wisdom ḍākinī.

Then with oṃ vajro haṃ one assumes the pride of Vajrasattva, and then one should summon. The wisdom maṇḍala of the pure ground is to be summoned into the maṇḍala of one's own heart, bound with a knot, brought under control and so accomplished. The mantras for this are as follows: oṃ vajra aṃkuśa jaḥ oṃ vajra pāśa hūṃ oṃ vajra spoṭa vaṃ oṃ vajra ghaṇṭe hoḥ. And it is said:

"Binding the wrathful hook,
the hook at the little finger is great as well;
a knot at the shoulder; the two index fingers,
the bracelet, joined back to back, are to be pressed."

These are the mantras and mudrās of summoning. They are the first application, given as instruction.

Saying that, having generated all of them without exception, the yogin with divine pride should meditate on them at the summit of Mount Meru, he spoke the passage beginning:

"At the outset of all the mudrās,
one should bind the pledge mudrā."

Binding the two vajra fists, one places the left at the heart and puts the right fist into the left armpit: that is the pledge mudrā. "As the seed is the vajra itself" is the syllable hūṃ. "With homage and burning set at the ends" is easy to understand. oṃ hūṃ svāhā is the pledge mantra and mudrā.

Saying that lion seats are to be imagined on the five squares of the inner maṇḍala, he says:

"With the five lion seats
one should arrange the five seats."

"Adorned with a white parasol,
one should imagine the disk of the sun"
means that it is to be adorned with such things; that is the whole of it.

"Coming forth from my breath, in the center, the wisdom ḍākinī": the meaning is that, generated from one's own seed and sent forth from the nose, the wisdom ḍākinī is to be set on the seat. The meaning of "with three faces and six arms" is clear. It is to be understood in this way for all of them.

"Arisen from my water" means from the water channel. "Arisen from my fire" means from the fire channel. "Arisen from my wind" means from the wind channel. "To arise from oneself" means from the earth channel. "Outside it, the four" means that the four, the outer ḍākinī and the others, are also to be placed at the four inner doors.

"Before their forms and the rest the pledge too is to be shown": this means that the pledge is to be shown before the wisdom ḍākinī and the others; that is, before the wisdom ḍākinī one shows her pledge: oṃ hūṃ svāhā. "Endowed with that very seed" means that for all of them, first of all, one takes just what was stated above. "Placing the staff at the side" refers to the third, with the curved stroke added. With it they are as follows: oṃ a svāhā oṃ ā svāhā oṃ oṃ svāhā. For the four goddesses of the corners: oṃ ṛ svāhā oṃ ṝ svāhā oṃ ḷ svāhā oṃ ḹ svāhā. For the inner door-guardian goddesses: oṃ phuṃ svāhā oṃ pheṃ svāhā oṃ phroṃ svāhā oṃ phriṃ svāhā.

"The syllable at the drop of nectar" is the syllable sruṃ. "Taking the seed that is last of nine": taking the letter ya as the first, the last of nine is the letter kṣa.

"The third from the first class,
joined with the vowel sign and the drop":
this is the syllable guṃ.

"With a nondual, full torma, one should offer to each with that very thing": this teaches what comes below: one should give the torma to the bhūtas, furnished with the five nectars, the five hooks, food and drink and the like.

"Circling with the lotus circling, one should imagine a lotus on the right; raising the vajra on the left, one should show the great mudrā": the meaning is that one imagines the lotus bhandha on the right hand, meditates on a vajra with the left, binds the mudrā three times, says oṃ ha ho hrīḥ svāhā, and shows it.

Concerning the making of special offerings, he says:

"The one possessed of wisdom
designates the worldly outer maṇḍala."

He says that the meaning comes to this: since offerings accompanied by thorough recitation are fitting, one should set out the transcendent goddesses, and immediately after that one should set the worldly and other goddesses in the outer maṇḍala.

The order of arrangement there is as follows. Outside the vajra garland of the inner maṇḍala, in the east, one sets Pukkasī, the Frowning One and Caṇḍī, holding vessels of nectar water in their hands, red, yellow and black in body color. In the north are the three, Raudrī, the Blood Drinker and Piśācī, holding vessels filled with the five hooks and the like, yellow, white and red in body color. In the west are the Wrathful Lady, the Blazing One and the Terrifying One, holding torma vessels, red, yellow and black in body color. In the south are the Skull-Garlanded One, Vajrī and the Vase Lady, holding vessels of food, drink and the like, black, red and white in body color. In the northeast, the quarter of Īśāna, are Lāsyā and the Lady of Scented Water, white and yellow in color. At the corner of fire is the Lute Lady, wild and blazing, her hair hanging loose, adorned with ornaments of snakes, clothed in garments and ornaments of every kind, seated in the posture of Indra.

Outside that, on the ground of the surrounding circle, in the outer region, in the eight charnel grounds laid out in the eight directions according to the sequence of the mantras of the torma for the bhūtas, one should set the lords of the charnel grounds, the Black Fierce One and the rest. The order there is as follows: in the east, the Black Fierce One; in the north, the Great Fierce One; in the west, Devadatta; in the south, the Black One; in the northeast, the quarter of Īśāna, the Gnasher; at the corner of fire, the Terrifier; in the southwest, the quarter of Nairṛti, Beyond Joy; at the corner of the wind, the King of Obstructors.

Adorned with the garments and ornaments as taught, holding vajra and bell, with the right leg in the sattva posture and the left fully extended, one sits in the posture of play. Making a square maṇḍala of scent and worshipping it with white flowers and the like, one presents at the eastern gate the torma food, fragrant sāli rice with curd, sugarcane, honey and butter, and binding the mudrā of the King of Desire, one should summon the gods with this mantra: oṃ ba ta li mahā pa ta li hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ jaḥ svāhā.

Bind the vajra hook firmly,
Raise the thumb and little finger;
Open the right hand fully

Then, facing west and standing in the round stance, make a triangular maṇḍala with red sandalwood and the like, and adorn it with red flowers and the like. As before, prepare sāli rice with curd and honey, together with the five nectars; set the vessel in front, and as before summon with the mudrā and mantra of Ṭakkirāja.

oṃ maṇibhadrāya svāhā. oṃ pūrṇabhadrāya svāhā. oṃ dhanadāya svāhā. oṃ vaiśravaṇāya svāhā. oṃ kalimālinaye svāhā. oṃ jibikuṇḍaline svāhā. oṃ mukhendraye svāhā. oṃ carendrāya svāhā.

Maṇibhadra and Pūrṇabhadra,

These and the others, together with their retinues:

This is the mantra and mudrā for summoning them. Binding the vajra bond, rotate it and place it at the heart; with that mudrā and with this mantra, oṃ da ni di ba ba ta li hūṃ svāhā, one should please them and then dismiss them just as before. These are the rite of the torma for the yakṣas.

Again, facing south and standing in the vajra stance, make outside a triangular maṇḍala with red sandalwood and the like, scatter red flowers profusely, and invite them with food offerings and the like. Dried fish and dried meat, garlic, asafoetida, vessels of blood, sesame cakes and broths, and above all the five nectars, foods of many kinds to eat, beans with meat, cooked beans, and full vessels are set in front. One should summon with the mantra given above, pa ta li and the rest, and with the two mudrās of Ṭakkirāja; then, touching the vessel with the left hand, recite this mantra:

The Terrifier, the Yoginī who dwells in the lands,
The Fierce-Formed and the Great-Formed,
The Fang-Gnashing Lady and the Great Skull Lady,

The Great Serpent, the Bearer of the Supreme Axe,
Vajrā, and likewise the Bow-Handed Lady,
The divine sovereign ladies,
The very nature of the five ḍākinīs,
Who bring about every rite,
Great queen of the maṇḍala of union,

All, all, are summoned:

oṃ kakka ḍhana babba na dhana khakhkhā da na sarva duṣṭā na hana hana ka ta ya so-and-so hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ phaṭ phaṭ jaḥ svāhā.

Binding the mudrā of Trailokyavijaya, with the two index fingers made like links of a chain, with that mudrā and oṃ tiṣṭha hūṃ bhakṣa bhakṣa phaṭ jjaḥ svāhā one should please them. This is the mantra and mudrā of pleasing. Having pleased them in this way, one should dismiss them with three snaps of the fingers. These are the rite of the torma for the bhūtas.

With the beating of drums and the like, and with everything filled by the sound of bells, having given the torma to the bhūtas with just the mantras and mudrās of the offerings, one should fill the ground where the maṇḍala is to be made.

Close by, outside it, in order to examine the disciples, one should consecrate seven times with the mantra oṃ a ji hva saṃ barta ni hūṃ phaṭ, give the tooth stick and water, and then confer the samaya vow.

There, first, the words of request by the disciple are these: "Consider me, Eternal One of all, Akṣobhya, the one called Ratna, Lokeśvara, Amoghasiddhi and the rest, and all the bodhisattvas! I, so-and-so, from this time onward until I abide in awakening, shall generate the supreme, unsurpassed awakening mind.

Just as the protectors of the three times
Have made themselves certain of awakening,
The three kinds of moral discipline, the training,
The gathering of virtuous dharmas,
And the discipline of acting for the sake of beings,
I shall each hold firmly.
The Buddha, the Dharma and the Saṅgha,
Supreme unsurpassed Three Jewels,
The vow born of union with the Buddha,
First of all I shall hold.
Vajra, bell and mudrā
I shall likewise each hold in suchness.
In the supreme family of the great vajra
I shall hold to the master.
Pure in samaya,
In the supreme family of Ratnasaṃbhava,
Six times each day
I shall give the four kinds of gift.
Through the arising of great awakening,
In the family of the great lotus,
The three vehicles, outer, inner and secret,
The true Dharma, I shall each hold.
Endowed with all the vows,
The act of offering as far as I am able,
In the great supreme family of karma,
I shall likewise each hold in suchness.
Having generated as supreme
The unsurpassed awakening mind,
For the sake of all sentient beings
I shall hold the vows without exception.
Those not ferried across I shall ferry across,
Those not freed I shall free,
Those not relieved I shall relieve,
And I shall set sentient beings in nirvāṇa."

These are the words of the disciple's request. The master answers:

"This is the answer of the glorious supreme one,
From which the fruit of the Great Vehicle arises:
That by which you will be freed
Is that you will become tathāgatas."

Having answered, one should teach the profound Dharma, the Perfection of Wisdom and so on, and afterward place them at the maṇḍala. These are the rite for the disciples' preparation.

Then the vajra master who dwells in the maṇḍala, setting the twentyfold offering before him and ringing the bell, should praise with this vajra song:

Your two feet steal the light of the jewels in the crests that crown the Great God's head;
Ever seated most firmly on your seat, you hold a body embraced about the neck;
You set in motion a net of radiating light, and hold a body born of the secret:
Holy Lord who has become the supreme state, Vajrasattva, to you I bow.

Then one should perform the fire offering in the sequence given above. Taking up the vajra and bell and seated in the maṇḍala house, one worships directly with the twentyfold offering and the rest, and praises with the hundred and eight names and by chanting various vajra songs. Then it is taught that one should give the samaya and the rest.

Mixing the five nectars,
One should meditate on the sun-wiping and the rest.

The meaning of this is as follows: the nectar mixed from the five nectars and the five hooks is to be thoroughly purified in the manner taught for the nectar light, and the samaya is to be fully examined. This is made clear in what is taught:

With hā the color is stolen,
With hoḥ it becomes stainless,
And with hrīḥ, destroyed by the hero:
Thus their sequence is shown.

That is, the purification of the nectar light is made clear.

"Having uttered it three times in three ways" means that one should recite it three times. The thought is this: reciting the mantra with the three natures, one churns three times with Meru, which is generated from the seed explained below; and moreover, immediately after the mantra has been applied, the nectar light too is meditated upon as nothing but the three natures.

"Form and beautiful color, the Lord of the Lotus" refers to Amitābha. In "The essence of urine, the most beautiful Illuminator," the Illuminator is Vairocana. Of the scent, "Vajreśvara, vajra in hand" refers to Akṣobhya.

By the gods of speech
It should be known through the three rites.

This means that the nectar light should be meditated upon as having the nature of the three deities.

On the left, the hand of the moon,
In the center of the moon, the syllable:

This means that on the palm of the left hand, upon a moon disk generated from the nectar syllable, the nectar syllable is again set out.

The nectar, bearing the mark of the All-Knowing:

Here the All-Knowing is Vairocana. His mark is the syllable oṃ. Attached to the nectar syllable, it becomes the syllable oṃ. The import is that one should meditate on that very syllable oṃ in the center of the moon disk.

Likewise the thumb and the earth:

This means that one should meditate on the left thumb by means of the syllable hūṃ.

And with the ring finger, Meru;
The power of churning the nectar:

This means that the left ring finger is meditated upon as Meru, the nature of nectar, and the syllable suṃ is set upon it; then one should churn.

The water should be known as threefold:

Here the water is the nectar light. "Should be known as threefold" means that immediately after churning, one should contemplate it in the manner of three times.

To each of all the yoginīs
One should offer drops flicked with the thumb.

This means that to each of all the yoginīs one should offer drops of nectar, flicking them by snapping the thumb against the ring finger.

Then, by one's own essence,
The work of one's own mind:

This means that afterward one should bless one's own body.

At the stūpa of the five proclaimers
One should wash in three ways.

This means: at the throat of one's own aggregates, upon a lotus wholly produced from the syllable hri, one meditates on the wisdom ḍākinī, generated from the syllable hūṃ, in the manner of a stūpa, at the place of the five proclaimers. Then, wishing to wash away the afflictions, one should wash three times by letting drops of nectar fall.

With those drops at the tip of the tongue,
The one endowed with understanding, at three places:

This means that one should offer drops of nectar precisely at the tongue, the forehead and the crown.

Then to the disciples who have completed the preparation one should give the samaya with this song:

Behold the beautiful, holy Dharma!
In this you must not doubt.
Brahmin, dog and caṇḍāla:
Regarding them as one in nature, partake.

The disciples, for their part, should bind the mudrā three times, with the lotus mudrā circling before them, and accept it with this song:

Come in bliss, supreme Dharma,
This, free of the stain of desire,
Released from apprehended and apprehender:
To suchness I bow.

Then the disciples who are to be brought in are directly purified by sprinkling with urine; they are made to bind flowers properly in their joined palms, their faces are covered with silk, and they are set at the gate of the maṇḍala, and one should give them the vows. There, first, the disciple requests:

I go for refuge to the Three Jewels,
I confess every misdeed, one by one,
I rejoice in the merit of beings,
And I set my mind on a buddha's awakening.

Then, with svabhāva śuddhaḥ sarva dharmaḥ svabhāva śuddho'haṃ and the rest given above, one should recite the joining of the three bodies, and setting out great love and the rest one by one, make three prostrations, saying: oṃ pañca ḍākīni pūjopasthānāya ātmanaṃ niryatayāmi, oṃ ācarya pūjopasthanāya ātmanaṃ nīryatayāni, oṃ sakala satva paritranāya ātmanaṃ nīryatayāmi. Having said these, one makes the three prostrations.

When the master has given the vows, he says samaya stvaṃ samaya ho three times, and then performs this blessing of the body by the three vajras:

āḥ, supreme of all wisdom,
Blesses speech as vajra;
hūṃ burns up the afflictions
And blesses the heart with the vajra;
And by oṃ, which draws in wisdom,
The body too is blessed as vajra.

And: oṃ hi hi ma na gur ni ta vajra ca le yuṃ yuṃ hūṃ svāhā.

This wisdom of the one who has wisdom:
Today, taste the nectar!

Having said this, he consecrates it three times with the mantra oṃ a svāhā, and should give into the mouth the nectar light consecrated with the five nectars and the five hooks.

Then, as for causing the descent upon the disciple: at the disciple's heart, upon a wind maṇḍala arisen from the syllable yuṃ, one imagines that very syllable yuṃ; likewise one sets yoṃ on the soles of both feet. Then the master, with the pride of the wisdom ḍākinī, terrifies with the vajra bell and with roaring; likewise, drawing forth the wind of the great Caṇḍālī that dwells in his own heart, he should make the disciple shake with this mantra: oṃ le lai hūṃ a svāhā. If the disciple does not shake through this, then one should cause the descent again by the sequence given above.

Immediately after the descent has taken place, one fills the space with the sounds of various vajra songs, and after that one should pacify the descent with this mantra: oṃ tiṣṭha vajra samaya pra ti ba le te hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ ho ho ho svāhā. Then the disciple is made to cast a flower, and the deity on whom it falls is the one whose accomplishment the disciple attains.

Then, with the mantra jñāna cakra hūṃ, the face covering is removed and the reality of the deities is shown; one should show them one by one, beginning with the Skeleton Terrifier and ending with the wisdom ḍākinī. Then the disciple touches the master's feet with the head, offers the fee, and should request whatever accomplishment is desired.

The disciple, with palms placed at the heart as before, is made to recite three times: oṃ vajra maṇḍal pra be so ahaṃ, oṃ yoga maṇḍal pradarśana ahaṃ, oṃ guhya maṇḍal mahā maṇḍal dṛṣṭi hi ahaṃ samaya ho. Then one should set out the eight limbs as before. Next, with samaya ahaṃ hūṃ vajra ahaṃ oṃ hud gato ahaṃ a aḥ oṃ oṃ hūṃ svāhā one should generate the pride of the deity, and also have the disciple say jñāna kāya ahaṃ.

One who is thus firmly established should then be empowered. With the vase of the southern gate the disciple empowerment is given; with that of the west, the mantra empowerment; with that of the north, the tantra empowerment. Seated on the seat facing east with the bearing of a universal monarch, once the disciple has been made to generate the awakening mind, one should empower with this:

oṃ With the vase of wisdom water,
By the stream of the vajra hook,
Empowered, you attain awakening;
Today you are freed from saṃsāra.
oṃ aḥ phroṃ phreṃ phaṭ hūṃ svāhā.

Then one should protect the eight places: binding the vajra chain mudrā, one blesses them with the mantra oṃ vajra ka ya hūṃ aḥ svāhā, and with the mantra given above, oṃ vajra ghaṇṭe raṇita bra ra ṇi ta and the rest, one should confer the vajra and bell empowerment. Binding the mudrā of the mind consort Vajrī, bending the two index fingers and joining their tips, with oṃ hūṃ a svāhā one should confer empowerment with the crown of the five buddhas.

Then one should give the prediction. After that, the disciple goes seven steps to the east, puts on clothing, and returns from there; then one should teach the supremacy of the three vehicles, and also teach the stages of blessing. In this sequence the others too should be brought in.

Then the disciple should offer to the master as a fee whatever substance is hard to give away and whatever substance he is attached to. As it is also taught:

Whatever substance is hard to give up entirely,
Or whatever is exceedingly special,
That and that, to the master,
Offer, wishing it to be inexhaustible.

Then the worship of the circle of heroes is begun. Its sequence is as follows. Having set out seats of varied arrangement in the portions of the eight directions of the maṇḍala, those who dwell in the feast gathering together with the goddesses should assemble various flowers, incense, scents, flower garlands, washing water, various foods, drinks, garments, ornaments and the like, as befits yogins.

There, first, when the circle of heroes is worshipped with the five offerings and the rest, the nectar light endowed with the ten substances is purified in the sequence given above and consecrated with mantra, and with that very mudrā one should offer the samaya to each one with this song: "Behold the beautiful, holy Dharma!" and so on. The others too should receive it with "Come in bliss, supreme Dharma," and so on. Having uttered the song and bowed, preceded by circling the hands, with the song accompanied by the mudrā three times, with a mind that is thoroughly settled and pure, one skilled in the mantra tantras, having tamed the senses and generated compassion for all realms of beings, should begin the mantra of the samaya mudrā, and not otherwise. This is taught:

As in the conduct of suchness,
By the yogin for whom all is pure.

And:

Joining with the deity of one's own awareness,
With the two tips of thumb and ring finger
Tasting food and drink,
One attains everlasting accomplishment.

This very thing the Blessed One made clear: "'Enjoy it as is blissful,' he said," and so on. There, "as is blissful" means "in whatever way it becomes bliss." Here too, the word "bliss" is spoken in the great sense. And so on, as far as:

Through suchness, the supreme of the very substance of joy,
Brahmā, is liberation into nirvāṇa.

In this, it is the one-pointed mind attained by thoroughly meditating on the vajra. Therefore the thought is this: one should proceed in such a way that the reality that has become one-pointed does not change into anything else.

Outside the red maṇḍala
Made with three kharaṅgas:

This means that outside the wheel one makes a triangular maṇḍala with red sandalwood and the like, and summons through the eastern gate. "Red flowers, incense fire," and so on: the meaning is easy to understand. With "oṃ At the lone tree and the charnel ground," and so on, the torma for the bhūtas is to be fully arranged by the sequence given above.

Filling the mouth with nectar,
Whatever it is, and so on, is begun:

This means that one should fill one's own mouth with the nectar light endowed with the ten substances, and, preceded by contemplating the rite one wishes, scatter it onto the torma with the syllable phruṃ. Then one binds two vajra fists:

Joining them back to back,
Entwining them with the two ring fingers,
Wrathful, in the manner of a chain.

And saying oṃ bhakṣaya bhakṣaya sarva yoga yogīśvari hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ jja svāhā, one should give the torma to the bhūtas for the sake of pacifying and the rest.

Then, in the worship in particular,
Thoroughly abandon thinking and not thinking.

This means that, without conceptual thought about what may be eaten and what may not, and so on, one should worship with the samaya, which is especially of the nature of virtue.

The five, the hook and the rest,
Should be regarded as the five realities.

This means that the five hooks, and, from the words "and the rest," the nectars as well, should be made into the five suchnesses by the characteristics that will be given below. The meaning is that one should meditate on them as having the nature of the five buddhas.

"What is called the great hook" means human flesh. "Of the five wisdoms" means that it is held to be of their very nature.

"The imperturbable vajra hook" means elephant flesh; it is of the nature of Akṣobhya.

"The mother's hook, endowed with the jewel" means dog flesh; it is of the nature of Ratnasambhava.

"The Victor's hook, the unfailing" means the flesh of an ox; it is of the nature of Amoghasiddhi.

"The moving hook, Amitābha" means horse flesh; it is of the nature of Amitābha.

Of the five hooks and the rest,
Thoroughly apply the suchness of all.

Here "the suchness of all" is nirvāṇa, and "applying it" means that it is the condition. These, moreover, are taught to be of the nature of Vairocana, Akṣobhya, Ratnasambhava, Amitābha and Amoghasiddhi. "And all the deities that one desires": because they are of the very nature of the five buddhas, they accomplish what is desired. With all of these alone the yoginīs should be satisfied, and not with anything else; thus it is said:

All the yoginīs should be pleased,
And likewise, not as one pleases,

and so on. "One's own desire" and the rest have already been clearly explained.

In its center, the heroes
Thoroughly apply āli and kāli.

This means the feast gathering of heroes, made utterly pure by applying the vowels and consonants that will be given below. It is evident that, as soon as this is done, one should perform the union of entering nondual absorption.

By thoroughly applying āli and kāli,
All the deities are to be pleased.

This means that by thoroughly applying that alone, one should make the secret offering of passion to all the buddhas and satisfy them.

Arising from the midst of that,
One should taste all the flavors of nectar.

The meaning is that the nectar arising from that which is of the nature of union should be tasted by all the yogins and yoginīs, and they should be satisfied.

"All the remaining substances" means whatever remains of what the circle of heroes has eaten and drunk.

Apply them to the outer maṇḍala.

This means that the torma of the remainder should be carried to crossroads and other places outside the maṇḍala. For that, too, the sequence is as follows:

Binding two vajra fists,
One should raise the two index fingers;
Bending their tips, at the mouth
One should set them in the manner of fangs.

That is the mudrā. And:

oṃ, as it is called, battāli battāli che,
Dweller in the charnel ground, whose face is accomplishment,
Fierce One, Terrifier, preta-mouthed,
Guardian of samaya, lover of blood,
Crying out kili, holder of union,
In the midst of the corpse grove of the charnel ground,
Together with your hosts and messengers,
Gnasher, Fierce One, Smoke-and-Ember Lady,
Take it, eat it, and abiding in the samaya,
Please remain and bring it under your power!
oṃ gṛhṇa gṛhṇa sarva duṣṭāna praduṣṭāna bhakṣa bhakṣa phruṃ phruṃ phruṃ phaṭ svāhā

These are the mudrā and mantra for blessing the torma of the remainder. Having worshipped with the five offerings, one should snap the fingers three times and send it off.

Then, holding the vajra and bell, the praise to the entire retinue of heroes is taught:

Taking up the melody of hūṃ,
Proclaiming auspiciousness, one should rejoice.

The sequence is as follows: "Joined in union, mutually embracing," and so on. Having praised them by reciting praises with the melodies of the various vajras, one should also worship with the twentyfold offering. Having also praised them with the hundred and eight names beginning "Great being, first and supreme," one should present the offering, and the dedication of the roots of virtue is taught. Having made the request,

Whatever rite of armor I have performed,
I pray the Blessed One to forbear,

one should bind the vajra palms, place them at the heart, and, for the sake of fully liberating the realm of sentient beings without exception and without remainder, protect with these words: "May all sentient beings be free of illness," and so on.

Having made the dedication, one should urge them to depart with this verse:

You have accomplished the aims of all sentient beings;
Having gone to the buddha realm,
Please come here once again.

Firmly binding vajra fists,
Placing the left at the heart,
Stretching out the right hand,
Set it on the ground and request them to depart.

And one should send them off with this mantra: oṃ ātma tiṣṭha hūṃ svāhā.

Then, from one's own breath,
One should dissolve them into one's own body.

This means that afterward one should have them enter through the breath of one's mouth into one's own heart and dissolve there; the meaning is that one should make them into a single essence, like milk and water mixed together. As it is also said:

By union, by mingled thought,
As the maṇḍala, so the tathāgatas.
Reciting at morning and evening,
One abides fully in each and every one.
Whatever is the path of the senses,
Whatever is, in its very nature,
Through the union of the unequaled
Draws all the buddhas into equality.

Through the union of a moment
One should worship one's own nature.

Here "a moment" means: imagining oneself as having the nature of the wisdom ḍākinī, and having worshipped with the five offerings and the rest, one should set out the eightfold practice, and also show the samaya as before. Then one should likewise worship the outer deities in just the same way and send them off with this mantra: oṃ duṣṭā praduṣṭāna haṇa grihṇa grihna kha kha khāhi khāhi gaccha gaccha hūṃ phaṭ. Snapping the fingers three times, one should send them off.

With oṃ kili kinatya hūṃ jaḥ one should gather up the colored powders and cast them into a clean river. Then, having anointed the ground of the maṇḍala with scents and the like, one should draw an eight-petaled lotus with yellow powder, and on it recite the Perfection of Wisdom, make offerings, and so forth.

This completes the explanation of the third chapter, the maṇḍala rite of the Seat of the Other, from the Glorious Catuṣpīṭha.

Now Vajrapāṇi, wishing to make manifest the special essential nature of the five nectars, the five hooks, the vowels, the consonants, the light of nectar, the outer mudrā and the rest, began the fourth chapter of the Seat of the Other with the words "The divisions of the five nectars," and so on.

First, the nectar, the vajra water,
Arising as the self of the five.

Here "first" means that first the awakening mind will be explained. "The nectar, the vajra water" is the awakening mind. "Of the five" means of the five essential natures. "Arising as the self" means born from the self; the meaning is this: it has the nature of the five elements of the bodhisattva that arise from one's own ground. The commentary states just this:

Warm to the touch, it is fire.

That is, the nature that is touched as warmth is the fire element.

Appearing as smoke, it is wind.

This means that it is seen to rise upward in the form of wind; therefore it has the characteristic of constant movement.

The moist substance is water.

This means that since moisture too is present, it is the water element.

Appearing as form, it is earth.

This means that because it is perceived as having the nature of atoms, roughness too is present; therefore it is the earth element.

Blessed as the wisdom vajra.

This means that because it is of one taste with the nature of the wisdom of emptiness, it is the space element.

The five deities should be meditated on.

The meaning is that what has the nature of the five elements should be meditated on as the essential nature of the five tathāgatas.

All the sphere of water, entirely.

This means that this too, without remainder, is chiefly the real nature of water; because it arises in dependence, it should be thoroughly perceived as emptiness. "Fire likewise" means that the sphere of fire too is empty, because its very nature is dependent arising. By the word "also," earth and the rest should likewise be diligently known in their nature.

Now the seeds to be set upon those very elements are taught: "As pure, the pure wind," and so on. Here "as pure" is the letter ha. "The pure wind" is the letter ya. "The meaning of faith" is the letter sa. "Of earth" is the letter ha. "Power" is the letter kā.

To the five letters of wisdom,
Join them all, mingling the drop.

The meaning is that those five letters of the Dharma should be properly joined with the drop, that is, the upper drop, the nāda, and with the subscript letter u.

In their center, upon a moon,
The nectar letter in the center.

Here "their" means "of those." "In the center, a moon" is the crescent moon located at the center of the head. "The nectar letter in the center" means that one should imagine the transformed letter sa in the middle of that drop and crescent moon.

In the teaching of the fifth of the five,
The self purified, the vajra water.

Here "the fifth" is the nature of the five buddhas. "Of the five," again, is the five seeds. "Teaching" is recitation. "The self purified" means that one should purify one's own body. "The vajra water" is the awakening mind. What does it become through this? The awakening mind having the nature of the five elements.

Together with the four, Vairocana and the rest, of the five nectars taught outwardly, and with the five seeds taught above, the body consciousness blessed by desire and the rest applies itself to what is to be known; and so here too a union with the nectar letter, the awakening mind alone, unmixed, is taught. Because excrement, urine and the rest are set out, it is taught as the essence. Because it is the cause that generates the three worlds, the awakening mind is taught as chiefly pure, and not the others. As it is also said:

The nectar is called the awakening mind;
From it the three classes of beings are born.

Clarifying just the meaning explained, it is said:

Through definite freedom from thing and non-thing,
Delusion is thereby cast far away.

Here by the word "thing," all phenomena are perceived as existent. By the word "non-thing," those same phenomena are perceived as nonexistent. "Definite freedom" means whatever abides having fully abandoned both of these. The meaning is that then delusion and the rest are cast far away. As it is also said:

Just as human food is enjoyed in common,
Just as the sun is seen in common by those who see,
And just as the wind is common to all the bells,
The ocean of conceptual delusion should be cast far away.

Thus, without the conceptual distinction of the purified substances into food and non-food, the fire offering in the hearth, taught with the characteristics that come below, is to be performed. So it is said:

The outer nectar, the good food,
Is like what is cooked by the churning fire.

Here "the outer nectar" is butter, honey, milk, sugarcane and the like. "Churning" is digestion. "Cooked by fire" means by the outer fire alone. The idea is this: just as fruits and the like, ripened and produced by the outer fire, and the especially refined essence of molasses and the like, are accepted because one desires them, so here too there is no contradiction in the holy ones adopting and accepting excrement, urine and the like, ripened and produced by the fire of wisdom; for all things are fit through nonconceptuality alone. As it is also said:

Whoever sees the suchness of one thing
Sees the suchness of all things.
Whatever is the nature of all things,
That is the nature of one thing.

And it is also said:

By the power of method and wisdom
There is nothing at all that may not be done.

Clarifying just the meaning taught, it is said: "The belly and the rest, by the fire of wisdom," and so on. Here "the belly" is the inside of the abdomen. By the words "and the rest," the sense sources such as the eye are meant. "By the fire of wisdom" means the fire of the deities and so on. "Burned" means ripened. "Excrement and urine" means excrement and the like.

By this the following is taught: excrement, urine and the rest, fully ripened by the fire of wisdom in the belly and the rest, become exceedingly special. Therefore the wise should not think, "Excrement, urine and the rest are utterly at odds with food offered to the deities," for the very purpose is to destroy the conceptual thought of impurity and the like. As it is also said in the Net of Illusion (Māyājāla):

Since the elements arise from the great elements,
How could they be called impure?

That which is ripened by fire,
Cooked in the midst of fire,
Is to be burned in the midst of fire.

Here "ripened by fire" means Vairocana and the rest. "Of fire" means the fire of wisdom. "In its midst" means in the midst of the fire of wisdom. "To be burned" means the fire offering is to be performed. The meaning is this: just as the outer fire, as a cooperating condition, makes what is to be produced, fragrant sāli rice and the like, into cooked rice and the like, a special condition producing something special and thus becoming exceedingly distinguished, so here too the substance of excrement, urine and the like, produced by the cooperating condition of the fire of wisdom, is called cooked sāli rice and the like. That purified substance is to be offered into the fire. Therefore Vairocana and the rest are to be presented as a fire offering into the fire of wisdom without the conceptual thought of pure and impure and the like.

Whose vajra water seed,
Likewise the seed should be recited.

Here "vajra" means the vajra imagined in the inner hearth. "The vajra seed" is now the recitation of the letter hūṃ. "Likewise the seed" means that having imagined the letter hūṃ in the middle of the vajra vase, it then comes to be endowed with the seed; that is all.

In its center, of the blazing sun,
Visualize the letter of fire.

This means that one should imagine the ḍākinī abiding in the blazing nature of the fire letter kṣuṃ, and make the offering into the middle of that blazing sun.

"Form and beautiful color are the Lord of the Lotus," and the rest have already been explained.

Now, because the awakening mind alone, arising from method and wisdom, has the nature of the sphere of reality (dharmadhātu), it is designated by the word "vajra"; so it is said: "What is called āli is the bee," and so on. Here āli, bee, vajra and Terrifier are synonyms. That very thing, like space reaching its farthest limit, is taught to pervade everything by the nature of the sphere of reality:

Āli is the boundless sky,
The nature of the sphere of reality.
Moving and unmoving, the holder of the secret:
All bodies arise from it.

Because it holds the secret nature of everything in a secret form, it is the holder of the secret.

Where does that nectar abide? It is said: "In the eighteen limbs of the body." The meaning is that it abides in the eighteen places, such as the breasts. On that very point it is said: "The five nectars of all the faculties." The meaning is that because they abide in all the faculties, they are of their nature.

Drawing it out from the center,
One should dissolve it into the center of āli.

This means that, drawing it out from those faculties, one should place it in the center of āli, in the secret vajra. The meaning is that, gathering it in from the eighteen places, one should make the eight signs.

"Terrifier" is said of the vajra nectar; it is its nature. "Terrifier" means "filled": the nature of nectar, having filled all sentient beings, sports exceedingly and knows all. Because it comes to pervade and abide in the three realms, it is called Terrifier. As it is also said:

It fills all things,
It sports at all times,
And since it abides everywhere,
It is therefore called the Terrifier.

"By the vajra sound" means the letter hūṃ.

The substance of union will come forth.

This means that it is to be brought forth by sending it out with the sound of the letter hūṃ. As soon as the yogin's substance is born through the sequence of the vidyā, the essential nature of the supreme Brahmā is born. As it is also said:

Through the union of vajra and lotus,
Vajrasattva, equal to oneself.

Then, wishing to explain the vajra sound by its essential nature, it is said: "By the seventh of fire and wind," and so on; its meaning has already been explained. "The seed above sound and the rest" is the letter ā.

One should hold it in its center.

This means that in the center of the vajra one should imagine the letter hūṃ upon the moon maṇḍala that arises from the melting of the letter a.

Having thus explained the essence of āli, and now wishing to explain the suchness of kāli, it is said: "Know the flower of kāli fully." Here kāli is the source of phenomena (dharmodaya). "Know the flower fully" means that it is called both "flower" and "fully knowing"; that is all.

The vajra-bearer that reaches the flower.

This means that because it is of the nature of the lotus, what reaches it, having reached it, is the vajra. Because that alone reaches the flower, this was already explained above with the word "bee."

In all that arises from saṃsāra,
All the flowers are the ground.

This means that because it is the very generator of all who take birth in saṃsāra, it is the mother. It is specified by "of that very thing, all the grounds," that is, "the very place of birth of all."

Again and again it is specially taught:

This ocean of the water of wisdom,
With the makaras and fish of discernment,

Here "this ocean of the water of wisdom" means an ocean filled with the water of nectar. Alternatively, because it is the cause of the reality of Vajradhara, the very ground of the jewel of the awakening mind endowed with various methods, it is the ocean. "With the makaras and fish of discernment" means that whatever power draws the whole realm of sentient beings out of saṃsāra is expressed by the word "discernment." That, moreover, takes various forms, like makaras, fish and so on.

In the midst of the nondual mud,
By the sprouts of method,

Here "nondual" is the lotus of union. "Mud" is the sign of method.

"That very sprout"; that is all.

"Like the bandhūka flower in bloom," and the lines that follow teach the aspect of color in the center of kāli itself.

Honey is the abode of nectar.

The meaning is that the essence of honey is the basis of nectar.

Kāli, by that very nectar.

This means that because it is the support of that nectar, kāli too is of the nature of nectar; that is all.

How is the lotus thus meditated upon to be opened? It is said: "The water-born with its flower opened." Here "flower" refers to the special lotus. "Opened" means with its mouth opened. "Water-born" is the lotus. Hence it will be explained that the secret flower, the lotus, is to be opened.

By what is it to be opened? It is said: "The doubled letter with the drop," and so on. Here the doubled letter is kṣa. "With the drop" means at its head, in the manner of a crescent moon, with the stroke of the drop above it.

One should join what leads to the root.

This means that below it one should join the subscript letter u. The meaning is this: upon the moon maṇḍala that arises from the letter ā in the heart of the lotus, one should set the seed of the place of birth.

Its seed letters,
The mother of the family of the goddess herself.

This means that the place where that seed letter is set comes to be of the essence of the fierce ḍākinī; that is all. "The mother of the family" means that as soon as she is thus born, she becomes the mother of the family. As it is also said:

Tārā, the White-Robed Lady (Pāṇḍaravāsinī),
Buddhalocanā and Māmakī,
And the Perfection of Wisdom goddesses:
These goddesses are the mothers of the families.

Imagining well in the center of the moon,
One should imagine the seed of nectar.

This means that the goddess too should meditate on the letter upon the moon maṇḍala that arises from the letter ā in the source of phenomena.

What is called āli is the bee;
Kāli is the Tathāgata.

This means that the Tathāgata is to be expressed in accordance with the meaning of the word "bee." Just as the word "flower" and the word "kāli" share a common meaning, so the Tathāgata too is to be expressed.

What arises and is born from both
Is the supreme rite of nectar.

Here "both" are method and wisdom. "What arises and is born" is the action that arises from them. The idea is that what is born in the union of method and wisdom is made into the exceedingly special vajra nectar. It is to be expressed in words. Therefore, in brief, what is called the nectar of the awakening mind, generated from the union of the lotus and the vajra, which become wisdom and method, is to be called nectar, because it is the condition of nirvāṇa; for the condition is figuratively designated as the goal.

Moreover, because it has the nature of the deity, the Dharma syllables are taught: "As the third of the third class," and so on.

As the third letter
Of the third class is to be taken,

This is the letter ḍa.

At its side the staff, the vajra,

This means that at its side one should add the staff stroke of the letter ra.

One should wind it with a chain.

This means that between it and the two staffs one should link it with the stroke of a chain. By this it takes the form of the letter.

Because the letter ā is the condition for the arising of all phenomena, the letter a is renowned in all the tantras: ākaro mukhaṃ sarva dharmāṇāṃ ādyanutpannatvāta.

It is taught strictly in order:

The suchness of the five nectars.

Here "that" is the letter a. Because the letter a, having been transformed, generates them, the five nectars, the awakening mind and the rest, become suchness. It is evident that this is because they are meditated upon as the nature of the five tathāgatas. Alternatively: "The letter a alone is the suchness of the five nectars."

For that very reason it is said to be fortunate for abandoning the view of self and the rest held by those of wrong views:

One should cleanse one's own body.

Whoever directly knows the nectar
Through the distinctions of the five nectars,

This means that those who come to know the essence of the distinctions of the nectar, the awakening mind, as the five nectars, will have the path of the tathāgatas made manifest. Therefore it is said:

Having looked upon all the sounds of beings,
In whom the body is pure,

Here, whoever has the pure body, the pure body that is the essence of the body of awakening, will attain buddhahood and the state of Vajradhara; that is the meaning. Clarifying just that, it is said:

Whoever, filled with nectar,
Should taste the nectar at will.

That is, one should purify rakta and the rest.

Now, wishing to explain the distinctions of the five nectars and the five hooks, it is said:

Listen to the vajra reality,
Through the distinctions of the five foods.

If one asks how these ten substances are the five foods, the meaning is this: making each five and five into one, they are called the five foods because they are applied together.

Whoever is held by this knowledge
Will quickly see the path.

Here "whoever ... by knowledge" means that one should know the essential nature of the practice. The meaning is that if the practitioner meditates on and contemplates the deity, he quickly sees the path, and by that quickly sees great bliss.

"Great intelligence" is the awakening mind. "With which" is rakta. "Of noble family" is the vajra water. "Generated" is Vairocana. "Crystal" in this context refers to mucus and the like. "Should be practiced" means that one should engage with a nature made pure, free from the conceptual thought of apprehended and apprehender. "Yogin" means that it is practiced by yogins. "The great hook" is the great flesh.

By reciting the seed of consciousness,
By it the seed is held.

Here the seed of consciousness is the letter a. What it fully generates are the five nectars and the great flesh. Therefore, since the five nectars and the great flesh have a single seed, the seed is taught after the great flesh. "By reciting" means that, honoring with that mantra, one should recite the mantra. The Dharma syllables of the five hooks have already been explained above.

Now the seeds for purifying these are set out; it is said: "By the single hook of the obstructors, joined to its path," and so on. Here the hook of the obstructors is elephant flesh; it should be purified with the letter hūṃ. "The lord of the land, the great hook" is dog flesh; it should be purified with the letter ghriṃ. To make clear that horse flesh is to be purified with the letter ji, it is said: "The horse hook, blessed," and so on. That cow flesh is to be purified with the letter sri is taught by "Blessed by the vajra hook," and so on. The seed of the great hook has already been clearly explained in the explanation of the five nectars; it is not set out here.

As for the fragrant substances and the rest made by mixing the five nectars and the five hooks, one should recite each of their own Dharma syllables, one by one, enclosing it in the manner of the armpit, together with namaḥ and the burning offering; and one should apply them by fumigating with smoke, anointing, making them into pills, making them into a tilaka mark, and so on. So it is said: "Joining them with the ten substances," and so on.

Beginning with "The accomplishment of the four yoginīs," and up to the end of the verse "Into the midst of the yoginīs I come; please open the door," the accomplishment of butter lamps and the like is taught. Further, beginning with "The characteristics of the mudrā of speech," and up to

The yogin and the yoginī, delighting, sport:
These are the mudrās of speech and sign,

the signs of mudrā are taught.

This completes the Seat of the Other from the Vision of the Glorious Catuṣpīṭha, composed by the glorious vajra master Kalyāṇavarman.

Vajrapāṇi, wishing to understand the division into four realities, began the Seat of Union with "Further, the teaching of the characteristics," and so on. With "First, the vajra reality," and so on, the four realities are divided one by one and made understood. Here the reality of the vajra is the ultimate nature itself, because it is the essential nature of the five wisdoms. The reality of the bell, because it follows sound, is the nature of the Perfection of Wisdom; that is all. The suchness of the rosary means that it is meditated upon as the nature of all the tathāgatas. The suchness of wisdom is the culmination, expanded by its essential nature, of the meaning of the words, as it fully abides. Whoever knows that reality attains unsurpassed nirvāṇa, the perfection of the knowable; so it is said:

Through the four limbs of suchness,
Beyond what is desired.

Here, first making the vajra reality understood, it is said: "In the center, the deity Vairocana," and so on. There:

In the center, in the thirteenth egg,
One should dissolve the wisdom deity himself.

Here "thirteen" means the ranks of those deities. The place where they are generated is the egg, that is, the egg of Brahmā. "The wisdom deity himself": Mahāvairocana, Vajradhara, Samantabhadra and the Supreme Glorious One are synonyms. The meaning is this: the import is that one should meditate on Mahāvairocana in the center of the vajra vase.

Again, that the deities as taught should be set out on the lotus and the five prongs below the vajra vase is taught by "The lotus, the eight goddesses," and so on.

The four, the vajra hook and the rest,
Likewise in the direction of the garland of the navel.

The import is that the four, the vajra hook and the rest, should be set in the four directions of the vajra vase where Mahāvairocana is placed.

That this very vajra, as taught, should be imagined in just that way is taught by "Whoever contemplates the vajra reality, the vajra self."

That on the bell too one should meditate on the eight goddesses, Tārā and the rest, in the eight directions and intermediate directions, is taught:

To be known in the eight directions,
They are renowned as the eight goddesses:
Beautiful-Colored, the Sweetener, the Radiant One,
And fourth, the Vajra Garland Lady.

These are the four goddesses Cundā, Ratnolkā, Bhṛkuṭī and Vajraśṛṅkhalā.

Forms in the center of space.

This means that the Perfection of Wisdom goddess should be set in the center; that is all.

A yogin who has meditated in this way does not attain the supreme accomplishment without recitation. Wishing to make this understood, the reality of the rosary is set forth; it is said: "Listen, true Vajra," and so on. That the rosary is to be recited in the manner of round beads is taught by "The accomplishment of mantra is fifty," and so on. Or, if one asks how it is to be recited, it is said:

The vajra lady is truly the left;
The right is the essence of the vajra.

The meaning is that the mantras of the wisdom ḍākinī and the rest are drawn with the left, and the mantras of Vajrasattva and the rest are drawn with the right.

Thinking, "Without understanding its reality one does not attain the accomplishment of all that is to be done," the reality of wisdom is explained:

The divisions of the reality of wisdom:
Listen, true great Vajra.

And so on. There:

It abides as the very distinctions of wisdom.

Here "the distinctions of wisdom" are the distinctions of black, yellow and the rest. "Abides as such" means that, their natures mingling, they fully abide. As it is also said:

All things, by their nature,
Fully abide together with their nature;
Both one's own entity and another's entity,
Since they have aspects, are conventional.

The idea is this: having made it understood by setting aside objections on both sides, all of these are mind alone. Making just that clear, it is said: "Speaking of the common wisdom," and so on. Here "nondual" means appearing undivided. "And the second" means appearing separately, divided. "The two rites" means fully realizing it as utterly divided.

The meaning is this: whatever arises through the undivided, the divided and the utterly divided, all of it is consciousness alone. For it is said, "O children of the Victors, the three realms are mind only."

Making just that clear, it is said, "Yakṣas, asuras and siddhas," and so on. That is to say, while it is wisdom alone that abides, it appears separately, distinguished according to beings.

If one asks how this is so, it is said, "By which the particular wisdom," and so on. There, "the twenty-three gods" means all those from the Four Great Kings up to the king of the gods of Akaniṣṭha. "Where it increases twofold" means that consciousness, becoming ever more distinguished, increases twofold in the order of the higher levels.

Again and again it is spoken of distinctively:

The wisdoms of the five desires:
Wisdom upon wisdom increases higher.

Here the meaning is this: from the first, the Four Great Kings, up to all those as far as the king of the gods of Paranirmitavaśavartin, the wisdom of the five objects of desire increases distinctively.

"Enjoyment, liberation, means and desire" refers to undertaking the state of the higher realms and of nirvāṇa. "The yoga treatise, the essence itself" means that this form is attained by growing familiar with the union of the essence. Wishing to make just that clear, it is said:

The essence of essences is the treatise;
The path of yoga should be beheld.

This is the explanation of the first chapter of the Seat of Union, from the examination of the Glorious Catuṣpīṭha.

It is said that, having fully realized suchness, one should accomplish the rites of pacifying and so on: "The particulars of accomplishing the wheel," and so on. There, "Abiding in the middle of the first and the last": here the first and the last are the letter oṃ and the letter hā.

First, in the central square,
Of all that is to be arranged,
Having imagined oneself in the center:

this is the seed of the wisdom ḍākinī. "On the eight letters of the eighth" means that the seeds beginning with the letter suṃ are to be placed in the eight intermediate squares. "On the twelve seeds of sound" means that the twelve seeds of sound are arranged in the twelvefold squares. "As with the third group" means that its letters are to be taken in their proper order: the fifteen letters of the three groups are to be taken. "With one remaining letter" means that the first of the fourth group, the letter ta, is to be taken; together with that letter ta, sixteen are to be arranged in the twelve squares.

Just as, from the first group,
One takes the initial letter,
Presses it with the hook,
Attaches the letter ra at its base,
And above it drops of nectar:

this is the letter kruṃ.

The letter doubled as the seed,
And at its base a vowel is joined:

this is the letter kṣuṃ. "What is called nectar is placed at the end": here what is called nectar is the letter sa. "Placed at the end" means it is to be placed beside the letter kṣuṃ. As soon as this is done, one should recite oṃ kruṃ svāhā. Even if homage and svāhā placed at the end are not taught here, they should nevertheless be understood here as implied. As it is also said:

Generally, in all mantras,
There is homage and svāhā.

Therefore, even though they are not explicitly prescribed for the nature itself, those two should be inserted as implied.

"Nectar together with all its curved signs" means the letter suṃ; that is, it is endowed with a bindu and a vowel. "Having imagined the hook letter" means the letter kṣuṃ. "With the seventh joined to a vowel" means the letter ṛ, the seventh of the sound letters, or else the letter e. "Wisdom consciousness" means the letter hūṃ. "In the directions, the four nectars" means a āḥ aṃ āḥ. "Endowed with all the subscribed signs" means that they are endowed with the small letter a, the bindu and the visarga, and that homage and svāhā are to be joined to them.

The two hands of the great mudrā
Are placed at the place of the heart:

this means that one should meditate on the Blessed One Vairocana with the mudrā of supreme awakening. This is the second chapter of the Seat of Union, from the examination of the Glorious Catuṣpīṭha.

It is said that, by these and other means, the yogin who has accomplished the rites should bless others: "What is the sequence of blessing? By which sentient beings is it realized?" and so on. The Blessed One spoke of just that again. Beginning with

Having imagined a moon at the heart,
Meditating on the seed hūṃ in its center,

and continuing up to the end,

By one endowed with the nondual torma,
Having been empowered, one becomes peaceful,

by all of this the sequence of blessing is taught. This is the third chapter of the Seat of Union, from the examination of the Glorious Catuṣpīṭha.

Now, it is said that one should apply the union of vajra and bell and so on, and how flowers and the rest are to be applied inseparably. With "Vajrapāṇi, wishing to know the essence of nondual union," and so on, the fourth chapter of the Seat of Union begins. Answering again through union with the equality of all phenomena, which is free from the conceptuality of apprehended and apprehender, it is said, "That very nondual union, and the vajra, bell and mudrā," and so on. The meaning is that the meditation on the vajra, the bell and the rest that is spoken of is to be undertaken in the sequence that will be shown below.

If one asks how it is to be undertaken, it is said:

That vajra is the self;
The bell is the dhāraṇī of the Dharma.

There, "That vajra is the self itself" means this: because of dependent origination, when examined ultimately, one's own aggregates themselves are transformed into the vajra, because like a vajra they are empty of intrinsic nature, and likewise because they are fearless. "The bell is the dhāraṇī of the Dharma" means this: because it is of the very nature of the Perfection of Wisdom, its nature too is thus; the bell, the counterpart to the vajra of method, is to be understood as wisdom, the female, as peace.

By the manner of the mudrā in union,
In her the method dissolves:

that is, all of these are of the very nature of mudrā. As it is also said in the Glorious Guhyasamāja:

If you desire the supreme accomplishment,
Do not form the mudrās of the hands.

With net, ritual dagger and fence,
One should dispel the māras and harm:

this means that just as outer foes, māras and the like are crushed by the mudrās and mantras of the net, the fence and so forth, so too those māras of conceptual thought and the rest that arise from one's own mind are to be abandoned by means of the fence of the perfection of generosity and the other perfections. Thus it is said in the Later Tantra of the Saṃvara of Equal Union:

The māras arise from one's own mind,
Because of avarice indulged before.

Wishing to teach just that, it is said, "With no māra, since it is not by māras," and so on.

"With the flowers of patience, the yogin," and so on, means that one should give, having filled up with the flowers of patience. What difference is there between the patience mentioned above and this one? The difference lies in the mind alone. It is the patience of bearing even being struck by weapons such as swords without thinking anything of it and meditating on loving-kindness, and likewise the patience of thinking nothing of harsh speech and the like that robs one. It is, for example, like loving-kindness and compassion.

If one asks how one should abide when focusing on emptiness in this way, it is said:

From the places of equipoise,
Up to the measure of one person.

The thought here is this: placing the eyes on the tip of the nose, one should contemplate with evenness at about one cubit's distance.

The vajras taught above,
And the twenty-eight deities:

this means that, setting aside the vase and the three deities at the upper, lower and central prongs, the retinue deities alone are said to be twenty-eight; counted all together, they are thirty-one.

If one asks with how many the bell is to be meditated on, it is said, "Meditating on the bell in the center, the twenty-three deities," and so on. There, on the petals of the lower lotus are the eight goddesses beginning with Tārā, and on it also the face of the Perfection of Wisdom; on the eight petals of the upper lotus are the eight bodhisattvas; on the five prongs the five families; and the handle, having the nature of wisdom and consciousness. The meaning is that they come to twenty-three.

At the tip of the vajra finger,
Having imagined the nectar letter:

the letter sa should be placed on the left finger. "Shown on the left side" means touching the body and so on. "It should be inserted before the goddess" means that it should be placed in the secret lotus.

"The eighteen places" are the breasts, the fingertips, the throat, the lower lip, the cheeks, the eyes, the roots of the ears, the crown, and the supreme limb, the secret place. If one touches the nine places twice, joining upward and downward in the manner of the digits of the moon, they become eighteen places. Thus it is said concerning the eights:

Breasts, fingertips and throat,
The lower lip, likewise the cheeks and eyes,
The ears, the crown and the supreme limb:
On all of these one drinks.
In the bright half, downward and upward;
In the dark moon it is explained as reversed.
At the root of the big toe, the letter ā;
Likewise on the two calves, ā;
On the two thighs, the letter ī;
The letter ī is placed at the secret place.
At the root of the navel, the letter ū;
Likewise in the gathered place, the letter ū;
At the root of the breasts, the letter ṛ;
The letter ṝ abides in the hands.
At the throat itself, the letter ḷ;
Likewise at the crown, the letter ḹ.
On the side of the cheeks, the letter e;
Likewise ai on the two eyes.
At the root of the ears, the letter o;
The letter au abides at the crown.
aṃ a on all the limbs of the body.
One should apply the rites:
Kissing, embracing and the like,
Biting and the acts of union.
Having examined the places, one applies them,
Contemplating each letter as a mark.
Merely by contemplating in this way
The woman delights, and not otherwise.
Even when man and woman join without the organs,
By mere caressing the fair-faced one delights.

"Where all nectars abide" means that the bodhicitta abides at the eighteen places. Clarifying the very meaning being explained, it is said:

Stirred by that finger,
The waters of nectar will flow.

It is said that immediately after arousing her in this way, one should make the great mudrā firm, enter into nondual absorption in union, and begin the act: "Having inserted, both embracing," and so on. There, "embracing" means the embrace that takes up the great transworldly compassion, so that those sunk in the mire of ignorance may attain the unsurpassed fruit. "One should abide facing east and so on" means that in nondual absorption the goddess should face east. The meaning is this: because the goddess who is to be accomplished becomes the principal one, and the god is not the principal one, it is therefore fitting that the goddess face east, and especially because this is a ḍākinī tantra. As it is said in what is called The Princess:

Because the second becomes principal,
She is to be relied on in that way.
Why is it so distinguished?
The supreme rite is being explained.

"The goddesses who extend in play" means the goddesses who abide in the playful cross-legged posture. "Vajra" means "Vajrasattva, the body that presses down." If one asks how he presses down, it is said, "foot to foot." The act they perform is what is called sitting. Moreover, the yogin's sitting comes to be imagined in two ways. As it is also said:

Placed outside the body,
The woman is to be filled with the feet.
To show the two kinds of sitting,
The male posture is for conceptual thought.

"The left bell, and the thighs too" means that the left hand of the Perfection of Wisdom goddess is to be placed beneath the root of the thigh; by this is meant the passion of great compassion. The placing of the right hand is not explained here, since it was connected above. Embrace alone, without method, has no engagement other than acting for the benefit of various sentient beings.

Making just that manifest, the teaching of the various is spoken: "Of the two hands said to hold the bell." It is said that one should not dwell in the conceptual thought of ordinary beings that has not yet arisen: "Both should recite the seed syllables," and so on.

The goddess who extends to the left
Will turn her face to the south:

this means that the goddess who abides with her left leg extended should be placed facing south.

"That vajra, again, by the noble one" means that Vajrasattva too should abide facing north.

"The two soles of the feet too should be pressed" means that with the goddess's two soles one should press Vajrasattva's two palms.

The vajra on the right at the breast,
Likewise with one at the secret place:

this refers to Vajrasattva.

With both hands of the bell,
Binding the legs, one should squeeze:

here "the bell" is the Perfection of Wisdom goddess. The meaning is that with her two hands she should press Vajrasattva's waist so that it is tightly bound.

The vajra on the right at the breast,
Likewise with one at the secret place:

this means that with his left hand Vajrasattva should touch the secret place, and with his right he should press the breast.

The mouths of bell and vajra
Should meet again and again:

here the bell is the wisdom woman. Kissing Vajrasattva's mouth with her mouth and so on means that one should kiss the nine places again and again, circling to the right.

"By reciting together with the two seeds," and so on, means that method and wisdom, each reciting their own seed, should liberate the sentient beings of the world who dwell in the southern direction.

"Feet even, the west," and so on: beginning with the act of the west, both set the right foot evenly in a stance, and each presses the other's left knee. As it is also said:

Drawing with the arms, at thighs and breasts,
At the meeting of the three in front, overpowering,
Joining the hands at the triangular place,
Mouth to mouth, in union, is mindfulness.

The meaning is this: winding the nape of each other's neck with the noose of the arms, she touches Vajrasattva's tongue by rolling the tongue of the bell, and, moving, each should recite their own seed.

Now the northern extension is taught. It is said how one should wind behind the neck: "In the center of the vajra's evenness," and so on. With his two arms Vajrasattva winds around the center of the goddess's even place, near the breasts, and with her two hands the bell also presses at the roots of Vajrasattva's ears; having joined, they should mark each other with the nails. As it is also said:

Embracing and striking,
Biting, and likewise scratching with the nails:
The upper is known to be fivefold,
And the lower fourfold.
In marking with the nails, on the left,
At the root of the navel and at the organ too,
At the supreme limb and the crown,
One touches like an elephant's trunk.

"With both lips fully bound," and so on, means that, passionately attached in joy, they should drink each other's lips. "All sentient beings," and so on, is clear in meaning.

"The unions of the deity of the bell," and so on, means that on the body of the Perfection of Wisdom goddess and on the body of Vajrasattva one should meditate on the eight letters of the mantra in the order stated above.

Immediately upon performing in this way the two suchnesses separately, it is done when the goddess and one's own body have become, in mind, the essential nature of the dharmadhātu, the nature of method, the nature free of conceptuality. Then again, the mind that comes after that is urged by uttering melodies and praises accompanied by conceptuality, and, for the sake of the cause of benefiting sentient beings, one should rise. Thus it is said in the Glorious Guhyasamāja Tantra: "Uttering, 'I am Vajrasattva,'" and so on. The meaning is that immediately upon rising, with a passionate mind, together with the wisdom woman, in the pride of Vajrasattva, one should utter oṃ vajrasatva haṃ.

The flowers are water-born ones,
By the character of the inner secret:
Sprout, root and flower,
The bandhūka, as if blazing:

this refers to what is called the ḍākinī water channel, which abides secretly in the center within, resembling a red lotus. The meaning is that it is the condition for sprouting into the sprout of name and form, and for stem, flower, fruit, root, seed and so on. As it is also said:

The twenty-four channels
Are the goddess channels of the great tantra;
There, at the ḍākinī water channel,
The wise one should stimulate.

In the two thongas, the seeds;
In the belly, too, one utters one.
Setting the letters at the mouth of the five channels,
One should always stimulate at the mouth.
Whatever moves at the sole of the foot,
The wise one, at the sole and the big toe as well,
At the crown, the meeting of the three, the two thighs,
And at the root of the hand, should touch.

If one asks how, when that inner secret lotus blooms, it becomes the seed, it is said, "In the two thongas, the seeds," and so on. The seed in the two thongas is the seed of the tigress. Because the plural word "seeds" is used, the meaning is that all four stacked letters are to be taken. Because the four beginning with sa and the eight letters have the very nature of method and wisdom, the meaning is that they should be known as the forms of going, of birth and of dwelling in saṃsāra. How the third group and the rest are arranged has already been explained.

"Having imagined the form of the goddess" means that one should generate the form of the wisdom ḍākinī produced from that very seed. "Of sa" means the letter hūṃ. "One should dissolve in the center" refers to all that belongs to the union of absorption.

Clarifying just this meaning, it is said, "The fire of time and the preta fire," and so on. There, "the fire of time" is the right hand, and "the preta fire" is the left hand. Embracing each other with both hands, at the place joined together with one hand one should set them in the manner of a trident: this is the form produced by the method of existence.

"With the mind drinking nectar," and so on, is clear in meaning. "At the places of the eighteen limbs," and so on, has already been explained above in terms of the digits of the moon. "It resembles the lip of the ḍākinī": here what is called the ḍākinī's lip is the channel of the secret lotus.

"One should stimulate that mudrā" means this: if the wisdom woman has become hard, then one should do whatever makes her melt, by such acts as the elephant's trunk. As it is also said:

The supreme limb is known as twofold:
Likewise it is supreme and hard.
Making the cause for what is hard,
Having done so, it is not without a son of good family.

"The lips of the right vajra," and so on, means that one should create the cause for the flowing of the vajra's water, in such a way that it comes at the same time. To arouse desire in that very mind, it is said:

At the thonga, or else at the places,
Then one should mark with the nails.

"Then the vajra tip, the mudrā," and so on, clarifies the very meaning that has already been explained.

"With the playful feet of the vajra," and so on: by extending toward each other in play, the act of arranging various arrangements of pleasant seats, and by the shapes and so on, the secret play of the yogin and the yoginī is to be explained.

"In the manner of the play of the bell," and so on, means that the Perfection of Wisdom goddess abides on the thighs of Vajrasattva, who sits in the sattva cross-legged posture, and she should wind the noose of her thighs around his body.

"Upon the essenceless, empty saṃsāra," and so on: there, "essence" is what is called the vow of the net of ḍākinīs. "Assembly" is the nature of union equal with all the buddhas. Therefore all the buddhas attained that supreme bliss of the net of ḍākinīs. If one asks from what, it is said in the preceding words: "upon the essenceless, empty saṃsāra." If one asks what it is like, it is said:

Taking an example from the world:
The plantain tree has no essence,
Yet its fruit has essence,
And in the eating is like nectar.

The meaning being spoken is this: the acts of the methods of play taught above and the rest are without essence; yet, just as a sugarcane field filled with accumulated heaps of manure, they will produce the fruit that has the character of nirvāṇa, which is like nectar. It is well known from scripture as well, as follows: "O Kāśyapa, just as heaps of manure become beneficial to the sugarcane plant and the like, in just that way, O Kāśyapa, the passionate too come to be of benefit to omniscience."

"Thing" means conceptual thought. "Liberation" means the complete abandonment of manifestation. There, "accomplishment" is the accomplishment of liberation. As the noble Āryadeva also said, "Liberation, because all conceptual thoughts are abandoned."

"Having made the union of embracing each other" means the meeting of method and wisdom. If one asks why, it is said, "By that, the aim of all will be accomplished," meaning that every realm of sentient beings without exception is to be liberated from saṃsāra. By that, in the vow of the yoginī's wisdom, for others saṃsāra is without essence.

Distinctions of medicines and so on,
And it should be endowed with extending:

this means making the stances and making the distinctions of medicines. Because the word "distinction" is taken up, the Kakṣapuṭa compound taught in the Twelve Thousand is first given in condensed words. There, the Kakṣapuṭa compound is as follows:

Victor, together with, uṣṇīṣa, jāri, hand tears, and girl,
Matted hair, man, and joy, fear, delusion, victor and vajra:
Making a quarter into four, and mixing again with the body of the five,
For the thirty-four that bring husband and wife under control.

With this and so on, one should write out the Kakṣapuṭa compound to its end. "Saffron and" is clear. "At the place of compounding" means compounding in equal parts. "Indra's hand" means in the manner of a root with five fingers. "Of the five selves" means the impurities of the five sense faculties, the eyes and the rest. "Five limbs," and so on: the meaning to be understood, the distinction of each goddess and the distinction of the acts, is easy to grasp.

"Further, the methods of extending," and so on, teaches the eighteen extensions: one should extend toward and, having struck them, expel the obstructors that dwell in all the directions and intermediate directions. These are outer and inner: by the outer extensions one should expel the outer obstructors, and by the inner extensions one should expel the inner māras.

"By extending, having joined the two" means union, because that alone is what is called the ultimate mudrā. Therefore, "One should bind that very mudrā": its characteristic has already been explained above.

"The even method" is the even method because the feet are joined to each other with the big toes side by side. "The dividing method" is the dividing method when one of those very feet is bent. In the round method, the toes of the feet should face outward beyond two cubits, and the two knees, raised up, are bent down, in the manner of a goose's wings: this is the round method. "The stirakrama method" means placing the right foot on the ground and raising the left foot with a swagger, in the manner of striding over the three worlds: this is the stirakrama method. "The tortoise method" means pressing the top of the left foot with the sole of the right: this is the tortoise method. "Right extended" means bending the left calf and extending the right to a length of five spans: this is the right extended method. "And left extended" means bending the right and extending the left in just the same way: this is left extended. "And abiding as one" means raising one foot and turning to the right on the other: this is abiding as one. "Sitting" means extending in play and so on, and then abiding facing a direction in that way. "And the extending of the feet" is the extending of the feet because the feet are extended in various ways.

If it is true that all are included in the opened and the rest, then even the union of placing the thighs in reverse breaks off at that very instant, as stated above; it should be made absent by both.

"And the manner of the lamp stand" means that the yoginī's two legs are drawn together and then set apart: this is the manner of the lamp stand.

As it is also said:

Placing them bent as one:
The woman's knees are stirred,
And joined with the seated man,
This is well known as "holding the feet."

"And the abiding of the knees" means pressing the two knees side by side against each other at the left flank: this is the abiding of the knees. "And well assembled" means that Vajrasattva's arms firmly embrace the Perfection of Wisdom goddess from the neck, and, setting the goddess's calves on top, they join in union: this is well assembled. As it is also said:

The woman tightly embraces the neck,
And with both the man's arms
Placed on the two elbows,
This is called assembling at the woman's calves.

"And abiding evenly" means that both abide. "And the heart of the fire of time" means the heart of the fire of time, which performs what is called traversing the thonga, the goddess placing the sole of her right foot at Vajrasattva's heart and so on. As it is also said:

The woman places the sole of her foot
At the man's heart, and moreover,
By both traversing the thonga,
It is sitting with the leg extended.

"Burnt extended" is clear in meaning. "And the bad place" means that sitting after moving is the conduct of a barbarian borderland: it is the bad place. As it is also said:

There, the thigh of the one seated is placed:
At the woman's calves it is called the borderland.
The woman's foot outside
Is held to be in the manner of the central extension.

"Assembled as one" means that, in that very well-gathered posture spoken of, one foot is assembled, so it is gathering into one foot. As it is also said:

Joined with the seated male,
In order for its trident to arise,
Joining in reverse completely,
The spread-out ones truly arise.

"By the eighteen extensions, one should join at the time of offering" means one should join them in order in the outer and inner offerings. "By the union of all good qualities" means one should offer the five enjoyments and so on, as one's resources allow. "And near the heart," and so on, is the action of the arms and is easy to understand.

"The vajra, the bell and the mudrās," and so on, has already been explained in detail above. Here it is explained in brief. "Vajra" is the appropriating condition. "Bell" is the cooperating condition. Through the union of these two there arises what is called the sprout of name and form, the union of the great mudrā that acts for the benefit of sentient beings. It is said that without just this it is not accomplished: "In the night without a moon," and so on.

"If one meditates first on āli and kāli" means one meditates in the sequence taught above. "Those who have gone to method and wisdom will abide having crossed saṃsāra": if one asks what the meaning is, it signifies that fools, śrāvakas and the like do not cross over. As it is also said in the Glorious Saṃvara Tantra:

All śrāvakas are of ill fortune,
Because they pass utterly into nirvāṇa.

And concerning the outer tīrthikas it is also said:

All tīrthikas are of ill fortune,
Because they cling to "I" and "mine."

Therefore it is said:

For the mind pure like crystal,
Its Dharma should be taught.

That is, by teaching them too the profound Dharma, their doubts are removed and they are brought to equality. Therefore one should teach this suchness, the pledge of all the tathāgatas.

To act for the benefit of sentient beings outwardly,
One earnestly focuses on suchness:

this means that by meditating on the absence of intrinsic nature one makes one's own mind equal, and one should perform the approach through the yoga of the deity of compassion as taught. If one asks what to do if one is unable to focus on suchness, it is said:

The inner seed abiding in the center,
One should take up the seed with āli.

There, the inner secret seed is hūṃ. "Palms joined from all directions" means that one should praise, with praises, offerings and the like, the net of yogins and yoginīs who abide in the ten directions, and then make aspiration prayers. "Oh, the supreme path of Dharma," and so on, is easy to understand in meaning. "The letter doubled, with the drop," and so on, has already been explained above, and so it is not commented on here.

This completes the explanation of the Seat of Union, from the examination of the Glorious Catuṣpīṭha, composed by the vajra master, the glorious Kalyāṇavarman.

These three seats were composed by the practitioner Kalyāṇavarman, with compassion for sentient beings in mind. It is also said that this seat, among the four sections, was not permitted by the ḍākinīs. It is also said that, right where he composed the commentary, he met the ḍākinīs face to face, attained accomplishment and vanished from sight. Later the Indian preceptor Smṛtijñānakīrti composed this commentary on the last seat of the Catuṣpīṭha, just as he had heard it in the lineage of the masters.

Then the Blessed One Vajrasattva, born of great compassion, urged Vajrapāṇi: "Listen, Vajra, to how the path is," and so on; with this the first chapter of the Secret Seat begins.

"What are the qualities of the master," and so on, is what Vajrapāṇi asked in return. There, "what are the qualities of the master" asks what the qualities of the major and minor limbs of the vajra master are like. "And how the master is joined" asks with which qualities one who is to be a master must be endowed. "How is it that one is called guru?" asks what the evil of disparaging the vajra master and the merit of praising him are like.

Then the Blessed One Vajrasattva, wishing to teach the qualities of the limbs of the vajra master, said, "Vajrapāṇi, listen to just this," and so on. There, "all the characteristics of perfect awakening" means that the actual body of the vajra master has the characteristics of a perfectly complete buddha. "The limbs are bodhisattvas" signifies that the limbs of the vajra master have the nature of bodhisattvas. "On the crown of the head, the five families" means that the head of the vajra master is adorned by the five families, Vairocana and the rest.

"The worldly ones are the seat of the feet" means that the seat beneath the soles of the vajra master's feet is the very substance of the worldly gods, the Great God and the rest. "Light rays, yakṣas, at the secret place, and so on" means that the secret region of the vajra master has the nature of yakṣas and the like. "The signs to be known by one who has wisdom" means that the disciple who possesses wisdom should recognize such qualities of the vajra master.

Now, wishing to explain the answer to "And how the master is to apply himself," he said, "With a disciplined and peaceful demeanor," and so on. There, "disciplined" is with regard to body and speech. "A peaceful demeanor" means without a wrathful manner. "With compassion, a beneficial mind" means that one should free sentient beings from suffering. "With a beneficial mind" refers to the wisdom that thinks, "In the end I shall free all sentient beings from all suffering." "If one fully knows the ten realities" refers to these ten: the reality of the vajra, the reality of the bell, the reality of primordial wisdom, the reality of the deity, the reality of the maṇḍala, the reality of the fire offering, the reality of mantra, the reality of the colored powders, the reality of the torma, and the reality of empowerment.

Now, in answer to "How is it that one is called guru?", he spoke to teach the meaning of honoring the guru: "Listen further. The real," and so on. There, "the acts performed by the disciple" are the things a disciple must not do toward the master. "Secret" is the union of method and wisdom. "The way of samaya" is the vow of samaya. "The yoga torma" is the torma of the ḍākinīs. "The secret and so on" are offerings such as the five nectars and the five hooks. "He will swiftly fall into Avīci" means that a disciple who disparages these will be born in Avīci, the hot hell of unceasing torment. "Bed, shoes and seats" means that one who steps on the master's bed and the like, and one who does not heed the master's words, will in this life be without wealth and without glory, and will be tormented by māras and obstructors. "If one steps over his shadow, it is like having destroyed a stūpa" means that stepping over the vajra master's shadow is the destruction of a stūpa, an act close to the five acts of immediate retribution, and so one will be born in the Avīci hell; for acts of immediate retribution lead to birth in the Avīci hell alone. Therefore, saying that one should not do this, he said, "Those who revile the vajra master," and so on.

If one asks why he must not be reviled, he said, "Because mantra, mudrā, union and the rest are accomplished by his path." That is, because through the master's instruction one obtains the mudrās, mantras and so on that become the cause of awakening.

Now, concerning accepting the disciple: "First give the trainings, then accept him" means that, having taught the empowerments, as conveyed by the words "maṇḍala and so on," and the tormas, one should accept the disciple. Wishing to explain the very distinctions of empowerment, he said, "Above, by a single syllable, the five buddhas are to be shown." There, the syllable of the five buddhas is hūṃ, for it is said, "The staff is Lokeśvara." The meaning is this: first one should confer empowerment on the disciple by showing him the single syllable, the heart syllable hūṃ.

"One who possesses the mantra empowerment should meditate on the eight syllables." There, the eight syllables are suṃ kṣuṃ yuṃ hūṃ smryuṃ hmryuṃ ymryuṃ kṣmryuṃ. The meaning is this: having set out the eight syllables for the disciple, immediately after empowering him with mantra one should meditate on the eight syllables on the eight limbs, the crown of the head and the rest, and show them to him.

"The nondual torma empowerment: its Dharma too will be known" means that, having taught the disciple the Dharma in which the entity and the non-entity of the nature of reality are not two, one confers the nondual empowerment.

"One is to be endowed with the empowerment of union; it is the teaching of primordial wisdom and consciousness." By "The vajra master empowerment," and so on: there, primordial wisdom is method, the god; consciousness is wisdom, the goddess. Having taught the meaning of their very union, one confers the empowerment of union. As it is also said:

The equipoise of method and wisdom
Is what is known as union.

There, the secrets are five: killing, taking what is not given, sexual misconduct, lying, and not abandoning afflictions such as attachment. There, killing is not shrinking from the afflictions. Taking what is not given is taking the sacred substances that are not given to the śrāvakas and the like. Sexual misconduct is, for the sake of offering to the Tathāgatas, meditating on oneself as the Tathāgata and so forth and then engaging in inverted attachment and the like. Lying is this: since the true meaning cannot be expressed, reciting mantras is lying. Not abandoning afflictions such as attachment is possessing genuine attachment and the like. The words "and so on" refer to the maṇḍala, the fire offering, offerings, tormas and the like. The meaning is this: teaching the disciple all of the secrets and so forth becomes the vajra master empowerment.

He said, "First, having taken up the bases of training." If one asks how that is done, he said, "First of all, the disciples should make supplication for the sake of the trainings." That is, the disciples should make their request to the master with whatever brings joy to a disciple. If one asks how the request is to be made, he said, "Arranging the upper robe over one shoulder," and so on. If one asks how the master is to give the trainings, he said, "The guru, seated on a cushion," and so on. This means that the master, seated cross-legged on the seat, takes with his right hand the joined palms of the disciple, who kneels with his knee on the ground, and gives him the trainings.

If one asks what words are spoken in request by the disciple who kneels before the master with his knee on the ground and his palms joined, he said: beginning with "The Buddha, the Dharma and the Saṅgha," and so on, up to "oṃ yoga śuddhaḥ sarva dharmāḥ yoga śuddho'haṃ." There, "I shall ferry across those who have not crossed" refers to sentient beings who have fallen into the ocean of existence and, lacking true awareness, have not been ferried across, namely the three lower realms. The point is that, teaching them the meaning of the true Dharma, one should, like Avalokiteśvara, go to the lower realms such as the hells, teach the Dharma, endow them with true awareness, and draw them out of the ocean of existence. "I shall free those who are not freed": even when they have come up out of the lower realms, those who, through grasping at self and mine, are not freed from the bonds of attachment to existence and the like are gods and humans. The meaning is that one should teach them too the meaning of the selflessness of all phenomena, free them from the bond of attachment, the bond of aversion and the bond of delusion, and bring them to nirvāṇa. "Thus, out of loving kindness" means that, for the sake of awakening, one should not harm the three lower realms and the two higher realms thus fallen into the ocean of existence, in the sense of neither killing them nor having them killed, but should cherish them with love and tender concern.

Wishing to show the master, seated on his seat, giving his reply to those disciples, he said: from "The three kinds of the body," and so on, up to "teach the Dharma as it was spoken." There, "the three kinds of the body" are killing, taking what is not given, and sexual misconduct. "The four kinds of conduct of speech" are lying, slander, harsh words and idle chatter. "And of the mind there are three kinds" are covetousness, malice and wrong view. "By being well restrained, guard the body" means, "You should keep your aggregates restrained from the ten non-virtues." "With a mind of the wisdom of equality, teach the Dharma as it was spoken" means that, once the disciple has given rise to a mind in which there is no self and other, one should teach him the meaning of the nonconceptual Perfection of Wisdom.

If one asks how the trainings are to be given after the Dharma has been taught in this way, he said, "Then, covering his eyes with a cloth," and so on. There, "at the place of the torma" is the ground between the outer and inner gates. He also gave the words of homage that the disciple addresses to the master before the trainings are given: "The actual body of all the buddhas," and so on.

Then, wishing to convey the master's aspiration for the disciple, he said:

May the disciple's understanding, through the lion who proclaims the ultimate,
Destroy the ground that breeds the faults of afflicted existence,
And, to cut off the stirrings of affliction through the rite of yoga,
Accomplish that very king, the ultimate of yoga.

That is: having expressed the knowable dharmas in words of ultimate truth, and being free of conceptual thought like a lion, may the rite of yoga, and that very king, the ultimate of yoga, be accomplished for the disciple, in order to cut off the afflictions that are the cause of existence.

After the aspiration has been made in this way, he spoke of placing syllables on the disciple's body, speech and mind: "a is the supreme of all syllables," and so on. There, "blessed as the primordial wisdom vajra" means that, immediately after the disciple's body, speech and mind have been blessed with oṃ āḥ hūṃ, one should place on his mind, between the breasts, the seed syllable of his yidam deity, in order to seal him with the four mudrās. "One should also give the samaya water endowed with the five nectars" means that, immediately after the syllables have been placed, one should give the luminous samaya nectar, endowed with the five nectars and the five hooks, pouring it into his mouth. If one asks what the nectar is like, he said, "By ha its color is taken away," and so on; the meaning has already been explained.

He said that immediately after the samaya has been conferred, the face covering is removed and the disciple prostrates at full length: "As soon as his eyes are freed and he sees, he should bow to all with the five limbs," and so on. There, "one should offer to the Seat of Union" means that, holding a garland of flowers in his hand, he should cast it onto the maṇḍala and bow.

He explained the meaning of this: "Then, what is to be examined: how the signs are to be shown" means that the master should take as a sign the direction in which the torma flower garland fell on the maṇḍala above. Teaching that whichever deity of the maṇḍala the flower falls upon is the one to be given to the disciple as his yidam deity, he said, "As one endowed with a body, the wise one should give." There, "endowed with a body" means that one should join the disciple's body with the deity marked by the flower. "The wise one" is the master. "Then the trainings and so on are done again" means that, after the examination of the disciple who has already been given the trainings above, one should again give the trainings and the samaya as well. If one asks how they are to be given, he said, "As taught, examined by the mind." This means that the samaya, which does not appear in the words of the trainings that were taught, and everything contrary to it, the master himself should examine with his mind, and then he should tell it to the disciple.

Again, before the trainings are given, he gave the words with which the disciple offers his own body to the ḍākinīs and to the master: from the beginning, "At the feet of the guru," and so on, up to "oṃ sakala satva paritranāyā ātmanaṃ niryatāyāmi." If one asks what the words of training are in "the trainings and so on are to be given above," they begin with "Be without pride and anger," and so on, up to "a a oṃ āḥ hūṃ jaḥ." There, "the outer treatises" are worldly treatises, the treatises of method and wisdom, and the treatises of Dharma.

Having thus given the trainings and the samaya, he spoke of placing the seed syllables on the disciple after the empowerment has been conferred: "Endowed with the vajra mudrā and the rest, one places the seed syllables on the six limbs." There, "the vajra mudrā" is the five-pronged vajra.

As it is also said in the Nopika:

Joining the wisdom finger with the lotus,
One joins the parasol of earth with consciousness;
Binding both together with a thread,
The others are raised upright.
Smoke, having cast off the three stains,
Is the holder of the five-pronged vajra and the rest.

"One places the seed syllables on the six limbs": there, the six limbs are the crown of the head, the eyes, the ears, the nose, the tongue and the heart. The seed syllables are kṣuṃ, hūṃ, yuṃ, suṃ and hūṃ. The meaning is this: the vajra master, having formed the five-pronged mudrā, touches the disciple's crown and the other places and, pronouncing the syllables kṣuṃ and the rest, should set them there. The five syllables are made into six because the one syllable yuṃ alone is placed on both the ears and the nose, and so there are six.

"Moreover, for all the dharmas, one teaches the stages of perfect awakening" means that, beyond the conferral of empowerment and the placing of the syllables, one should teach the Dharma of scripture and pith instruction, and also the reality of the deity, the reality of the maṇḍala and the rest, which are the causes of perfect awakening.

He spoke of offering the fee immediately after the empowerment has been conferred: from "Then always, to the master," up to "should be offered as the guru's fee."

By the words above, "one teaches the stages of perfect awakening," whatever the stages of perfect awakening are, he spoke of them in terms of the accumulation of wisdom:

The nature of dharmas is pure; that nature is without essence.
Nor do things have intrinsic nature: so one teaches the stages of perfect awakening.

There, "that nature is without essence" means that, because the nature of reality is by nature emptiness, it does not become a thing, since it transcends thought. "Nor do they have essence or intrinsic nature" means that the very essence of the dharmas that are taken as objects has the nature of emptiness, because they arise in dependence. Therefore essence and nature are taught to be inseparable, because dependently related dharmas and emptiness are not different. Accordingly, Nāgārjunapāda said in the Praise of the Perfection of Wisdom:

Emptiness is not other than essence,
Nor does emptiness have an essence.
Therefore dependent relation is emptiness:
This, O divine one, is what you have taught.

Again and again, he further spoke of the stages of perfect awakening in terms of the accumulation of merit: "First, by way of patience," and so on. There, "the all-knowing disciple" means that the disciple to whom the outer and inner meaning has been taught is called all-knowing. "Having given rise to the wisdom of equality, one should regard all things equally" means that, having given rise to the mind toward things and non-things, one regards all sentient beings equally. "Always practicing equality, one strives in the supreme concentration of union" means that one should cultivate the samādhi that joins existence and nonexistence as its object.

He gave an example of exactly this:

The ground of sentient beings' craving for giving,
Like trade, increases its fruit;
And by that sprout, the tree,
The path of beings is blocked.

The meaning is that if a yogin performs giving and the rest in a manner that takes objects as its basis, it increases like trade and becomes a fruit in a later life. Since that fruit is a thing of existence, it blocks the path by which one goes to nirvāṇa, like a tree.

If one asks what the reality of the fruit is, he said, "For the sake of wanting the enjoyments of the higher realms," and so on. The meaning is that, through merit of the kind that takes objects as its basis, one is born in the higher realms as a god or a human, and enjoying that is a fall into existence. Wishing to teach that therefore a yogin who has meditated on the nondual meaning should give up all object-based actions, such as circumambulating stūpas, he said, "One should not do the work of stūpas," and so on. If one asks why, he said, "Treatises are easy to find, but the scripture of this is hard to hear," and so on. The meaning is that the outer treatises are easy to hear and to understand, but the nondual meaning, which has become pith instruction, is hard to find anywhere other than from the master's own mouth; therefore one should meditate on that alone.

"The deity that is oneself and the other deity: the deity that is oneself is supreme" means that, between the other deity, such as an image, and the deity that is the wisdom being meditated on in one's own heart, the latter is chief; therefore meditation alone is praised. For that reason the Blessed One himself said, "The mind is the measure of the body." If one asks what, then, outer images, stūpas and the like are for, he said, "Because one does not know oneself as the form, one should examine images and so on."

From the Vision of the Glorious Catuṣpīṭha composed by the paṇḍita Smṛtijñānakīrti, the explanation of the first chapter of the Secret Seat is complete.

Now, wishing to show the meaning of the words, "The master should teach from scripture," the Blessed One Vajrasattva himself began the second chapter of the Secret Seat, accomplishment through the mind, with "There, wealth is hard to find," and so on. There, "Mantra practitioners who act for their own aims wander through craving, without suchness" means that yogins who practice giving and the rest with their own aim as their object, and who do not know reality, the very teaching of the nondual meaning, wander in existence even if they possess realization.

Then Vajrapāṇi, having come to doubt the meaning of the Tathāgata's words, asked, "The wondrous pure body," and so on. There, "the wondrous pure body" means that it is a wonder that, within the nondual nature of emptiness, aggregates appear. Explaining just this, he said, "These are taught as the nature of reality." That is, from the nondual nature of reality, emptiness, these aggregates, elements and sense sources are shown. He asked further what is the valid measure for such a body as that: "By what is it joined as a valid measure," and so on.

To this he gave an answer by way of summarizing the meaning: "Listen, how the vajra is named," and so on. There, "The mind is the measure of the body" means that the measure of the five aggregates of the sentient beings of the three realms is the mind. Answering the question "By what are the measures known," and so on, he said, "By nectar in my own elements, the acts of torma are taught." This means that satisfying the deities of one's own body by the rite of nectar that comes below is the measure of the torma. Answering the question "Water and fire," and so on, he said, "The maṇḍala, fire and water." That is, he said that the four great elements, water and the rest, which have become the measure of the body, are the maṇḍala. This is the measure of mantra and mudrā.

Answering the question "Beings in this," and so on, he said, "The yogin who attains the fruit will fall into the ocean of existence." That is, if one meditates relying on some object, it becomes for the yogin a fruit of existence; by that, the path that goes to nirvāṇa is blocked and he circles in existence, because he meditates in a manner that takes objects as its basis. As Nāgārjunapāda also said in the Yogācāra Madhyamaka:

Those who do not know emptiness
Have no share in liberation;
Deluded, they circle round and round
In the six destinies of cyclic existence.

Having come to doubt the meaning of the Blessed One Vajrasattva's teaching that the mind is the measure of the body, Vajrapāṇi asked again: "Vajrapāṇi again requested," and so on. Wishing to teach the answer to the question in detail, he said, "Listen to that question about saṃsāra," and so on. There, "the characteristic of mind is to be tamed" means that, within saṃsāra, it is only that mind which has become the measure of the body that is to be tamed and passes into nirvāṇa, and nothing else.

Wishing to show how that very mind appears distinctly in the three realms, he said, "At will, the formless wisdom, like the wind moving through the sky." This means that in the formless realm the mind, having become the four aggregates, appears at will like the wind in space. "Form, and form by the mind as well, are accomplished by focusing on samādhi": there, "form" is the form realm. "And form by the mind as well" means that mind becomes the seventeen form realms, the Brahmakāyika heaven and the rest. "Are accomplished by focusing on samādhi" refers to the four dhyānas, focused on the four: conception, analysis, joy and bliss. The meaning is this: the form realm, having become the five aggregates in the four dhyānas, appears as the seventeen forms.

"The desirous, the gods of desire and the rest, always engage in desire" means that in the desire realm that mind becomes the five aggregates in the six classes of desire gods, the Four Great Kings and the rest, and appears always enjoying through attachment. "Thus the people of saṃsāra accomplish the grounds at will": in that same desire realm, the mind becomes the five aggregates in the eight hells, among the hungry ghosts, the animals and the four continents, and appears circling there. There, too, when one becomes human, one should tame the mind and accomplish the desired ground, nirvāṇa. Therefore, since mind is chief in the three realms, it is said that the mind is the measure of the body. And it is said in a sūtra as well: "O child of the Victors, the three realms are mind only."

Now, wishing to teach in brief the means of taming the mind, he said, "The thing that frees from that," and so on. There, "the thing that frees from that" means, "Listen to the cause by which they are freed from the three realms and pass into nirvāṇa." If one asks what the cause is, he said, "The yogin who accomplishes the going should know consciousness as coemergent." The point is that the yogin who accomplishes the path of nirvāṇa should know method and wisdom, and the characteristic of primordial wisdom and consciousness alone, coemergent and one in nature; this is to be understood by Vajrapāṇi.

He asked about that very point again and again: "If there is doubt about the essence of mind," and so on. Giving the answer to exactly that, he said, "The mind, like crystal," and so on. There, "the mind, like crystal" means that, because the afflictions are adventitious, the mind is pure like crystal, since its nature is luminosity. Therefore, "In the mind there is neither fault nor quality." There, the fault is affliction; the quality is the wisdom of the two paths, its antidote. The point is that ultimately neither of these exists.

Teaching that such a mind, through apprehended and apprehender, becomes dependent and comes to be afflicted with existence, he said, "Through the joining that enters into objects, it is stained by the faults of saṃsāra." There, "the joining" is the six faculties, the eye and the rest. "Into objects" is the six domains of experience, form and the rest. "That enters" is the six engaging consciousnesses. Through the power of that apprehended and apprehender, the mind is stained by the faults of the afflictions of existence.

He said that by abandoning what has become the cause of existence one should rely on what has become the cause of nirvāṇa: "Having abandoned dwelling on objects, one should join it to the abode as it is." That is, having abandoned the inverted domains of experience that are the things of existence, one should meditate, as the goddess, on the true abode, the reality of nirvāṇa, in a manner that does not take it as an object. As it is also said in the Verses on Affliction and Purification:

The nature of this dependent
Has two portions in its character:
By its imagined portion it is saṃsāra;
By its perfected portion, nirvāṇa.

As an example of this he said, "As a son or a disciple is trained." That is, just as a son or a disciple, who is not yet inclined toward either fault or quality, is trained in qualities alone while still a child and made to possess true awareness, so the mind, luminous by nature, is to be brought to realize the true meaning and made to pass into nirvāṇa.

"What is called its middle: consciousness should be examined like space" means that this mind, being examined as the middle between existence and nonexistence, consciousness too always appears like space, existing conventionally but not withstanding analysis.

If one asks what its characteristic is like, he said, "Colorless, yet the actual substance of all colors." There, "colorless" means that, because that mind transcends the faculties, it is not a thing such as black or yellow, for all things arise through mere dependent relation. "The actual substance of all colors" means that, since the essence of all colors also arises from mind, it is the actual substance of all colors, because they do not exist as outer substances. "Without connection" is because that mind is unobstructed. "Connected form" means that the mind also becomes form complete with major and minor limbs, or vases, cloths and the like. "Formless" is because it is the nature of mind and mental factors. "And all that has form" is because it is the essential nature of form, sound, smell and the rest. "Not far" is because the nature of reality is without measure. "And likewise far" is because the nature of reality is by nature endless. If one asks whether, through the reality of mind, magical display without substance is accomplished: the meaning is that, through the reality of the mind that is in the middle between existence and nonexistence, one should accomplish what appears as actual magical display yet is by nature without substance. "And by the mind without characteristics" is because the general characteristic is emptiness. "All characteristics are to be examined" is because the specific characteristic of each nature is the essence of vases, cloths and the like.

Now, if one asks how such a nature is to be meditated on by the mind, he said, "Through the substance of meditating on the insubstantial, that meditation is to be examined." This means that in terms of ultimate truth it does not exist, but in terms of conventional truth one should examine it in meditation. If one asks how it is to be examined, he said, "In the middle between form and emptiness, one should be skilled in the mind." This means that the skilled yogin should examine the mind that is in the middle, between existence and nonexistence, between these two: perceiving as existent form and the rest, which arise from dependent origination, and the nonexistence, as emptiness, of the dharmas that are by nature conditioned.

Now he spoke of the qualities of the yogin who has such an object: "By focusing on everything entirely, the yogin's body becomes free of illness." That is, if the yogin meditates in this way, he does not fall into the fault of the annihilation of existence or the fault of substantiality.

Again, wishing to declare the qualities of mind, he said, "The secret of secrets," and so on. There, it is "the secret of secrets" because it cannot be expressed. It is "the great secret" because it transcends the domain of experience. "Consciousness and primordial wisdom are clear" is because it transcends characteristics and signs. He said that the mind of such reality is obtained only through the guru's instruction: "From the secret of reading the treatises," and so on.

Having thus explained in detail the measure of mind, and wishing to teach in detail the measure of mudrā, he said, "The body together with the mind of equality." There, "Place the two hands above the secret place" means that the yogin, seated in the sattva cross-legged posture, should form the mudrā of samādhi. "The breath is near the navel" means that one meditates on the vase samādhi. "One should join them in the manner of a maṇḍala" means that, without bending forward or leaning back, one should extend the arms and hold them in a circle. The rest has already been explained in the fourth chapter, on the Seat of Union, so it is not connected here.

Now, wishing to teach in detail the torma of measure as a continuation of that very meditation, he said, "The body satisfied by the nectar torma," and so on. There, "yellow in color," and so on, is the placing of the syllables before one is satisfied by the nectar. "The syllable in which ka and ṣa are doubled" is kṣuṃ, yellow, the nature of fire, because ka and la are stacked. "All forms are the Terrifier; the bell syllable is at the eyes" is the syllable hūṃ, black, the nature of the earth element; since it is said here, "The body is the Gentle One and the supreme bell," Mañjuśrī and the bell are the nature of both, for they are to be known from the syllable hūṃ. "Possessing the seed of the empty" means that the empty places are the openings of the ears and nose, and what is set there is yuṃ, red, the nature of wind. "The seed of nectar water" is the syllable suṃ, white, the nature of water. "By the stacked seed of the lion" is smryuṃ, white and yellow. "By the stacked seed of the tigress" is hmryuṃ, white and black. "By the stacked seed of the jackal" is ymryuṃ, red and black. "Protected by the seed of the cattle" is kṣmryuṃ, red and yellow. "Having taken up the seeds, one should also arrange the deities" means that, by these eight seed syllables, kṣuṃ and the rest, one should meditate on the eight, the fierce ḍākinī and the others, at the eight places of the yogin, the crown of the head and the rest. "Their colors and seed syllables are all blazing on the body" means that one meditates on these seed syllables blazing at the eight places of the yogin's body.

Then he spoke of the rite by which the yogin who has thus meditated on the deities brings down the nectar: "From the two upper fangs," and so on. There, "the lip of like taste that gives water" is the channel called the milk-water channel, which, on the inner side of the upper teeth and fangs, reaches to the root of the tongue and always drips water. If one asks what the water that drips there is like:

Resembling the color of salt water,
The water is the water of the two fangs.

If one asks from where the water descends, he said:

The water too, supported by the tongue itself,
Is the nectar above, the tongue;
It arises above.

If one asks which are the channels through which the nectar descends: "the forehead" is the channel of the eyes. "The crown of the head" is the channel of Brahmā, the channel of the crown. "And the nostrils" is the channel of the nose. "And from behind the two ears" is the channel of the ears. The meaning is that these four, together with the fang channel above, making five, gather, and the nectar that descends arises near the tongue. If one asks what this is like as an example, he said:

As it is held in a cow's udder,
Drawing out its essence, one should make it abundant.

That is, just as the milk that pervades a cow's body gathers in the udder, so one should draw it out from the substance of the five channels and set it right at the tongue alone.

If one asks what is to be done with that collected nectar itself, he said:

The seed at the head of the stūpa,

and so on. That is, having imagined a stūpa inside the channel knot, one places the syllable at its tip. Or else, the shape of the syllable hūṃ should be understood as the stūpa, and the nectar gathered from above should be poured onto the syllable hūṃ. If one asks where what drips down from that stūpa dissolves, he said:

The remainder should be dissolved into the upper channel.

Having thus shown the meaning of drawing the nectar from above, he then taught the rite of drawing it again from below:

By the seed that draws upward and draws downward,
Having drawn it all by skillful means,

That is, the seed that draws upward and draws downward is the syllable phaṭ; having drawn the nectar by skillful means from the lower body, one should dissolve it into the upper channel. He taught the nine places from which it is drawn in the lower part:

The urinary passage, the junction of the waters at the back,

and so on. Here,

In the feet, the time fire and the fire of the hungry ghosts,

means this: the time fire is the right foot, and the fire of the hungry ghosts is the left foot. "The joint of the place" is that of the navel.

The nectars of that place
Should be dissolved into the cow's hoof.

Here, the cow's hoof is the lungs, because they resemble a cow's hoof. The meaning is this: having drawn that nectar from the upper channel, one should dissolve it again into the lungs. If one asks where that nectar gathered in the upper channel should be spread and set, he said:

Having drawn it from a single door,
One should set it in the nine doors.

That is, the nectar drawn from the single door, the upper channel, and gathered in the lungs, should in turn be set in the nine places, beginning with the base of the spine.

If one asks what meditative absorption the yogin who thus draws the nectar by skillful means should cultivate, he said:

Of all that abides in the body,

and so on. Wishing to show clearly that very training in meditative absorption, he said:

In the center of the heart, imagine a moon.

Here, "in the center of the heart" means that one should meditate on a moon disk in the center of one's own heart. If one asks what it is like, he said:

Exceedingly beautiful and radiant.

He taught the seed set in its center:

Like snow, the kunda flower, and the moon,

and so on. Here, "the All-Knowing" is Vairocana. "Together with that" means the syllable oṃ, because it is the seed of Vairocana. That oṃ is called the syllable of nectar. So too it is said in the Nopika of the Guhyasamāja, the Illumination of the World:

At the crown, the moon, moist, with homage;
With a holy mind, the stream of water
Is made to descend in sequence,
And dissolves into body, speech and mind.

The meaning is this: in the center of the moon disk moistened with milk, one should set the syllable oṃ, white like snow and the kunda flower, wet with milk and possessed of cool light.

By the passage beginning

Having set aside the neuters,

the line "having set aside the neuters" means that of the sixteen vowels, the four ṛ ṝ and ḷ ḹ are to be set aside. "All the letters of the vowels" means that once four are set aside from the sixteen, twelve remain: a ā i ī u ū e ai o au aṃ aḥ.

On the rim of the moon maṇḍala,
All are arranged around in their places,

means that the twelve syllables should be set encircling the rim of the moon disk. Showing clearly the place where that nectar syllable oṃ is meditated upon, he said:

Upon the cow's hoof,
Having set the nectars,

That is, the meaning is that one should set and meditate upon the nectar syllable oṃ resting on a moon in the maṇḍala of the heart, which is like an egg above the lungs. Or else, wishing to show that this syllable is to be meditated upon as transformed into the wisdom being, he said:

Having meditated on it as the form of wisdom,
Radiant in form, color and the rest,

That is, in the center of the heart, upon the moon disk, one meditates on it in the form of the wisdom being, white in color, with the mudrā of supreme awakening, and with cool rays of light.

Without fear and without harm,
One should meditate on the entity as the entity,

means this: when the yogin, who is an entity, meditates on the entity as the wisdom being, then, not fearing the absence of intrinsic nature, and without harm from focusing on an entity, he should meditate having abandoned both emptiness and entity.

To the yogin who meditates thus he taught the moon mantra:

The milk, the seed of the moon,

That is the syllable oṃ.

Placing homage and burning at the ends,

means that oṃ and svāhā should be placed before and after the syllable oṃ, thus: oṃ svāhā.

Wishing to show the qualities and power of the yogin who cultivates the meditative absorption that is the method of nectar, he first said:

Through the nature of recitation in meditative equipoise,

and so on as far as

The great self of the wisdom of form.

If one asks where the meditative absorption by the method of nectar is to be cultivated, he said:

The places of its application,

and so on. Thus, having understood the meaning of the four beginning with mind and valid cognition, Vajrapāṇi, wishing to hear the meaning through the blessing of the yogin's meditative absorption that joins the mind with luminosity, asked:

The characteristics that transcend saṃsāra,

and so on. Then the Lord of Wisdom, Vajrasattva, wishing to show the meaning of what was asked, said:

Listen to wisdom, to reality,

and so on. Here,

First, with a nondual mind,
With the stainless water of attachment and affliction,
One should wash one's own body,

means that before the meditative absorption one cultivates a mind free of the duality of apprehended and apprehender, and, with it, which is like water free of the stains of the afflictions such as attachment, one washes one's own body and makes it pure.

Wishing to show the qualities of that reality, he said:

It is like the deep ocean,

and so on. Here, the wisdom of emptiness is the wisdom that has no intrinsic nature, called the unchanging reality. If one asks what it is like as an example:

Like the deep ocean,

That is, just as the ocean's depths are not fathomed, and as it is wet, so too the wisdom of emptiness is free of conceptuality and moistened by compassion. "Outer and inner" means grasping at self and what belongs to self.

The body becomes pure like crystal,

means that if one meditates on such a wisdom of emptiness, one's own body becomes pure.

That body equal to crystal,

and so on: the faculty of wisdom, knowing, is mentation, because it is the support of mental consciousness. The meaning is this: just as crystal, conch and the kunda flower, white and clear, are dyed with various colors, so on the stainless body one should apply, as one wishes, the assembly of the holy Dharma, the various empowerments of wisdom.

Wishing to teach the method of making the body clear, the Blessed One said:

By nature, for the yogin,
What has come about through good deeds and bad deeds,

and so on. The things of existence that have come about through the virtue and nonvirtue of all yogins are empty by intrinsic nature, because that is the nature of conditioned phenomena. Therefore, having realized the intrinsic nature of emptiness, one destroys them and makes nondual wisdom appear.

Having thus examined the characteristic of emptiness, he taught the direct realization of emptiness:

Of the characteristic of whatever emptiness,

and so on. Here, "beings" are the inhabitant world, and "form and so on" are the container world. The meaning is this: by the emptiness taught above, one meditates on all things, the inhabitants and the container, as empty by intrinsic nature, and should generate the mind in sameness.

Immediately after the meditation on emptiness is complete, he taught the generation of the seed by the mind of sameness:

One should imagine in its center,

and so on. Here, "one should imagine in the center" means, as he said, that one should meditate on the seed in the center of that mind, through sameness. If one asks what seed:

The milk, the moon, the seed in the center,

that is, in the center of the moon moistened with milk is the seed ā, and so on. Showing clearly that very seed, he said:

Of the union of vowels and consonants,
Abiding in its center,

means that what arises from within the union of skillful means and wisdom is the syllable ā; there it is qualified by the words "vowels and consonants."

By that, that very seed
Should be imagined blazing with rays of light,

means that one should meditate on the seed syllable a upon the moon disk, white in color and blazing, as inseparable from one's own mind. He taught its qualities:

By which, through that very seed,
Wisdom swiftly comes to appear.

Having explained how the seed arises from the mind of sameness, he explained the generation from the seed:

After that, the wisdom body,

and so on. He taught the recitation mantra for that meditation:

Placing homage and burning at the ends,
One should recite this seed,

that is, oṃ a svāhā, and so on. As for the passage beginning "as with the third group," I have already explained the hidden syllable of a above, in the fourth chapter on the seat.

With recitation and meditative concentration, the yogin,

and what follows is easy to understand.

Then, within the blessing and so on,
Through supreme effort in recitation and meditative concentration,

means that after the yogin who has thus meditated and recited has come to one-pointedness of mind, he should perform the blessing that appears in the worlds of the ten directions. If one asks how this is done, he said:

Emanating the rays of light that are sent forth,
One imagines the seed again in three stages.

Here, "seven stages" means "the seven worlds," and so on. Or else it means "up to the wind maṇḍala," and so on. The meaning is this: one should emanate rays of light into the ten directions as far as that, and gather them back.

Wishing to make it appear,
One should always meditate with the mind,

means that the yogin who wishes to meditate thus at all times will make the worlds of the ten directions appear.

Now, teaching the meditation for knowing the minds of others, he said:

Abiding in the center of the heart maṇḍala,

and so on. That is, having meditated on the syllable hūṃ at one's own heart, one should, by its rays of light, meditate on all things as emptiness, in accordance with the characteristic given above. With the mind of emptiness he taught the seat and the seed:

Abiding in the center of the wind maṇḍala,

and so on. Here,

In its center, the seed of wisdom,

is hūṃ. He taught the generation of the body from that seed, from

Then, in the form of wisdom,

as far as

Knowing all the characteristics given above.

Abiding in the center of the maṇḍala at the heart,

and so on, means that one should meditate on the milk seed a described above at one's own heart. If one asks how, moreover, one should meditate on it, he said:

White in color, blazing with light,
White silver everywhere,

means that one should meditate that from the white color, rays of light of white silver are emanated and strike the minds of others.

If one asks what deity one should meditate upon in the sentient beings struck by those rays of light, he said, from

In the many things of others,

as far as

One should vividly imagine Vajrasattva.

He taught the meditation on a syllable at the heart of that Vajrasattva who is meditated upon:

In the center of whose heart,

and so on. The meaning is this: the consciousness of the sentient beings whose minds are to be known becomes the syllable hūṃ, and one should meditate on it as red, in the eight directions. Showing its qualities, he said:

By always seeing sameness,

and so on. Here, "thereby one will see in that syllable all thoughts" means that, in the syllable hūṃ meditated upon in the mind of the other, one sees with one's own mind the signs of whatever the other is thinking.

Now Vajrapāṇi, wishing to hear about the inner fire offering, asked:

Then, the actions of fire,

and so on. The Blessed One gave the answer to what was asked:

Listen to the reality of fire,

and so on. Here, "all of that abides in wisdom" means: first of all, Vajrapāṇi, all sentient beings rely on wisdom.

In the body that is born, the fire of wisdom,

means that this fire of wisdom also abides in the bodies of all sentient beings. If one asks why:

Because all is blessed by the mind,

that is, since mind is inseparable from all sentient beings, they are blessed by one another.

Its burnt offering, by the yogin,

means that the yogin should perform the fire offering of that fire of wisdom.

Having shown the fire of the inner fire offering, wishing to show the place of the hearth of the inner fire offering, he said, from

The burnt offering of the mind of oneself,

as far as

It is encircled by a coiled nāga lord.

This is to be understood through the guru's oral instruction.

In its center, know it as a lotus,

means that in the center of that hearth one meditates on an eight-petaled lotus blazing with rays of light.

Abiding in the center of the sun maṇḍala,
Imagine the Wisdom Goddess seated there,

means that upon the sun disk at the heart of the lotus one meditates on Jñānaḍākinī, blue in color.

On the spine, white and blazing,
One should imagine Vajraḍākinī,

means that on the petal on the western side, at the spine, is Vajraḍākinī, white in color.

In the center of the right thigh,
One should imagine the Fierce One, like fire.

On the petal on the side of the yogin's right organ is Ghoraḍākinī, yellow in color.

On the navel, red and blazing,
One should imagine Vajraḍākinī,

means that on the petal on the side of the yogin's navel is Vetālī, red in color.

On the left thigh, black,
Is the fierce one, the blazing Caṇḍālī,

means that on the petal on the side of the yogin's left organ is Caṇḍālī, black in color.

Those whose nature is the five fires,

means the fires blue, white, red, yellow and black, blazing in the body.

The form is to be seen as the size of a thumb,

means that one meditates on the bodies of those five goddesses as the size of a thumb. If one asks what the nature of those five goddesses is:

Water, fire and wind,
Earth and wisdom, the five kinds,

that is, the four great elements beginning with earth, together with consciousness, are made into five kinds. In the Buddha's word only four great elements occur, and space is not among them, because it is not something that functions. Wherever "five great elements" occurs, there space is not meant; it should be understood as wisdom alone.

To the deities of the fivefold nature,

means that the yogin should match his own four great elements and wisdom, five, with the five goddesses beginning with Jñānaḍākinī. That is: wisdom with Jñānaḍākinī; the water element with Vajraḍākinī; the fire element with Ghoraḍākinī; the wind element with Vetālī, the vetāla ḍākinī; and the earth element with Caṇḍālī, the caṇḍāla ḍākinī. These are to be matched.

The supreme rite of burnt offering,

means that of the yogin's two fire offerings, outer and inner, the inner fire offering is declared supreme. So too it is said in the Nopika of the Guhyasamāja, the Illumination of the World:

The outer fire offering is inference;
Since mind belongs to sentient beings,
This is unsurpassed:
Having kindled the fire of wisdom,
One burns the firewood of the aggregates.
From the limbs of the eighteen,

and so on. This has already been explained.

Having explained the hearth and so on of the inner fire offering, he taught the meditation of the inner fire offering:

The cause of burning within the fire,

and so on. Here, first of all, "the cause of burning within the fire" is food, drink, nectar and so on.

First, the seeds that are the vowel letters,

means the syllable a. "If one meditates on reality in this way" means that the substances of the inner fire offering, drink and so on, should be purified by the very reality of the syllable a.

The burnt offering of oneself becomes abundant,

is easy to understand. Wishing to show clearly the meaning of that very point, the Blessed One said:

Whatever food and drink there may be,
Having dedicated them, one should eat.

He taught the qualities of the inner fire offering:

The yogin who practices in this way
Does not fall into debt.

That is, when the yogin applies the instruction given above to food and drink, they become nectar and do not become dregs. Or else, it means that they do not become a cause for burning in existence, but become a cause of nirvāṇa. The meaning is that, as with a debt, it does not turn into a fault.

This completes the explanation of the second chapter of the Secret Seat, from the Vision of the Glorious Catuṣpīṭha composed by the paṇḍita Smṛtijñānakīrti.

Now, the words of Vajrapāṇi's question, wishing to understand the four instructions, on the sameness of the body, the sameness of wisdom, the emanating, and the activity of the breath going out and coming in,

The characteristics of the application of the body,

and so on, begin the third chapter of the Secret Seat. Here,

Unsteady like a monkey,

means: just as a monkey does not stay in one place, so consciousness, even in the instant of engaging with form and so on, is distracted and unsteady; how is it to be applied in sameness? So he asked:

By what sameness shall it be applied?

"Wondrous" means without before or after. The Blessed One gave the answer to that:

The very distinctions of the mind's soaring,
Listen, O Vajra, great abode,

Here, "Vajra" is Vajrapāṇi. "Great secret": the secret ones are the yakṣas, and since he is their chief he is the great secret; this is an epithet distinguishing Vajrapāṇi.

Now, wishing first to show, among those four, the sameness of the body, he said:

By the mind abiding in sameness,
Having abandoned, one should enter kāli,

Here, "kāli" is the body, because it has become the cause through skillful means itself. The meaning is this: from the nature of reality, the nondual mind, one should generate a body for the sake of sentient beings.

In teaching that the body is equal to the five, first, for its application as equal to water, four measures are taught. The application with the measure of the action of water is:

Through the measure of water in all,
The sprout of the body arises,

because a sprout arises from a seed through moisture, and the arising of the sprout of the body, too, is because the water element is chief. He taught the measure of the application of the nature of the water element:

Without water, form is not possible;
The measure of form is water,

because the wet nature in the body comes from the water element being chief. He taught the measure of the application of the support of water:

If all is pervaded by this,
O wise one, in the measure of water,

because it is chiefly by water that it abides, pervading all the limbs of the body.

He taught the measure of the application of the goddess of water:

The Vajra Goddess of the body,
O wise one, is called the measure,

that is, the chief of the water of the body is the goddess called Vajraḍākinī.

Then, of the four measures taught for the application of fire, he taught the measure of the application of the nature of fire:

All in the body is of fire;
The blazing in form is warmth,

because the nature of the body that possesses warmth comes from the fire element being chief. He taught the measure of the application of the action of fire:

The measures of what is eaten within:
Fire is known as the measure,

because it is chiefly the fire element that performs the action of accomplishing and ripening all the food and drink in the body. He taught the measure of the application of the support of fire:

Since it pervades all of the body,
Know it everywhere as what is called fire,

because it is chiefly the support of the warm substance in all the limbs of the body. He taught the measure of the application of the goddess of fire:

The one taught as the Fierce One, of the body,
Strive supremely in what is known as the measure,

that is, the chief of the fire of the body is called Ghoraḍākinī, the fierce ḍākinī.

Of the four measures taught for the application of wind, he taught the measure of the application of its nature:

By wind, everywhere in the body,
Like the openings and network within,

because the nature of moving through the openings and network of the body comes from the wind element being chief. He taught the measure of the application of the action of wind:

Since the measure of the mind is the breath,
What is known as the measure is realized,

because it is chiefly the wind element that performs the action of the mind of the body holding life through wind. He taught the measure of the application of the support of wind:

That by which the breath, in direct knowing,
Abides in application in the body,

is wisdom; it is because the wind element is chief in what relies upon the wisdom of the body. He taught the measure of the application of the goddess of wind:

The goddess Vetālī, everywhere,
Should be known by the wise,

means that the chief of the wind of the body should be known as the goddess Vetālī.

Of the four measures taught for the application of earth, he taught the measure of the application of the nature of earth:

All of earth, in every shape,
The qualities in form, adorned with faculties,

because it is chief in the nature that adorns the body with the faculties, form and so on. He taught the measure of the application of the action of earth:

The measure that arises as the place of earth,
Through earth appearing as form,

because it is chief in performing the action of holding, as form in the body becomes the place of water, fire, wind and consciousness. He taught the measure of the application of the support of earth:

Therefore, endowed with qualities,
The nature known as earth,

Here, the qualities are form, the faculties and so on; it is because the earth element is chief in being the support of these in the body. He taught the measure of the application of the goddess of earth:

The nature is the Caṇḍāla Goddess;
Everywhere she is known as the chief,

means that the goddess chief in the earth element of the body is called Caṇḍālī.

He taught four measures of the application of wisdom. He taught the measure of the application of the nature of wisdom:

Gathering, acting and enjoying;
The mind cuts through doubt,

that is, because it is chief in cutting through doubt as to whether there is in the body an agent, a gatherer and an enjoyer, it is the nature of wisdom, since it possesses conceptual thought that ascertains. He taught the measure of the application of the action of wisdom:

All the faculties, by the minds,
The indicated characteristics should be known,

Here, "all the faculties" are the six beginning with the eye. "By the minds" means the six beginning with eye consciousness. "The indicated" means form, sound and so on. "Characteristics" means color, shape, pleasant and unpleasant sound, and so on. The meaning is this: it is called the chief in performing the action of knowing the characteristics of form and so on, through eye consciousness and the rest, by way of the faculties such as the eye. He taught the measure of the application of the support of wisdom:

The enjoyment of application, the sovereign lady,
The bliss of objects and so on,

Here, the king is wisdom. The meaning is this: because the faculties are applied to objects of experience such as bliss, and wisdom, having enjoyed them, is chief as the support. He taught the measure of the application of the wisdom goddess:

The queen of the wisdom of the body,
Should be realized by the yoginī as the measure,

Here, "queen" is Jñānaḍākinī. The meaning is that the skilled yogin should know that the chief goddess of the wisdom of the body is Jñānaḍākinī.

Then, having established the application of the body with the five great elements beginning with water, he taught the application of the four great elements to wisdom. Here, first, is the application of water and mind:

Since it arises from water and mind,
Consciousness is the water element,

that is, because the water element is generated from the nondual mind, wisdom and the water element are one. To show that these two are inseparable:

If water were something else,
Why does water not arise from flesh?

that is, since there is no water in a corpse devoid of wisdom, it is evident that wisdom and the water element are not different. He taught the application of wisdom and fire:

Since consciousness arises as fire,
It is certain as the very warmth in the body.

He taught that these two are indeed inseparable:

In a body from which wisdom has departed,
Why does warmth not appear?

that is, since there is no warmth in a body devoid of wisdom, it is evident that wisdom and the fire element are not different. He taught the application of wisdom and wind:

By consciousness as wind, in bodies,
The breath going out and coming in is like smoke.

He taught the very meaning of these two being inseparable:

In bodies from which wisdom has departed,
In them too there is no breath,

that is, since there is no breath in a body devoid of wisdom, it is evident that wisdom and breath are not different. He taught the application of wisdom and earth:

Since mind arises as earth,
It appears as heaviness.

Since the mind inseparable from these two is earth, it appears as heaviness in the body, because the essence of earth is heaviness. He taught the very meaning of these two being inseparable:

Bodies from which wisdom has departed
Float in water,

that is, since a corpse devoid of wisdom floats on water, it is evident that wisdom and the earth element are not different, because a corpse has no heaviness.

All mental consciousness, too, should be understood as in fact nothing but mind and wisdom, because they are a single substance differing only as earlier and later in time. So it is said in the Treasury, the Koṣa:

Whatever consciousness immediately follows
The cessation of the six, that is mentation.

All five measures of the body,

means that since water, fire, wind, earth and wisdom, taught above, are applied as measures, it is called the application of the body. With what precedes, the answer to the words of the question "The characteristics of the application of the body" has been explained. With the following words he answered the question asked with the words "By what sameness shall it be applied?":

All five are of a single essence;
Having known them as become one,

that is, since these five, water and the rest, are of the nature of the one mind, the meaning is that they should be known as of the nature of the one nature of reality, because the nondual mind and phenomena are not distinct. He taught its qualities:

It is the view of the place of the yogin,

that is, this is the instruction on the application of the body; the meaning is that the yogin who has meditated thus attains the path of nirvāṇa. Again, he taught its qualities alone:

Thus, of the one who directly knows the characteristics,

and so on. That is, having relied on a guru, a spiritual friend, the characteristics of the application to the body means that one is freed from the saṃsāra of existence.

Now the Blessed One Vajrasattva, moved by compassion, knowing with his own mind the mind of Vajrapāṇi, considered, and wishing to teach the application of wisdom as the answer to "By what sameness shall it be applied?", he said:

The wisdom of the faculties and objects,

and so on. Here,

By the wisdom of the faculties and objects,
What does harm should be restrained,

Here, the objects are form, sound and so on. Wisdom is eye consciousness and so on. The meaning is this: the yogin should first restrain and tame eye consciousness and the rest, which engage in a distorted way with the objects of experience, form and so on, that do harm; for the yogin's objects of experience are only a matter of the pledges, not of enjoyment. So the Blessed One himself said in the fourth chapter of the Seat of Union:

It is not delight in desires;
Enjoyments are not to be indulged outwardly.

Having thus taught the restraint of the five engaging consciousnesses, he taught the restraint of the conceptual consciousness:

In the places of the house of the egg-born,
One should restrain it in the center.

Here, "egg" is the heart, because it resembles an egg. The mind born from that egg is called "egg-born," just as the consciousness that relies on the eye is called eye consciousness. The meaning is that in the place of the egg-born, the center of the heart, one should restrain the conceptual consciousness, inseparable from the mind. Showing that very point clearly, he said:

The mind that moves and causes movement
Through distraction by unstable things:
The support of the place of restraint
Is the lotus seat of consciousness.

That is, the meaning is that one should meditate on a lotus in the center of the egg, as the place where the conceptual consciousness that makes the mind move is restrained. If one asks what shape the consciousness meditated upon in the center of the lotus has, he said:

By the yogin of all the tantras,
Consciousness is indicated as a form.

Here, "the form of consciousness" is the syllable hūṃ. The meaning is that the yogin, at the initial stage of meditating on the cause, should indicate that consciousness solely by the form of the syllable hūṃ. If one asks why formless consciousness is visualized in the shape of the syllable hūṃ, he said:

Because, by the thing called form,
It is for the sake of accomplishing the union.

That is, because he wishes to attain the accomplishment of the union of meditative absorption, the yogin meditates on the thing that is the form of hūṃ.

Then, having taught the place of the restraint of wisdom, he taught the method of applying the restraint of wisdom:

From the plantain-flower navel,

and so on. Here, the plantain flower is the one resembling a cow's hoof, because it faces downward and its shape is like a plantain flower; this is the line

From the navel upward rises the life channel.

Abiding also as rising to the heart,

means that rising upward from the navel it abides at the heart.

Always with its mouth facing downward,

means that the one resembling a cow's hoof curves up and down and faces downward.

The wise one should make it expand,

means that the yogin skilled in means should make that nectar cow's hoof expand. Then he taught the maṇḍala from which nectar drips from the expanded plantain flower:

It always becomes eight-petaled,

he said, and "endowed with anthers," that is, just as with the fire offering taught above, here, above, directly at the navel, one should meditate on eight petals endowed with anthers. He taught the deity set there:

What is always called its breath
Is the very union of the syllable of consciousness,

Here, the wisdoms are the syllable hūṃ; the meaning is that one should meditate on it in the center of that lotus. Then he taught the rite for making the plantain flower expand above:

The perfect union, from the east,

Here, the perfect union is the syllable hūṃ, because it is of the nature of the union of the five buddhas, the five wisdoms and consciousness. "From the east" means to the east of that hūṃ, on the side of the navel.

A circumference of eight fingerbreadths,

means outside, at eight fingerbreadths toward the front.

On the western side, at the spine,

means on the side of the spine, behind that very syllable hūṃ. If one asks how far outside that is:

Below, again at five fingerbreadths,

Here, "below" means the circumference outside at five fingerbreadths.

The seeds of its field,

Here, "its field" means from the east to the west, and the seeds of these two are the syllable su, white, and yu, red.

Like rays of light from which a sprout arises,

means the sprout arising from the white su, the seed of nectar.

The rays of light of that seed,

is called "the light of yu, the seed of wind."

Make the plantain flower blossom,

The meaning of this is as follows: the white syllable su abides in the region of the navel. The light of nectar, driven on further by the red syllable su that abides on the western side of the spine, dissolves into the plantain flower and makes it blossom.

Having taught the method for increasing the lower nectar, he teaches the method for increasing the upper nectar:

By the five resounding ones, drawing it down
Beneath the stupa, make it proceed.

As in the meaning of the previous chapter, the nectar descends from the root of the tongue to the stupa; descending from the stupa, it goes to the upper channel; and descending from the upper channel, it should be made to dissolve into the plantain flower. That is the sense.

Then, teaching the meaning of the nectar that drips from the plantain blossoming on the eight-petaled lotus taught above:

Adorned with eight petals,
The lotus, the Radiant One: make it proceed there.

That is, the seed, the Radiant One, set in the center of the eight-petaled lotus: there it should be made to dissolve through drops of the plantain's nectar. Just this is stated:

On the seat of this lotus
Is the abode of the egg-born wisdom.

That is, the seed, the Radiant One, in the center of the lotus is the syllable hūṃ, indivisible from the egg-born mind. If one asks what the color of the syllable hūṃ is, it is said:

In the center, like crystal,
Is the Teacher, wisdom-consciousness.

Then again he teaches the placing of seeds on the eight lotus petals:

The seeds of the four ḍākinīs
Should be placed on the petals of the directions.

That is, the seeds of the four, Vajraḍākinī and the others, should be placed on the petals of the directions. That is, the seeds of Vajraḍākinī and the others are to be set on the eastern petal and the rest.

What are their seeds?

The first seed, sound and so on,
Just as the characteristics were taught.

Here, "the first seed, sound and so on" is ā, because of the sixteen vowel letters. "Just as the characteristics were taught" means affixing a sign below, affixing a stroke at the side, and affixing a crescent moon and a dot above. The letters are these: a ā aṃ aḥ.

He teaches the placing of the seeds of the four intermediate directions:

In the intermediate directions, Siṃhinī and the others:
Their neuter seeds should be known.

That is, the seeds of the four, Siṃhinī and the others, the neuter letters ṛ ṝ li ḹ, should be set on the petals of the intermediate directions.

At the heart, by the entity, the entity,

means that the yogin of the entity should meditate on the syllable of the entity at the heart. Which one?

Place the stable letter at the heart.

In the previous chapter, the syllable placed at the heart was taught to be the syllable oṃ moistened by nectar; here too it should be understood as just the nectar syllable oṃ.

Then he teaches that a stream of nectar is drawn out from such a plantain flower and strikes the syllables of the lotus:

From the cow's hoof above,
The stream of nectar through the nine doors
The yogin constantly drinks:
All the white letters and the rest.

That is, a stream of nectar-water flows from the cow's hoof above, and the nectar should strike the nine white syllables set on the eight lotus petals below and in its center. The nine syllables are these: hūṃ a āḥ aṃ aḥ ṛ ṝ ḷ ḹ.

Then he teaches the radiating of light rays by the yogin who meditates in this way:

From the pores of the blazing hairs
One should make white nectar appear.

Again, he teaches the mantra to be recited by the yogin who meditates in this way:

The seed of wisdom-consciousness
The yogin recites at all times.

Here, "the seed of wisdom, consciousness" is the syllable hūṃ; that is, oṃ hūṃ svāhā. Even where oṃ and svāhā are absent, they should be understood as implied, because it is said:

All mantras, for the most part,
Are furnished with homage and the burnt offering.

"Going, walking, sitting," and what follows has already been explained.

Of the union arising from wisdom,

This gives the name of their meditative absorption: "the one that reveals wisdom." Then, wishing to teach the union in which the mind radiates out, he prompts with the passage beginning "The characteristic of the union for attaining Vajrasattva." Here, "radiating" means one's own consciousness radiating, by means of method, to the higher realms. "The characteristic of the union" is the characteristic of the union of such meditative absorption, which comes below.

Then Vajrapāṇi asked, in the passage beginning:

How do the nine doors and the rest abide?
I bow to the Lord of Wisdom.

Here, the Lord of Wisdom is Vajrasattva, because he is the chief of the eight consciousnesses. The reply is given in turn, in the passage beginning "The measures of the time of death." Here, "the time of death" refers to the cessation of breath and so on, taught in the second chapter of the Ātmapīṭha; these are the measures of the time of death.

Teaching thus the virtues and faults of the different doors at the time of death:

The good path is the abode of beauty;
The bad path is the fault of existence.

Here, "the good path" is the six doors. "The abode of beauty" is the higher realms. "The bad path" is the three doors. "The fault of existence" is the three lower realms. If one asks what the six good doors and the three bad doors are, it is said, in the passage beginning "The drop, the navel, the crown of the head."

Then, in teaching the faults of union with the doors, he first teaches the virtues:

The navel: the higher realm of the desirous.

This means that if wisdom radiates from the yogin's navel, he goes to be born among the six classes of gods of the desire realm, the Four Great Kings and the rest.

The bodies of the three drops,

means that if wisdom radiates from the drop at the forehead, he goes to be born among the seventeen classes of gods of form, Brahmā and the rest.

In the abode of the higher and higher,
That destiny is to be thoroughly examined.

Here, "the higher" is the crown of the head. "The abode of the higher" is the formless realm, because it is the highest existence. The meaning is this: if consciousness radiates from the yogin's crown, it should be examined as going to be born among the four classes of the formless realm, Infinite Space and the rest.

By the nose, to the abode of the yakṣas,

means that if wisdom radiates from the nose, one is born on the summit of Mount Meru among the classes of yakṣas, Virūpākṣa and the rest. That is the sense.

The two ears: the accomplished gods.

Here, the accomplished gods are eight: the gods accomplished in the sword, the gods accomplished through eye salve, the gods accomplished in pills, the gods accomplished in the underworld, the gods accomplished in invisibility, the gods accomplished in arriving in an instant, the gods accomplished in flying through the sky, and the gods accomplished in seeing everything. The meaning is this: if consciousness radiates from the ears, one is born among the classes of those gods.

If wisdom radiates from the eyes,
One becomes a king among humans.

This means that if wisdom radiates from the eyes, one is born as a wheel-turning king among humans and the like.

Then, having taught the virtues of the doors, he teaches their faults:

Through the door of existence, the hungry ghosts.

Here, the door of existence is the mouth. Why is that? Because the food that sustains the five aggregates of existence is put in through it, it is called the door of existence. The meaning is this: if wisdom radiates from it, one is born among the hungry ghosts.

Likewise, through water, the animals.

Here, "water" refers to the male organ and the female organ, because they are the paths of water. If light rays radiate through the door of the path of water, one is born as an animal.

The states of the eight hells:
Through not-water, consciousness swiftly goes there.

Here, "not-water" is the path of excrement, because waste comes out of it. If it radiates through the door of not-water, one is born in the eight hells, Saṃjīva and the rest.

Since virtues and faults are thus joined to the doors, it is supreme for the yogin, the moment the time of death has come, to radiate through a door of virtue. This is stated in the passage beginning "Whoever, by the distinctions of the doors."

Then, teaching the method of transference, he said, in the passage beginning:

Having first undertaken the vase,

that is, one should practice the actual meditative absorption of stopping the breath.

All the doors should be blocked,

means that the nine doors should be blocked. If one asks how they are to be blocked, he said:

By the five resounding ones, the doors
Of the stupa should be meditated on as blocked.

The instruction is that one blocks directly at each of them, the mouth and the rest. Then the blocking is the blocking at the six doors, the crown of the head and the rest.

Then, at the lower door as well:

The seed of the cause is white.

Here, the lower door is to be understood as the navel; there one should block it with the white su, the seed of water.

At water and not-water, by fire,
Its seed made to blaze,
The yogin who blocks the openings,

Here, "water" is the male organ, and "not-water" is the path of excrement. At the doors of these two, one should block with the yellow seed of fire. Then he teaches that the yogin who has blocked the doors in this way meditates with the breath unmoving:

In that vase, with the breath,
One should meditate in perfect equipoise.

Again, if one asks how the yogin who stops the breath should meditate on the body, he said, in the passage beginning:

In the body, the color of wind,

that is, one meditates on the whole body as filled with wind, in the color of wind, the color of ash.

In the wind maṇḍala of the mind,

means that one should meditate on a half-moon as the wind maṇḍala of one's own mind. He teaches the mantra set in that maṇḍala:

The seed of wind, until it is drawn,

that is, the syllable yaṃ should be set in the maṇḍala. If one asks where in the body it is to be meditated on, he said:

At the root of the wind's end,

that is, one meditates on it at the end of the body filled with wind, the soles of the feet.

With the drop and the nāda,

means that to the bare seed of wind, ya, one should add the vowel sign below and the dot. Thereby it becomes the syllable yuṃ.

The seed should be drawn by the mind,

means that the seed of the mind, the syllable hūṃ, should be drawn upward. If one asks by what it is drawn:

To the vajra seed and the fierce one,
Joining the hook syllable,

Here, "the vajra seed" is the syllable hūṃ, and "the seed of the fierce one" is the syllable kṣuṃ. The meaning is this: the syllable hūṃ is to be led by kṣuṃ. If one asks from which place and how far it is to be drawn, he said, in the passage beginning:

With twenty-one breaths,

that is, it should be drawn over the course of twenty-one breaths.

From the place of one joint to the next joint,
Up to the highest of the nine joints.

This means that it should be drawn through the soles of the feet, the ankles, the knees, the waist, the navel, the heart, the shoulders, the throat, and up to the crown of the head.

Having taught the drawing upward, he explains the hidden syllable that pushes from below:

By the seed that turns upward and turns downward,
One and a half syllables are to be joined.

Here, the seed is phaṭ. Explaining it, "half a syllable" is said: pha is a complete syllable, and ṭa is the half, because it is attached to pha.

Then again he states the hidden syllable of the seed that draws upward from above:

By fiercely uttering the nāda,

refers to the vowel sign below and the dot of the syllable kṣuṃ.

And the seed that is last of the eight,

is kṣa, the last of the eight syllables beginning with ya. Showing that very kṣa, he states it clearly:

The first of the classes, the highest,

that is, the syllable ka.

Joined with half a syllable,

means that in the syllable kṣa, ka is the half, for it is half of the stack of ka and ṣa.

He also teaches the meditative absorption of the rite that follows the pushing described above:

From below the wind maṇḍala,
With the nāda, from the half, it should be driven out.

The sense is that from the soles of the feet, below the wind maṇḍala, it is pushed from below by phaṭ and raised up.

Endowed with the seed of wind,
The wind maṇḍala should be contemplated.

This means that one meditates on the wind maṇḍala pushed upward by phaṭ as endowed with breath, mind, and life force.

From the place of the twenty-one,
From where it is given its name,

The sense is that, drawing it over twenty-one breaths, once it has gone up through the nine places, one does not let it go back to the lower places.

The yogin who would turn it back
Should bring the half down to the root.

This means that the yogin who turns the wind maṇḍala upward from the soles of the feet should bring it down as far as the waist.

If it is sent upward from the nine joints,
It is called instant transference.

This means that the transference of consciousness from the crown of the head, the uppermost of the nine joints, is called instant transference; that is, it is the best. The virtue of knowing transference in this way is that even if one kills brahmins and the like, it does not become a sin. This is in the passage beginning:

Even if one kills a brahmin every day,

Here,

And commits the five deeds of immediate retribution,

refers to killing one's father, killing one's mother, killing an arhat, drawing the blood of a tathāgata with malicious intent, and causing a schism in the Saṅgha. He gives an example for this:

Just as from the mud is born
The lotus, whose radiance arises beautiful,

He states the meaning:

So, in the body of mud,
The wisdom body arises freely.

The sense is that even from the appropriated aggregates, which are with defilement, if wisdom transfers through a special door, one is born in the higher realms, unstained by impurity like a lotus.

If one asks at what time transference is to be applied, he said:

When the time has come, transference should be done,

This means it should be done when the time of death has come, and not otherwise. If one asks what happens if transference is done otherwise, he said:

Out of time, one comes to kill the gods.

That is, if one performs transference when it is not the time of death, one comes to kill the gods of the five families and the rest, because the five aggregates have the essential nature of the five families.

Now, wishing to teach the concluding union of the mind, the Blessed One said, in the passage beginning:

Listen, Vajra, to the very reality
Of the distinctions of accomplished union.

Here, having meditated on the emptiness of sameness, he teaches the meditation on the pledge being, in the passage from "By the entity of the mind of sameness" up to "Set out the seed syllables." Here, the seeds are the eight beginning with su.

He teaches the syllable set in the center by one who has thus meditated on the deity:

The vowels and consonants and the rest,

This is the white syllable ā. Explaining just the seed to be meditated on, he says, in the passage beginning:

On the lotus of the plantain flower,

The sense is that at the heart, above the cow's hoof, one meditates on the egg-born mind's syllable hūṃ together with ā, as one and indivisible.

Then he teaches the outer activity of the yogin who meditates on that meditative absorption: "Then the outer union and the rest are to be understood." In the passage beginning "The mind doubled, to oneself," "the mind doubled" means meditating with one's own mind divided into two, outer and inner. If one asks how the outer mind is to be meditated on, it is in the passage beginning "On the grounds." That is, in the middle of the stacked outer maṇḍala of the being, wind maṇḍala, fire maṇḍala, and sun maṇḍala, one should meditate on the red seed syllable a upon the vowels and consonants.

Having meditated thus on outer and inner, he teaches the recitation:

One should recite in arising and entering.

The sense is that one recites the mantra oṃ a svā as the breath goes out, and gathers it back as the breath comes in. If one asks what such recitation does, he said:

By the arising of the wisdom seed,
It comes to destroy in the center.

This means that by the arising of the seed a, accompanied by the entering breath and the recitation of the mantra, one should imagine it reaching the center of the a in the outer sun maṇḍala. He states the meaning of just this clearly, in the passage beginning "Reciting with the arising."

He teaches the equal meditation of the yogin who thus meditates on arising and entering as the same, in the passage beginning "Teaching the characteristic of arising." He teaches the rite of knowing the minds of others for the yogin who meditates on that very meditative absorption, in the passage beginning "Then the outer and other bodies."

The explanation of the fourth chapter of the Secret Seat, from the Vision of the Glorious Catuṣpīṭha composed by the paṇḍita Smṛtijñānakīrti, is complete.

Now, wishing to ask about the union of mind and wisdom, the four immeasurables, the union of the mind, the meditative absorption of the drop, the subtle meditative absorption, mantra recitation, the signs, the reality of the stupa, and the joining of the expanse and wisdom, Vajrapāṇi began the fourth chapter of the Secret Seat with the passage beginning "Two in the Secret Seat." It is called this because this chapter is secret within the Secret Seat.

Making mind and wisdom equal,

Mind is with defilement; that is because it is the wisdom of discernment that distinguishes the nondual nature of reality. Wisdom is vast, because it is the very essence of compassion and the rest.

How is the completion stage of awakening?

This asks which, among the nine vehicles, those of the śrāvakas, the pratyekabuddhas, the bodhisattvas, the perfectly complete buddhas, the vajra family, the buddha family, the jewel family, the lotus family, and the action family, is the vehicle of perfectly complete awakening.

The Blessed One Vajrasattva gave the answer to what was asked, in the passage beginning:

The secret of secrets, of the supreme Dharma,

Here, "secret" is body, speech, and mind, because they are not shared with the śrāvakas and the rest. "Secret" is also because of the very intrinsic nature of method and wisdom, and of mind and wisdom. The meaning is this: because method and wisdom are more secret even than body, speech, and mind, it is called "the secret of secrets." Therefore it is the supreme Dharma, because it is the cause of the non-abiding nirvāṇa of the perfectly complete buddhas. The meaning is: "O Vajrapāṇi, this is the stage of perfectly complete awakening."

If one asks how it is to be meditated on in brief, he said:

By generating the mind of sameness,
Desire and the rest are cast far away.

This means that the yogin who generates the mind of sameness, whose meaning is nonduality, should abandon the afflictions, the distorted desire and the rest.

Then again he teaches the meaning of method and wisdom extensively, first teaching method, in the passage beginning:

In a delightful place,

Here, "delightful" means ground endowed with water, flowers, and fruit trees; or else it means a secret place.

Seated on a comfortable cushion,

means soft and thick; or else it means seated while experiencing the bliss of the union of method and wisdom.

With a blissful mind, the elements are purified.

The sense is that one should meditate on the eighteen elements, the eye element and the rest, with the mind that arises from the bliss of method and wisdom.

If one asks what is to be meditated on, he said:

Make the mind that benefits through compassion.

This means that the merit of pleasing the deities of the body maṇḍala with the secret offering should be dedicated, with a mind of compassion, to the love of sentient beings.

Now, to teach the meaning of wisdom extensively, he said, in the passage beginning:

The teaching, having attained suchness,

Wisdom is to be understood as the yogin's attaining the reality of the teaching. If one asks how it is to be attained, he said:

In the master's scripture and instruction,

That reality of wisdom is obtained through the master's instruction into the ear, and not otherwise, because it is the very cause of the supreme state. It is the lineage of reality. Here, "secret" is said because of its primordial nature itself. "That itself" is said because of its indestructible nature, that is, because of its unborn nature. Thus it is said in the Guhyasamāja:

Indestructible and unborn:
That is what the Protector taught.

Through the lineage of reality,

means that it is to be known through the lineage of instruction.

Now he teaches the four immeasurables and the union of the mind, in the passage beginning:

Whose characteristic in all,

Where is it said? It says that one should meditate on emptiness by means of the characteristic of emptiness. With the mind purified by that and by emptiness, one should meditate on the stage of awakening; so he said:

The completion stage of awakening is principal.

If one asks how it is principal, he said, in the passage from "Abiding in the center of the moon maṇḍala" up to "Declare, 'I am Vajrasattva'"; this is easy to understand.

He teaches meditating inwardly, beginning from the pledge beings, in the passage beginning "From the navel, the plantain flower"; up to "As before, make the lotus blossom," this has already been explained.

On the most beautifully adorned eight petals,
Together with the filaments and the stalk,

Here it is held to be above the plantain flower. One should meditate on what is called the eight-petaled lotus, together with its center. If one asks what is to be meditated on, he said:

On the seat of the egg-born in the center,

Here, the egg-born is the mind, and its seat is the egg of the heart's core. The sense is that one should meditate on the eight-petaled lotus in the middle of the heart.

He teaches the mantras set on the center and the petals of the lotus, and their attached signs, in the passage beginning "The letters to be set should be set." Then, to teach the setting of the syllables, he said:

In its center, consciousness
And wisdom, these two, should be joined.

That is, it says that at the center of that lotus one should set the white hūṃ, the essence of consciousness.

Endowed with love, on the eastern
Petal, the unconfused seed,

means setting on the eastern petal the syllable ma, the seed of Akṣobhya, whose nature is love.

Compassion at the southern door:
The jewel seed itself.

means setting on the southern petal the syllable a, the seed of Ratnasambhava, whose nature is compassion.

On the petal of the western door,
Set out the seed of Amitābha.

means setting on the western petal the syllable a, the seed of Amitābha, whose nature is joy.

On the petal of the Great Meru,
Contemplate the lord Amoghasiddhi.

Here, "the side of the Great Meru" means the northern petal. There one should set the syllable āṃ, the seed of Amoghasiddhi, whose nature is equanimity. These four syllables are to be concealed:

The first, the seed that utters sound,
With staff and dot, in their order,

This has already been explained.

The setting of the four syllables
Is adorned with white light rays.

This is the color of the four seeds above. He teaches the setting of the syllables on the petals of the intermediate directions:

In the four intermediate directions,
The neuter ones, as taught above,

that is, the white ṛ ṝ ḷ ḹ.

The mind, in their center,
Should be examined as syllables.

This means that one should meditate on those syllables having imagined them in the center of the empty mind.

Vajrapāṇi, in doubt about this very point, asked again, in the passage beginning:

Emptiness is without characteristics.

He asked whether, in what lacks characteristics, the nine syllables, whose nature is compassion and the rest, arise from the empty mind. In reply, the Blessed One said, in the passage beginning:

Listen, Vajra, to reality.

Here,

By indicating the characteristic of emptiness,
The essences that are without essence,

The sense is that the indication of the characteristic of emptiness is that there is no real essence, and yet it appears as real. He gives an example for this:

Like the nectar of the plantain's cluster.

The sense is that just as, from the plantain that has no core, there grows a cluster of fruit which, when eaten, yields a taste like nectar, so compassion arising from the empty mind becomes the cause of the result, the enjoyment body of the Tathāgata.

Having taught the condensed meaning, wishing to teach it again extensively, he said, in the passage beginning:

The wisdom abiding in the center,

Here, wisdom is the syllable hūṃ.

The form of wisdom is formless.

means that the syllable hūṃ is formless, its nature being empty. If one asks what it is like, then:

It is the characteristic without characteristics.

That is, although it has the characteristic of the shape of a syllable, it is without characteristics, because it is by nature empty. If one asks why:

The wisdom body has no stain.

That is, the wisdom body of the nature of reality is not stained by entities, because it is by nature luminous. Therefore, teaching "the nature of the nine syllables is as follows," the Teacher said, in the passage beginning "Being free of entity and non-entity." The passage beginning "Whoever knows the characteristics" means that whatever yogin knows sameness, knowing the general and specific characteristics as endowed with wisdom, swiftly attains the path of awakening.

If one asks how that sameness is to be known, he said, in the passage beginning:

Through the instruction of the guru's scripture,

The characteristic of the form of wisdom and consciousness is to be taught as the teaching through the guru's instruction, and not otherwise. He states just this clearly:

Reality is taught by the hero.

Here, the hero is the Tathāgata, or the guru and the like, because they are heroic in the battle against the afflictions.

The sense is that the hero himself teaches the emptiness of the two, because it is transmitted from ear hole to ear.

By meditating on this, everywhere
It is adorned with white light rays.

Here, "this" is the nine syllables. It means that one should meditate on the ten directions, all of them, as illuminated by their white light rays.

Now, wishing to teach the meditative absorption of the drop, the Blessed One said:

Having imagined the stupa of the five resounding ones
And the seed of nectar,

This means that at the place where one has meditated on the stupa of the five resounding ones, one should meditate on the seed of nectar, the syllable oṃ, upon a moon maṇḍala. If one asks how it is to be meditated on, he said:

Beautiful with light of white color,
The stream of nectar is to be set down.

This means that the moon maṇḍala is moistened with nectar, and the stream of nectar trickling from oṃ should be set down onto the drop below. Below that, he teaches the meditation of the drop, in the passage beginning:

Round, the size of a thumb,
Held in the center of the moon,

It says that one should meditate, at the throat, in the center of a moon maṇḍala, on a round drop the size of a thumb. He teaches the seed in its center: "Arisen from the vowels and consonants"; that is the syllable ā.

In its center, at the doors,

means that one should bring the stream of nectar down onto the syllable in the center of that drop.

Contemplation of the drop is well renowned.

Up to this point, it is taught as the meditative absorption of the drop.

Now, wishing to teach the subtle meditative absorption, he said, in the passage beginning:

In a hundredth part of the tip of a hair,

Here, "a hundredth part of the tip of a hair" states its measure. Of what?

The mind, the mere form of a minute particle,

That is, one meditates on the mind, which is beyond the senses, as a minute particle. If one asks what its shape is, he said:

Possessing the wisdom that marks the characteristic,
The very essence of consciousness,

One should contemplate it as marked with the characteristic of the shape of the syllable hūṃ, the seed of wisdom and consciousness. He teaches the meditation:

Where the method of the drop abides,
Contemplate it by knowing the characteristic.

The sense is that at that very place of the drop above, one should also contemplate the syllable hūṃ, with the characteristic of the subtle meditative absorption.

If one asks where in the body and speech that subtle meditative absorption is to be set and meditated on, he said:

At the heart, and at the door of Brahmā,
And on the very path of the door of the drop,

Here, "at the heart" is at the heart center. "The door of Brahmā" is at the crown of the head. "On the very path of the nose of the drop" is the throat. This is because it says that one should meditate on the subtle meditative absorption there.

Through the guru's scripture alone,
Consciousness and wisdom are to be grasped.

The sense is that the syllable hūṃ of consciousness and wisdom, and its measure, are to be known through the guru's instruction alone. Teaching that this subtle meditative absorption is attained only through the guru's instruction, and not through other outer paths and the like, the Teacher said:

Not by an outer path:
Even through treatises it is far away.

If one asks why, he said:

Treatises are easy to find,
But their purpose is hard to find.

If one asks how, then, it is to be known, he said, in the passage beginning:

By the yogin skilled in method,

Now, teaching the method of mantra recitation, he said, in the passage beginning:

Phenomena have mind as their forerunner,

Here, "mind" is the mind that is the support of mental consciousness. It means that mind is the origin of all phenomena. That is, among body, speech, and mind, mental action comes first, because body and speech act after the mind has intended. Thus it is also said in the Treasury:

Intention is mental action;
Produced by it are the actions of body and speech.

Mind pure, and mind going,

means it is the pure mind, not body and speech. That is, the meaning is that for the yogin whose mind is pure, even if the actions of body and speech are not pure, it does not become a fault. Thus it is also said:

Whether the thought of a bodhisattva
Is virtuous or nonvirtuous,
All of it turns into virtue,
Because the mind becomes the master.

If one asks why, it is "mind going": because mind radiates out to the habitual tendencies of virtue and nonvirtue, and mind goes to nirvāṇa.

Therefore mind, in all things,

The sense is that it is the entity of body, speech, and mind. Therefore it means that the intelligent yogin should apply himself with diligence to purifying the mind.

Having taught that mind is principal among body, speech, and mind, he teaches the point that there is nothing other than mind:

If the yogin wishes to recite,
It is abandoning the thought of the heart.

The sense is that once the yogin has abandoned the mind, there is no recitation elsewhere, because the actions of body and speech are form itself. If one asks why: "The seed is for the sake of the cause." In reality, even the mantra of action performed from form and the rest is seen ultimately as syllables, because of dependent origination. That is, in saying the words "so-and-so śāntiṃ kuru," when one has only said "so-and-so," the sound of śāntiṃ kuru has not arisen. When one says śīntiṃ kuru, "so-and-so" has already gone. Again, when one has only said "che," "ge" has not arisen; when one says "ge," "che" has gone. Likewise, when one has only said those two, "mo" is not there; when one says "mo," "che ge" has gone. Therefore even the mantra used for an activity is merely conceived as dependently originated, and is not so ultimately.

Having thus taught the nature of mantra, he teaches the method of mantra recitation:

The enjoyments of existence, form and the rest:
Recite the seed and apply it.

This means that one recites with the syllable hūṃ, focusing on form and the rest as correct conventional reality, and by that accumulation of merit one should bring about the result of enjoyments within existence in this life.

If one asks what mantra is to be recited, he said, in the passage beginning:

Honored by each and every one,

Here, "honored by each and every one" is the crescent and the dot, whose nature is Vairocana, because they are the utterly pure expanse of phenomena itself, wisdom itself.

Likewise, abiding at the crown,

means that the crescent and the dot are always to be placed above the head of hūṃ.

Always making a point at the root,

means that the vowel sign is always attached below. Showing just those two points:

With the drop and the nāda,

If one asks what such a seed is, he said:

Wisdom and consciousness are the seed itself,

that is, the syllable hūṃ. Showing that even if one recites mantra for the sake of the enjoyments of saṃsāra, one should recite it with the mind alone, without sound or words, he said:

Partaking of the enjoyments of saṃsāra,
Having given up sound, soundless,
One should engage in mental recitation.

Then, wishing to show the eight signs, the Blessed One said the passage beginning:

Unmoving, always abiding in means,

Here, "unmoving" is because it is without destruction. "Always" is because it is unceasing. "Abiding in means" is because, by those two, it has the nature of abiding in means. "Permanent" is because it is the very cause of the Tathāgatas. "The expanse of phenomena itself" is because it is the cause of the noble Dharma. "Continuously subtle" is because even in the five later instants it is beyond the senses. "Unstained" means without afflictions, because it is luminous by nature.

If one asks what that mind is which is endowed with such qualities, he said:

It makes wisdom and consciousness dissolve,

because method and wisdom are nondual. Saying that with that mind one sees the path of awakening, he said:

Through consciousness that is wisdom in reality,
One comes to see the stages of awakening.

He spoke of the eight that are signs of the path of awakening, in the passage beginning "Like a lamp, everywhere." Here,

Like a lamp, everywhere,
Is the seeing of the first sign,

means that at the first wisdom, the yogin's unerring wisdom, which burns up the afflictions, blazes like a lamp, because it is small.

Appearing like the fire of a firefly,
The sign of the second is examined,

means that at the second wisdom it flickers and sparks like a firefly, because it is middling.

White particles, and other sparks,

means that through the third wisdom the yogin sees the fire of wisdom radiating white in all directions, because it is great. The moment one sees this, the obscuration of afflictions is purified, one becomes a noble one, and one realizes the path of seeing.

Then he spoke of the signs of having abandoned the cognitive obscuration:

The enjoyments of the gods of desire
Are seen by the mind in the fourth,

which means that through the fourth wisdom the yogin is freed from attachment to the five objects of desire of the desire realm by way of the nine grades of great, small and middling.

The gods of the heavens of form as well
Will be seen by the mind in the fifth,

which means that through the fifth wisdom one is freed from attachment to existence in the four meditative concentrations of the form realm by way of the nine grades of great, small and middling.

The sixth is the formless enjoyments,

means that through the sixth wisdom one is freed from attachment to existence in the four formless realms.

In the seventh, the expanse of phenomena,

means that through the seventh wisdom, the yogin who is free from attachment to the three realms abandons all obscurations, abides in nirvāṇa, the fully accomplished Dharma body, and becomes free of apprehended and apprehender.

In the eighth, freely, at will,
The yogin attains the fruit of buddhahood,

means that through the eighth wisdom the yogin who has thus actualized the Dharma body, by the power of his former aspirations and of compassion, for the sake of sentient beings, within the nonabiding nirvāṇa, takes as his focus the enjoyment body or the emanation body of pure mundane wisdom, and at will brings about the fruit of benefit for sentient beings.

Having thus spoken of the distinctions of wisdom, he said:

Through the distinctions of the yogin,
The earlier and the later are refuted.

Now, wishing to show the reality of the stupa, the Blessed One said the passage beginning "By one who has the form of correct view." Here,

By one who has the form of correct view,

"one who has form" is a sentient being; it means that first one meditates on emptiness, the true meaning. Why is that?

Knowing correctly that very reality,
One diligently undertakes the practice,

which means that if one does not know the meaning of true emptiness, meditation, application and the rest are faulty.

Then, showing the method of meditating on emptiness, the Blessed One said the passage beginning "Having imagined a moon at the heart." This means that, having meditated at the heart on the syllable hūṃ with its crescent, one meditates by its light rays that the aggregates and elements are empty.

Showing the conclusion of the meditative absorption of emptiness, the Teacher said:

At the end, the seed of the moon
Should be meditated on by the wise.

That is, the yogin who has meditated that the aggregates, the elements and all things are empty of intrinsic nature should, at the end, meditate on the seed, the syllable hūṃ, upon a moon maṇḍala.

He spoke of how the stupa is to be generated from that seed, in the passage beginning:

What is generated there by the mind
Are the places where the stupa abides,

If one asks how that stupa is to be meditated on, he said, "It is called by the curving of the vowel sign below." This means that the curving vowel sign below the syllable hūṃ is to be known as the eight-cornered base of the stupa, because the syllable hūṃ and the stupa are inseparable.

It is the earth, the realm of the eight directions,

means that, as the nature of the eight-cornered base, one should meditate on the gods of the world-realms of the continents of the eight directions.

The bell, abiding in the middle of space,
As the very gods of the abode of Meru,

means that the nature of the vase of the world is Mount Meru, and one should meditate on the gods from the Heaven of the Thirty-Three downward abiding there.

The gods who partake of desire
Should surely be imagined in the harmikā,

means that the nature of the four, the shape of the stupa's harmikā and the rest, is Yāma and the others; one should meditate on the four classes of desire-realm gods abiding there.

Abiding in the center of the selfless Dharma,
Above and below there is no limit,

means that above that there is no connection to any limit; it is simply that the nature of selflessness rests upon the central axis pole.

If one asks what those are, he said:

The heavens of the form realm
Adorn the wheels,

That is, the nature of the stupa's seventeen wheels is the seventeen abodes of the classes of gods of the form realm, the Brahmakāyika heaven and the rest; one should meditate on the gods of form there. Making just that meaning clear, he said:

Those of form, the seventeen,
Abide in their proper order.

Showing the nature of the formless realm, he said:

All formless existence is seen
To dissolve into the very axis pole of the Dharma.

This means that in the nature of the axis pole, which rises above the seventeen wheels, one should meditate on the formless realm, Infinite Space and the rest.

Generating the stupa as the body,
All is the abode of the gods,

means that, having thus meditated on the form of the stupa as having the nature of the three realms, one meditates on the abodes of the gods of the three realms.

On that egg-born seat above,
One should imagine one's own mind.

Here "egg" is the birthplace of the mind, like the heart; it means one should imagine one's own mind abiding in that round part at the top of the stupa.

Through the inseparability of mind and wisdom,
By the distinction of nondual equality,

If one asks in what one meditates on equality and inseparability:

In the middle of form and emptiness,
Likewise, of entity and non-entity.

Here "form" is grasping outer and inner entities as a self, or else it is the form of the stupa. "Emptiness" is the view that from the very beginning things are cut off from the causes of virtue and non-virtue. It means the equality in which those two are absent. Then it means that one should meditate that all phenomena are neither entities nor non-entities.

Showing the distinction of that mind of equality, he said:

Beyond apprehended, apprehender and the rest,

Here the apprehended is the field of activity, and the apprehender is the sense faculties and so forth. The words "and the rest" refer to one and many and so forth. The mind that has abandoned those has gone beyond.

Neither doing good nor wrong,

Here "good" is virtue and "wrong" is non-virtue; it means the mind that has abandoned both of these as well.

In the middle of that, the stability is attained
That marks the characteristic of entity,

These are form, emptiness and so forth. It says that one should meditate on and mark the characteristic of that, having attained the mind of equality in the middle.

He said that, having meditated thus, one should worship the stupa with offerings of generosity and the rest, and meditate, in the passage beginning:

By generosity, moral discipline, patience and the rest,

Wishing to show the qualities of meditating on that stupa of the expanse of phenomena, he said the passage beginning:

The essence of the expanse of phenomena,

Now Vajrapāṇi, wishing to ask the Blessed One about uniting the expanse and wisdom, made the request beginning:

Of the characteristic of secret union,

Here, "secret" is the expanse and wisdom. "Expanse" is the four elements; "wisdom" is the formless. He asked for the method of uniting those two.

That reality, reality supreme, that reality,

is so because it is the supreme reality of body, speech and mind.

Then, in answer, comes the passage beginning "The Blessed One declared." Here, "Expanse: first, for the time being, the yogin acts freely at will" means that in the way of the Vajrayāna the yogin should tame his own mind naturally, and not through austerities. Showing this by an example:

That mind, which is like a wild horse,
Should be watched by the wise,

This means that just as one skillfully trains a wild horse to the reins until it is tamed, so the mind that engages in what is inferior should also be turned by the reins from perverse fields of activity and made to engage in correct engagement.

Again he gave the example and the meaning of how it is tamed:

Just as a bee, drunk with intoxication from the flowers of the season, has its mind held steady,
So the mind, distracted by enjoyments and bearing the seeds of ripening,
Should likewise be drawn out of craving and restrained.

This means that just as a bee attached to the base of a lotus stalk, when the sun has gone, is enclosed by the lotus petals without noticing it in its mind, so the afflicted mind too, having given up austerities, is allowed to be distracted by enjoyments; and then that mind, united with true wisdom free of dualistic view, should be placed in nirvāṇa.

If one asks why, when in the Vinaya, in the Prātimokṣa, it is taught that sentient beings are tamed through austerity by way of the seven characteristics of abandonment, here it is taught that the mind is tamed by way of the mind, he said the passage beginning:

By restraining the mind, accomplishment upon accomplishment,

and its meaning has already been explained above.

He spoke of how the yogin whose mind has thus come under control meditates on the goddess, in the passage beginning:

Abiding in the middle of the moon maṇḍala,

Here, "the great mudrā at the heart" is the mudrā of supreme awakening. The passage beginning:

Of the stupa of the five proclaimers,

means that one should protect the throat with the syllable yu.

With the blazing syllable of fire,
Likewise, the two, water and non-water,

means that with the syllable kṣūṃ one should protect the male organ and the passage from which excrement comes. From "the plantain, the flower, the lotus" at the start up to "will practice extensively for a long time," the opening of the plantain flower has already been explained above.

In its middle, an egg,

means that the egg in the middle of the plantain is the heart, because it resembles an egg. He spoke of its color:

White as jasmine, the color of the moon.

Then, placing the seeds in the center of the egg, he said:

In direction after direction, the seeds of the directions,

that is, placing the four seeds of the directions. If one asks which seeds:

In the two directions are the four elements,

which means one should place sruṃ kruṃ yuṃ huṃ, the seeds of the elements of water, fire, wind and earth, and not the wisdom seeds a ā aṃ aḥ.

The four stacked seeds in the intermediate directions,

means that here too one should place in the four intermediate directions the stacked syllables that have the nature of the elements, smryuṃ hmryuṃ ymryuṃ kṣmruṃ, and not the neuter syllables ṛ ṝ and ḷ ḹ.

Focusing on that very reality in the center,
The essential nature of the wisdom mind,

means that in the center of those eight syllables one meditates on hūṃ, the seed of wisdom and consciousness. He spoke of the light and color of those syllables:

White and pure, with even light,
One should meditate on the syllables of the goddesses.

Then, wishing to show the method of uniting the expanse and wisdom, he said:

Meditating on the white silk of the mind,
One should then dissolve the forms,
Dissolving them in direction after direction,
And likewise in the intermediate directions.

That is, the yogin who has thus freely meditated on the nine syllables should dissolve the eight syllables that are the nature of the forms of the directions and intermediate directions, and meditate that they gather into the central syllable hūṃ. Making those two points clear, he said:

Having dissolved all forms,
Wisdom and consciousness remain,

That is, having dissolved the form syllables of all eight directions, one meditates that only the single syllable hūṃ of wisdom and consciousness remains. He explained in particular the color and nature of that hūṃ the moment they have gathered:

Water is in the eastern direction,
White in color, the hue of light.

This means that because the syllable is joined with the seed of water, it takes on the nature of water and a white color.

Fire, blazing with yellow light,
Is always in the north,

means that because the fire seed kṣuṃ dissolves into the northern side of hūṃ, it has the nature of fire and a yellow color.

Wind is black in color;
The one with the form of space is in the west.

Here, "the one with the form of space" is hūṃ, because it has the nature of formless wisdom. Because the wind seed yuṃ dissolves there into its western side, it has the nature of wind and a black color.

The southern direction as red
One should imagine for the southern direction.

On this: because the seed hūṃ dissolves into the southern side of hūṃ, it has the nature of earth and a red color. The interchange of the colors of these two is because it depends on latent tendencies.

With colors that lead into the intermediate directions,
One should imagine the four corners.

This means the colors are mixed at the four corners of hūṃ, as follows: the quarter of fire, the southeast, is a color mixing white and red; the quarter of Īśāna, the northeast, is a color mixing white and yellow; the quarter of wind, the northwest, is a color mixing yellow and black; and the quarter of Nairṛti, the southwest, is a color mixing red and black.

Then, having shown the union of the expanse and wisdom, and wishing to show the union of that wisdom too with the nondual mind, the Blessed One said:

The remaining consciousness, in the center,
Should be dissolved together with the egg-born.

Here, "the egg-born" is the nondual mind. The meaning is this: the syllable hūṃ of consciousness should be mixed with the syllable that is the nondual mind and made to enter it; one should meditate on nonduality.

If one asks what, then, the characteristic of that hūṃ is like, he said:

The characteristic that marks it,
The characteristic of marking, is formless.

Showing its meaning clearly, he further said the passage beginning:

Since the entity of the mind is empty,

As to how such emptiness appears by nature as form:

In the middle of nonconceptual emptiness,
Just a tiny particle, a spark.

That is, the middle of emptiness, in which self and other are inseparable, is like a spark: when it appears as form, it is like a spark that is gone the moment it falls into open space.

The seed of the vowels and consonants,
Abiding in the center of the eye of the atom,

means that in the atom abides the nature of the syllable ā, the seed of the vowels and consonants, because it is by nature unborn from the very beginning. Therefore it is said of the arising of all phenomena: a kā ro mu khaṃ sarba dha rmā ṇāṃ ārya nutpanda tvāt.

By forms that are free of names,
All, every one, is called an atom.

This means that, being beyond the senses, no perception of form arises; all phenomena, being beyond names, are held to have the nature of atoms, because they are nirvāṇa.

Therefore, showing that the meditative absorption uniting the expanse and wisdom is neither existent nor nonexistent, he spoke the passage beginning "Nor is it apprehended as empty," and gave as its example:

Not wind, and not space either:
One should examine it evenly, in the middle.

That is, for example, moving wind, which has form, is in space, and space is emptiness that does not obstruct the wind. Just as space and wind are inseparable, so too form and emptiness are inseparable: whatever is form is not that nature of emptiness, and whatever is emptiness is not that nature of form, and yet they do not exist apart. It means one should view them as equal. Thus Nāgārjunapāda, speaking of the emptiness of the Perfection of Wisdom, said:

Apart from entities there is no emptiness;
Apart from emptiness there are no entities.
Dependent arising is emptiness:
This, O Lord, is what you have taught.

If one asks how it is to be realized, he said the passage beginning:

The guru's instruction is realization.

Then he spoke of the conduct and the fruit of the yogin who has actualized the expanse and wisdom, in the passage beginning "The yogin accomplished in wisdom." There,

Bodies and minds of thorough conceptualization,
If one wishes to protect the realm,

Here "the realm" is the aggregates and elements. It means that if he wishes to generate aggregates, the yogin, conceiving a mind for the sake of sentient beings, should generate aggregates for the sake of sentient beings.

The enjoyments of the Lord of Wisdom,
The accomplishments, are in the body at will,

means that a yogin who has attained such wisdom, if he wishes, attains the fruit of the lower vehicle's ascetic practice, the fruit of stream-entry, by having passed through all five, the path of seeing and the path of meditation.

Seeing the wisdom of the three realms,

is the arhat, because he is free from attachment to the three realms. This means that a yogin who has attained nondual wisdom, if he wishes, attains the fruit of an arhat. Since the first fruit and the last fruit are spoken of, he also attains the fruit of the once-returner and the fruit of the non-returner; this must be understood as implied. It means that the yogin attains these two as well, if he wishes.

By the mind, freely, in their abodes,
Those with form who move through the sky,

means that the yogin who has attained nondual wisdom enjoys whatever various fruits, mundane and supramundane, he wishes, just as those with form move unobstructed through the sky.

Having shown the fruit that is one's own benefit, he spoke of the fruit that is the benefit of others:

To sentient beings, whatever they wish,
He will swiftly grant the supreme.

If one asks how he works for the benefit of sentient beings, he said:

Body, faculties and desires
He freely makes selfless.

That is, in working for the benefit of sentient beings, the yogin engages the body, the faculties and the rest in a manner that focuses on self and other.

Showing that it is only the yogin who realizes the expanse of phenomena who passes into nirvāṇa, and not anyone else, he said the passage beginning:

Through the essence of the expanse of phenomena,

Showing the places for the yogin who meditates on these, he said the passage beginning:

Mountains, mountain retreats, and forests,

Then, showing that the yogin who wishes to meditate on the meaning should accomplish it after revitalizing his unfit body with an elixir, he said the passage beginning:

na ga mu la pa la śa,

Here, na ga mu la is a special kind of gift, and pa la śa is the flower of the lac tree.

Prepared with fire and butter, in equal portions,

means that the medicinal ingredients are made equal in three portions. If one asks how much each is, he said, "Twelve palas of Magadha," meaning that one should combine twelve of the large palas of Magadha, the land near Vajrāsana, for each of all three. If one asks how it is to be taken, he said the passage beginning:

In boiled cow's milk,

He spoke of the qualities of the medicine in the passage beginning:

For a hundred years, the yogin's,

Here, "guarding discipline and enjoying" means that while accomplishing that medicine one should guard the body from sexual desire, from harmful food, and from alcohol.

He gave an example showing that when the unfit body is mastered by medicine it becomes a cause of what is correct, in the passage beginning:

Just as a young girl, beguiled by words,

The meaning is this: just as a girl who is afraid to engage in desire is enticed by talk and then engages in it, so too, once the revitalization of the unfit body by the elixir has been accomplished, the body is enticed and should be made to enter the correct path.

Now, the meaning of the sixteen chapters having been understood, that whole retinue, Vajrapāṇi and the rest, delighted and gratified, offered praise to the Blessed One Vajrasattva, in the passage beginning, "Then yoga and."

Vajrasattva, lord of great compassion, wishing to show that retinue, Vajrapāṇi and the rest, the great qualities of the yoga treatise, the Catuṣpīṭha, spoke the passage beginning, "Listen, Vajra, how."

Here, "how many lines" means a quarter of a verse. "Heaven" means the six classes of desire gods, the Four Great Kings and the rest, and the seventeen of the form realm, Brahmā and the rest.

Or else, the wheel, the blazing wheel,

means the golden wheel-turning king and the rest.

The mind of formless existence is accomplished,

means the sphere of Infinite Space and the rest, the four formless realms.

Perfect awakening is swiftly attained,

means actualizing the state of Vajrasattva, nonabiding nirvāṇa.

Through the master who understood the meaning of the tantra
Composing this commentary on the secret key points,
Whatever merit I have gained,
May all sentient beings obtain it.

The Indian preceptor Smṛtijñānakīrti, having received the instructions from his guru, composed this explanation of the Secret Seat from the Catuṣpīṭha, and translated, edited and finalized it. It is complete.

Translator's notes

The opening verse of homage is highly compressed and its imagery is obscure; the rendering is tentative. The requester, "Armor of Virtue," cannot be identified with confidence and is translated by meaning. The twelve names used for the zodiacal squares (the Dripper, the Thick One, Fear, Desire) and the bodhisattva "Keen Wisdom" are rendered literally since their Sanskrit equivalents are uncertain. The commentary plays on "one with r" (ra can) and "horned one" (rwa can), which sound alike in Tibetan; in the second list "Fear" replaces "the Dripper," and a stray "succeeds" precedes it, apparently a copyist's repetition. The line about Subhūti is quoted from the root text and is too terse to construe with confidence. In the root verses, "twelve" and "thirteen" with "multiplied" or "fold" evidently stand for two tens and three tens, as the commentary's figures show (twenty-six, thirty-four, and so on); the root lines are translated as the Tibetan gives them. bhiśu is the equinox (viṣuvat), when day and night are equal. "From the two courses one should make two cognitions" is literal and its sense is unclear. "Lords of speech" renders ngag gi dbang phyug (Skt. vāgīśvara) literally; in this divination it evidently denotes the syllables or letters of the questioner's utterance that are counted. The opening quotation is written dag gi dbang phyug, an evident slip for ngag gi dbang phyug, as the lemma is quoted later in the passage; likewise dag mi dge near the end of the first chapter is read as dge mi dge, "virtuous or non-virtuous". "The twelve places" and "divided by the places" render sa, here the cells of the diagram, not the bodhisattva grounds; the commentary itself glosses the division as by twelves. 'Du byed is rendered "preparatory procedures" (the ritual preparations of the ground), and chad pa as "cutting off" of the breath, since the glossary senses ("formations", "nihilism") do not fit this context. Rdo rgyus is taken as chalk or a powder line used for drawing. The commentary's list of the outer measures passes from the sixth cell to the eighth, with no entry for the seventh; this is translated as it stands. Āṃbara, trota and udum are Sanskrit or Prakrit terms transliterated in the root verses for points or channels of the eye and ear; they are left untranslated. The phrase rendered "the inner part of the nose" (sna yi nang ka) is uncertain. Seng ge'i snod, "lion vessel", is read as steng gi snod, "upper vessel", which the context plainly requires. The animal-faced goddesses (the Lioness, the Tigress, the Jackal, the Owl) are given in English because their Sanskrit names in this tantra cannot be fixed with confidence. The root line rendered "the vajra hand on the side that is reached in examination" is obscure; the commentary identifies it with the subscript foot stroke of hūṃ. The "sound of homage" is spelled simply o in the Tibetan, though the mantra given later begins with oṃ. "Nopaki of the maṇḍala" transliterates the Tibetan as it stands; it probably reflects a Sanskrit title in -upāyikā (a maṇḍala manual), but the work cannot be identified with confidence. The names of the goddesses of the neuter vowels (the Blazing One, the Sucking One, the Robbing One) are given in English, since their Sanskrit forms cannot be fixed. Dbang por, "for Indra", in the line on placing oṃ first is read as dang por, "first", which the parallel "at the end" requires. In the Nairṛti corner the Tibetan writes e twice, where ai would be expected; the letters are given as spelled. "Bitten" renders zos pa, literally "eaten", used here of snakebite or poisoning. The place rendered "the upper back" (thong ka) is uncertain. The nāga in the Wind quarter, transliterated Lindaka, cannot be identified with confidence; Karkoṭa is the standard Sanskrit for stobs kyi rgyu and Kulika for rigs ldan. The phrase rendered "one should imagine a torma for the wisdom ḍākinī" (gtor ma ye shes mkha' 'gro ma) is terse and might instead mean that the drawn-out poison is cast into the water maṇḍala as an offering to her. The bhandha she holds is presumably a skull bowl. The application statement gives as its example a form of the mantra without a name, though it speaks of the name being inserted; it is translated as it stands. The opening clause about Vajrapāṇi (gzhan gyis gdon gyis bcings pa'i thugs kyis) is syntactically difficult; it has been taken to mean that the Blessed One understood Vajrapāṇi's concern for those bound by grahas sent by others. "Crest" for sgro, where the two nāga heads are placed, is uncertain. Excellent Victor (rgyal ba bzang po) and the eight cloud kings are given in English, as their Sanskrit names cannot be fixed with confidence; Agatsar is a transliteration of an unidentified name, and Hulunta, the nāga of the Wind quarter, is spelled as in the Tibetan (an earlier passage gave Lindaka for this position). Dus pa at the start of the nāga line is read as "then". The rendering of several root-verse lines quoted only in fragments (for example "the arising of the later application" and "the side that has gone") is tentative, as their sense depends on the root tantra. Among the eight rites, "filling" renders dgang ba literally; it may be a slip for dgug pa, "summoning." The listed accomplishments (sword, underworld, vetāla, invisibility ointment, bezoar, etc.) follow the standard list of mundane siddhis; "the stars" and "the cloth" are literal for skar ma and snam bu. "Regulating one's food" is an interpretive rendering of zas spags pa. "The u written beneath" renders zhabs skyed, taken as the subscript vowel sign zhabs kyu. The commentary spells out two encoded mantras letter by letter; the root-text lines it glosses are known only from these fragments, so their wording here is tentative. "The Mare" (rta rgod ma) is taken as Vaḍavā, the mare whose mouth is the world-ending fire (vaḍavāmukha), which matches the mantra and the burning of rain clouds. "The wrathful" finger is glossed by the commentary as the index finger. "The Seat of Others" renders gzhan gyis gdan, read as gzhan gyi gdan, the name of the next section of the tantra. The four goddesses set in the directions (sems ma rdo rje ma, rin chen rdo rje ma, chos kyi rdo rje ma, las kyi rdo rje ma) are given their Sanskrit family names Sattvavajrī, Ratnavajrī, Dharmavajrī and Karmavajrī; rdo rje sems ma is rendered Vajrasattvī. In the Kailāsa measurement, bsregs ma thag tu ("as soon as burned") makes little sense and is read as bsres ("combined"). "Having brought forth the bodhisattva" follows the Tibetan literally; it may intend generating the awakening mind. The several dance movements and stances named at the four doors are rendered literally; sa ga is read as the vaiśākha stance (sa ga being the Tibetan name of the Viśākhā asterism) and "the circular stance" as the maṇḍala stance. The geometry of the maṇḍala grid is terse and rendered closely to the wording. "Complete the fence" renders ra ba smin par, literally "ripen the fence." "The place of the dance" renders gar gyi phyogs; the sense is uncertain. Most of the goddesses of the outer maṇḍala and the lords of the charnel grounds are given by Tibetan descriptive names whose Sanskrit originals cannot be fixed with confidence here, so they are translated by meaning (the Frowning One, the Blood Drinker, the Vase Lady, the Lute Lady, the Gnasher, Beyond Joy and so on); gtum mo, drag mo and sha za ma are given as Caṇḍī, Raudrī and Piśācī. "The five hooks" (lcags kyu lnga) is literal; it probably refers to a coded set of ritual substances alongside the five nectars. "The third, with the curved stroke added" (gug skyed gsum pa) is a tentative rendering of a coded description of the vowel signs. Many of the retinue names here are translated Tibetan forms whose Sanskrit originals I cannot securely identify; these are rendered literally: the Ager, the Fierce Black Lady, the Subjugating Lady, the Moon Lady, the Fierce Hating One, the Flame Lady (literally "fire-butter," perhaps an inversion of "lamp"), the Fierce-Formed, the Great-Formed, the Fang-Gnashing Lady, the Sword Lady, the Bow-Handed Lady, the Upholder, the God of the Seventh, the Lord of Heat, the Lord of the Last Scattering, the One Who Sets the Hand Drum Behind, and others.
