About this text

The Dhāraṇī of the Noble Lady, the Invincible One with the White Parasol, Who Emerged from the Crown of the Tathāgata, known in Sanskrit as the Āryatathāgatoṣṇīṣasitātapatrāparājitā nāma dhāraṇī and in Tibetan as Phags ma de bzhin gshegs pa'i gtsug tor nas byung ba'i gdugs dkar po can gzhan gyis mi thub pa zhes bya ba'i gzungs, is a dhāraṇī text centered on Sitātapatrā, the protective goddess of the White Parasol who arises from the crown protuberance of the Buddha. It opens with an extended series of homages to the Three Jewels, to countless buddhas and their communities of hearers, to the noble ones at each stage of the path, to accomplished sages and vidyādharas, and to worldly deities such as Brahmā. These homages set the stage for a powerful incantation invoked to avert harm, repel hostile forces, and grant protection. It is Toh 986 in the Kangyur, where it belongs to the class of dhāraṇī texts, the collections of protective and ritual spells within the tantric portion of the canon.

Translator's notes

"Lord of Umā": the Tibetan literally says "lord of the one who turned back austerities." This is the Tibetan etymological rendering of Umā's name. The homage is to Rudra (Śiva) and his companions. The last buddha in the list (literally "Wide-Eyed King of the Banner of Utpala Fragrance") could not be securely matched to a Sanskrit name, so the meaning is translated. Several goddess names in the verses are rendered from their Tibetan meaning because the Sanskrit form is uncertain: "Vajra Voice," "Safflower Lady," "Jewel Lady," "Yawning Lady," "Lotus Eye," "Moonlight," "Vajra Maiden," and "Vajra Chain." "The graha of Vāyu" renders rlung lha'i gdon; the Sanskrit parallel likely reads marut-graha. In the first graha list the text reads གྲུལ་བུལ. This is evidently a slip for གྲུལ་བུམ (kumbhāṇḍa), as the later list and the dhāraṇī have it, and it is translated accordingly. The spirit classes skanda, apasmāra, unmāda, chāyā, ostāraka and Revatī are identified from their Tibetan renderings and from the parallel Sanskrit sequence in the dhāraṇī itself. Their Tibetan names mean roughly "one who causes withering," "one who causes forgetfulness," "one who causes madness," "shadow-oppressor," and "oppressor." The rendering ནམ་མཁའ་ལྡིང་ཡང་དག་པ་དང་ལྷན་ཅིག་པ ("the true garuḍa together with his companions") is uncertain. The two graha names at the start, "the grahas that cling all about" and "the grahas that draw out thorns," are translated from the Tibetan wording. Their Sanskrit equivalents could not be identified with confidence. Several disease terms in the list of afflictions, for example the words rendered "abscesses," "erysipelas," and "carbuncles," are traditional Tibetan medical terms whose exact modern equivalents are uncertain. The phrase rendered "one who has not kept ascetic observances" is literally "one who has not made able." It is understood here in light of the parallel Sanskrit formula akṛtavrata, "one who has not kept vows." "Calamity" renders the Tibetan mi ngas, whose precise sense is unclear. The number of the vajra family uses khrag khrig, a very large numeral, here rendered loosely as "myriads."
