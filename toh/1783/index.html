About this text

The Clear Realization of Acala, Entitled Utter Delight, known in Sanskrit as Acalābhisamaya Suratābhidhāna and in Tibetan as Mi gyowé ngönpar tokpa rabtu gawar ngönpar jöpa, is a tantric means of accomplishment (sādhana) devoted to Acala in his fierce form as Caṇḍamahāroṣaṇa, the Great Wrathful One. Drawing on its root tantra and other treatises, it guides a properly initiated practitioner through visualizing the deity and his consorts, the three samādhis, the recitation of the root and garland mantras, a subtle yoga of dissolution into great bliss, and the offering of torma, closing with verses on the free and fearless conduct of the accomplished yogin. Its colophon names the Indian master Vanaratna as its author and records that it was translated into Tibetan by the monk Shönu Pal. It is Toh 1783 in the Tengyur, where it belongs among the commentaries and ritual works of the tantra section.

Translation

In the language of India: acalābhisamayaḥ suratābhidhānaḥ. In the language of Tibet: The Clear Realization of Acala, Entitled Utter Delight.

Homage to the glorious Great Fierce Wrathful One.

Having bowed to the glorious and mighty host of heroes,
from what I have seen in its own tantra and in other treatises,
the means of accomplishment called the Glorious Great Wrathful One,
drawn out from them, shall here be written.

First of all, the mantrin who wishes to make the three bodies of all the buddhas directly manifest in this very life; who has trained in the true perfections; who, having pleased the vajra master, has entered the maṇḍala and received empowerment and been properly authorized; who, having heard the tantra, is skilled in the reality of union; and who, with a mind of great compassion striving for the benefit of all sentient beings, wishes to meditate on the glorious Great Wrathful Fierce One, should sit on a comfortable seat in a place agreeable to the mind and free from all harm. Or else, that on which one sits by means of the vajra or the maṇḍala of emission is the seat, namely the body of the supreme wisdom consort.

Seated upon it, one should then place a pill of the five nectars in the mouth and meditate on the four abodes of Brahmā. Of these, the attitude of cherishing all sentient beings like an only child and bringing about their happiness is great loving-kindness. The nature that wishes to draw out from suffering and the causes of suffering all sentient beings, who are sunk in the ocean of conceptual thought and are without refuge, is great compassion. The characteristic of being fully satisfied through possessing the common and the supreme bliss that come from the teaching of the most exalted means of accomplishing Acala is joy. The attitude of practicing the path as the antidote to the eight worldly concerns is equanimity.

Then the yogin, as Acala himself, with the color, form and shape that will be explained, at his own heart, on a lotus, a moon and a sun, has a blue syllable hūṃ. With the rays of the five wisdoms radiating from it, he illuminates the limitless world systems and invites the Blessed One together with his retinue, the gurus and the bodhisattvas into the sky in front. Looking upon that assembly, he worships them with the yoginīs who abide in his wheel, Mohavajrī and the others, holding in their hands offering substances arisen from the seed syllable at his own heart and from the samādhi of the sky treasury. Then he recites the verses of the unsurpassed sevenfold worship:

Whatever misdeeds I have committed,
against precepts or by their very nature,
and whatever done by others I have rejoiced in,
all of these I confess.
I rejoice in the merit
gathered by the buddhas, their heirs and the noble ones.
I go for refuge to the buddhas,
together with the supreme Dharma and Assembly.
Proclaiming the voice that teaches the glorious and lovely Dharma,
please remain!
We offer, above all,
our own bodies.
Whatever merit has been gathered,
I dedicate to perfect awakening.
Having become the glorious Lord of the Fierce,
I shall bring benefit to beings.

Then, bowing with deep faith to the body, speech and mind of the Blessed One and his retinue, and with intense devotion to the nature of samādhi, one should recite the mantra oṃ śūnyatā jñāna vajra svabhāva ātmako'haṃ. This is the yoga.

Then one should see oneself, abiding in the sphere of space, free of flesh, bone, blood and the rest, as Vajrasattva, like a mass of white light rays, greatly peaceful, with one face and two arms, the hands in the lotus mudrā of samādhi, with a crown of matted locks, bearing the thirty-two major marks and beautified by the eighty minor marks, seated in the vajra posture of the hero on a white lotus and a moon seat. The tantra also says:

Like a stainless, clear crystal,
one should meditate on one's own body.

This is the subsequent yoga.

Then, at the heart of oneself, now stable as Vajrasattva, in the middle of a sun and moon joined face to face, that blue hūṃ transforms, and from it comes a vajra marked with hūṃ. Imagining all of these, the sun and the rest, as a reflection of the moon, one sees for an instant in front of oneself the glorious Lord of the Fierce, who will be described, emerging from it in an utterly wrathful form. Then, absorbing him once again into oneself, one should meditate on oneself as just such a Lord of the Fierce, utterly fierce. The tantra also says:

Arisen from the reflection of the being,
meditate on the lord of the maṇḍala.

Then, in a wrathful mood, one terrifies all the hosts of vicious obstructors with the sword, uttering hūṃ hūṃ traṭkhedaya hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ traṭkhedaya bho. One binds their necks with the vajra noose held in the left hand, and with the sword held in the right hand, saying hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ phaṭ, like a crashing cymbal in one's frenzy, one roars the sound hā hā at the hosts of obstructors. Some read "flesh."

Then, with a mind free of obstructors and full of joy, one imagines on the crowns of the obstructors' heads kīla pegs, born from the vajra noose, blazing, flaring, setting ablaze and causing to blaze. One strikes them with a vajra hammer arisen from the sword, so that they pass from the brain down to the secret lotus, and one should recite the mantra of the kīla. Having pegged them down, one should cast them into the wells of the ten directions.

For this, in the east: oṃ caṇḍa mahāroṣaṇa gha gha ghātaya sarva duṣṭendro pondrān na sa parivāra na kīlaya kīlaya hūṃ phaṭ. In the south: oṃ caṇḍa mahāroṣaṇa gha gha ghātaya sarva duṣṭa yamāna sa parivārā na kīlaya kīlaya hūṃ phaṭ. In the west: oṃ caṇḍa mahāroṣaṇa gha gha ghātaya sarva duṣṭā varuṇāna sa parivārā na kīlaya kīlaya hūṃ phaṭ. In the north: oṃ caṇḍa mahāroṣaṇa gha gha ghātaya sarva duṣṭa kuberāna sa parivāra na kīlaya kīlaya hūṃ phaṭ. In the quarter of Fire: oṃ caṇḍa mahāroṣaṇa gha gha ghātaya sarva duṣṭāgnīn sa parivāra na kīlaya kīlaya hūṃ phaṭ. In the quarter of Nairṛti: oṃ caṇḍa mahāroṣaṇa gha gha ghātaya sarva duṣṭa nairṛtīna sa parivāra na kīlaya kīlaya hūṃ phaṭ. In the quarter of Wind: oṃ caṇḍa mahāroṣaṇa gha gha ghātaya sarva duṣṭa vāyūn sa parivāra na kīlaya kīlaya hūṃ phaṭ. In the quarter of Īśāna: oṃ caṇḍa mahāroṣaṇa gha gha ghātaya sarva duṣṭa śānān sa parivāra na kīlaya kīlaya hūṃ phaṭ. Above: oṃ caṇḍa mahāroṣaṇa gha gha ghātaya sarva duṣṭa brahmaṇaḥ sa parivāra na kīlaya kīlaya hūṃ phaṭ. Below: oṃ caṇḍa mahāroṣaṇa gha gha ghātaya sarva duṣṭa vemacitri pṛthvī devatā sa parivāra na kīlaya kīlaya hūṃ phaṭ. So it is.

Knowing that, by striking the kīla pegs in this way, there are no more obstructors, one makes the Lord of the Fierce disappear. Then, having once again become one whose nature is Vajrasattva, one should imagine in front of oneself the maṇḍalas of wind and the rest. There is a maṇḍala of wind arisen from the syllable yaṃ, shaped like a bow, pale blue, and marked with banners. Above it is a maṇḍala of fire born from the syllable raṃ, triangular and red, its corners marked with the syllable raṃ. Above it is a maṇḍala of water born from the syllable vaṃ, round and white, its center marked with a vase. Above it is a maṇḍala of earth born from the syllable laṃ, square and yellow, its corners marked with three-pronged vajras. Above it one imagines Mount Meru, born from the syllable suṃ, made of the four precious substances. Above that, in the heart of a white source of phenomena pervading the whole expanse of space, one should imagine a multi-storied vajra palace born from a yellow bhrūṃ. It arises in dependence; it is by nature the condensed buddha field of utterly pure mind only; it is the city of great liberation, square, with four doors, beautified by eight pillars, encircled by four terraces, adorned with four porticoes, made exceedingly beautiful with jeweled necklaces and half-necklaces, bakula garlands, parapets and music, marked with parasols, canopies, bells, yak-tail whisks, lions and the rest, and adorned with banners stirred by the wind.

At its heart, on the anthers of a variegated eight-petaled lotus born from paṃ, upon a moon born from aṃ and a sun maṇḍala born from raṃ, one sees the Tathāgata Akṣobhya, born from the syllable hūṃ, fully embraced by Māmakī. These then pass in through the crown of the head of Akṣobhya, lord of yogins, who becomes passionately attached to Māmakī. Melted by great passion, they fall as fluid through the avadhūtī, from Akṣobhya's vajra opening into the goddess's lotus, and enter the hollow of the lotus. On the right of the body, it is blessed by the seed hūṃ, and from that it emerges from the lotus in the fully formed shape of Acala. One imagines that, through intense passion for Māmakī herself, it dissolves once more into Akṣobhya. Then one sees oneself as the glorious Leader of the Wheel, with the form, color and shape that will be described, in union with Dveṣavajrī, who is Māmakī transformed. This is the supreme yoga.

Thus, consisting of the three yogas, this is the first samādhi, called the Initial Union.

Then, the yogin whose nature is Acala generates his wisdom consort in the form of Dveṣavajrī, and in order to give her the portions of the five families, he places oṃ hūṃ svā āḥ hā in sequence at the crown, heart, secret place and the pair of thighs, namely the wheel, vajra, jewel, lotus and sword, the seeds of the tathāgatas. After that, on the vajra and the lotus, born from hūṃ and āḥ, he visualizes the anthers born from āḥ and oṃ, and a yellow syllable phaṭ at the mouth of the vajra jewel. Then, setting in motion the channels in the left and right limbs, and also with the finger and the tongue, on whose tips stands a red syllable hrīḥ, with the sound śīt going first, he says śīḥ śīḥ śīḥ oṃ sarva tathāgatānurāgaṇa vajra svabhāva ātmako'haṃ, and so the delight should begin.

In order to generate the wisdom of suchness through bliss, one should properly recognize, step by step, how each of these arises. Here, through embracing, kissing, squeezing the breasts, marking with nails and teeth and so on, there arises from the mere union of lotus and vajra the mind characterized by the moment in which joy dawns. Holding it at the mouth of the wisdom consort, one should properly recognize it as it is, unmoving.

From the intense friction of lotus and vajra, there arises from the awakening mind situated at the tip of the jewel the mind characterized by the moment in which supreme joy arises. Holding it at the heart of the consort, in the middle between her two breasts, one should properly recognize it just as it is, pressed, unmoving, with the breath held still.

Generated from the awakening mind placed at the tip of the jewel is the mind characterized by the moment in which the innate dawns. It is free from appearing as self and other, beyond all activity, by nature utterly suppressing verbal conceptuality, the culmination of self-awareness alone, nondual, its nature the dharma body. Holding it inside the consort's bhaga and properly recognizing it just as it is, at rest, unmoving, with the breath held still, one attains, each in turn, the three bodies of higher bliss and utter bliss.

Experiencing it, and with the awakening mind become unmoving, one merely properly knows, "It has been consumed by these blisses": the mind characterized by the moment in which the special joy dawns. As it moves, the green one should be emanated. In the quarter of Fire is Mohavajrī, appearing white. In the quarter of Nairṛti is Piśunavajrī, like gold.

In the quarter of Wind, the red Rāgavajrī should be emanated. In the quarter of Īśāna, the green Īrṣyāvajrī should be emanated. This is the second samādhi, called the Supreme King of the Maṇḍala.

Then, through the taste of the supreme great bliss utterly transcending existence, self-awareness, together with great passion, melts into a drop and comes to abide in the form of a seed. The tantra also says:

Together with the wisdom consort, he begins to melt.

Mohavajrī and the others, seeing that there is no lord, are amazed and dismayed, thinking, "We have become protectorless!" With vajra songs composed of verses that awaken his mind, they rouse the Leader of the Wheel:

All-pervading lord, do not forsake love
and make all things of the nature of emptiness!
Since, parted from you, I shall die,
do not make all beings empty!

So says Mohavajrī.

Since, parted from a mind of compassion, I shall die,
all-pervading lord, do not make all empty!
Since this body of mine is in suffering,
do not let it be parted from life!

So says Piśunavajrī.

Why, O wise one, do you
abandon joy and enter into emptiness?
All the worlds without exception
and my own mind dwell in you.

So says Rāgavajrī.

Youthful one, your eye of equanimity
is empty and without fruit.
Abandon the very nature of emptiness,
and join with me in equality!

So says Īrṣyāvajrī.

Hearing their words that rouse him,
he wakes as if from a dream,
rises up from the melted form,
and emanates in the aspect of his own deity.

Now, the Blessed One, at the center of a variegated lotus, in the middle of moon and sun maṇḍalas, is in a crooked posture, his left knee planted on the ground and his right foot stretched out to the right. He bears the form of fresh youth of a sixteen-year-old. His body is blue, with one face and two arms. His right hand brandishes a sword; his left hand, with the threatening forefinger, holds a yellow vajra noose at his heart. He wears variegated garments and is adorned with jewel ornaments. He has a crown marked with the Lord of Mind, five locks of hair, and a jeweled diadem. He bites his lower lip with his teeth, crushing the four māras, and with red eyes glancing sideways he strikes terror. His wisdom consort, Dveṣavajrī, is blue in color; her pair of hands is beautified with a curved knife and a skull cup. She wears variegated garments, is adorned with jewel ornaments, and is intent on passion for the Blessed One. All the heroes, white Acala and the rest, should be meditated on as resembling the Lord of the Wheel, and the heroines, Mohavajrī and the rest, as resembling Dveṣavajrī.

Having thus seen the depths of the sky pervaded by the mass of light rays from the wheel of the fully perfected maṇḍala, one should take pride in the aggregates, elements and sense bases. For the aggregate of form, it is Vairocana; for the aggregate of feeling, Vajrasūrya; for the aggregate of perception, Padmanarteśvara; for the aggregate of formations, Vajrarāja; for the aggregate of consciousness, Vajrasattva. For the eyes, Mohavajrī; for the ears, Dveṣavajrī; for the nose, Īrṣyāvajrī; for the mouth, Rāgavajrī; for touch, Piśunavajrī. The earth element is Pātanī, the water element Māraṇī, the fire element Ākarṣaṇī, the wind element Padmanarteśvarī, and the space element Padmajālinī. At the crown, throat and heart are oṃ āḥ hūṃ.

Then, with the light rays of the seed at one's heart, one summons the wisdom maṇḍala in front, and with the mantra oṃ hrāṃ hrīṃ hrauṃ and the like drives away all obstructors. Offering water for welcome, water for the feet and so on, one worships and praises. Reciting three times oṃ śrī caṇḍa mahāroṣaṇa sa parivāra sahita āgaccha gaccha jaḥ hūṃ vaṃ hoḥ atra maṇḍale adhiṣṭhānaṃ kuru hūṃ phaṭ svāhā, one performs the summoning and the rest, and makes the wisdom beings and the samaya beings complete as a single self. Then, with the light rays from the heart of oneself, holding the pride of the deity, one summons into the sky the five Tathāgatas together with their hosts of heroes and heroines, and makes the request, "May all the Tathāgatas empower me!" Thereupon one sees those Tathāgatas, holding in their hands jeweled vases of victory, say, "Just as, at the very moment of birth ...," and so forth, and empower oneself. While the empowerment is being given, a great festival of the gods takes place:

jo jo jjota The vajra queens bathe the Victor;
the wrathful ones go in their own bodies to the ten directions;
from the depths of the sky, sandalwood, saffron and other
fragrances, supreme flowers and a rain of jewels;
cymbals and dance, with praises and auspicious verses;
the heirs of the Victors with their hosts, joyful and delighted: a true festival.
The Lord of Sages is adorned with the jewels of the qualities of his body,
everywhere throughout the limitless, boundless realms.

After the empowerment, the Blessed One is sealed with the Lord of Mind, and with the same one Dveṣavajrī is sealed. The heroes, white Acala and the rest, and Mohavajrī and the rest, are sealed with Śāśvata, Ratneśa, Amitābha and Amoghasiddhi.

Then, with the light rays of the seed at one's heart, one ripens every realm of sentient beings without exception and establishes them in the state of Acala; then one meditates that those light rays are gathered back again and dissolve into the seed. Then one should firmly resolve that the entire three realms are in the form of the wheel of the maṇḍala.

The Lord of the Wheel, too, through passion for the sacred limb of Mohavajrī, first lets white Acala enter by way of his own crown, so that he himself becomes white Acala. Gathered in just this way, he should enter into union with Mohavajrī, who is Dveṣavajrī transformed. In the same way, gathering yellow Acala and the others into himself, each in turn, he enters into union with each of the yoginīs, Piśunavajrī and the rest. Afterward, having become glorious black Acala in union with Dveṣavajrī, one should hold the pride, "I myself am accomplished as Acala."

It is also said in the tantra:

Through passion for the four goddesses,
gather the whole maṇḍala completely;
in the union of oneself alone,
the one who keeps the vow should meditate intensely.

This is the third samādhi, called the Supreme King of Action.

Thus, having visualized the entire three realms as a single body whose nature is skillful means and wisdom, one should recite the mantra in accordance with the commitments: oṃ caṇḍamahāroṣaṇa hūṃ phaṭ. This is the root mantra. oṃ hrāṃ hrīṃ hrau candraruya caṭa caṭa pracaṭa pracaṭa kaha kaha prasphara prasphara prasphāraya prasphāraya hana hana grasa grasa bandha bandha jaṃbhaya jaṃbhaya staṃbhaya staṃbhaya mohaya mohaya sarbaśatrūṃṇāṃ mukhabandhanaṃ kuru kuru sarbaḍākinīnāṃ grahabhūtapiśācabyādhiyakṣaṇaṃ trasaya trasaya mara mara māraya māraya ruru caṇḍaruka rakṣa rakṣa caṇḍamahāsena sarbamājñāpayati oṃ caṇḍamahāroṣaṇa hūṃ phaṭ. This is the garland mantra. This is the mantra recitation.

Then Dveṣavajrī, together with her curved knife, is placed into the seed syllable at the heart of the Blessed One, and from there she arises at the wheel of emanation and blazes. With the tip of the caṇḍālī fire one burns the tathāgatas with their consorts who abide in the wheel of emanation, together with their respective wheels; the āli and kāli, the vowels and consonants, that abide in the wheel of enjoyment; and the letter ha that abides in the wheel of great bliss. Then, taking up the indestructible awakening mind, the sword as its emblem, one should meditate on it within the seed syllable at the heart. That seed syllable, too, should be seen as blazing, the very embodiment of the five buddhas.

Then again, the letter ū of the syllable hūṃ, whose nature is envy, is gathered into the letter ha, which is attachment; the ha into the rekhā, the stroke above it, which is slander; the rekhā into the half moon, which is delusion; and the half moon into the bindu, the drop, which is aversion. Having gathered the bindu into the nāda, which is the sphere of reality (dharmadhātu), one should then abide in the nāda, great bliss, whose nature is the single taste of all phenomena, free from permanence, annihilation and a middle, and not abiding in anything at all. This is the subtle yoga.

Then again, having beheld the Lord of the maṇḍala together with his retinue, arisen in the form of the wheel of the maṇḍala, accomplished through the power infused by aspirational prayers and by force of the dispositions of the beings to be trained, the yogin who is like Acala should undertake the reality of the torma.

For this, one sets out in front a vessel and so forth, arisen from khaṃ, filled with special foods, enjoyments and the like. There, upon a wind maṇḍala arisen from yaṃ, on a fire born from raṃ, upon three hearthstones in the form of heads arisen from oṃ āḥ hūṃ, stands a white lotus vessel produced from the letter a. It is filled with all the foods and other things born from bhrūṃ āṃ jrīṃ khaṃ hūṃ lāṃ māṃ pāṃ tāṃ bāṃ, which take shape all together as the five nectars and the five lamps. Blessed by those seed syllables, they blaze through the force of the fire maṇḍala, and, heated by the blaze, they melt. Having seen this as the color of the sun, one imagines above it, arisen from hūṃ, a sword whose nature is nectar, at the tip of a hilt about a span in length. This melts in the steam and becomes mixed in, and one thinks that it has become cool, like quicksilver. Then above it, with light rays from the three principles, one draws in the nectar of wisdom of the heroes and the heroines, the sovereign ladies, who dwell in the ten directions, and dissolves it into that very nectar. One sees the syllable oṃ and the others also melt in turn, and blesses it with the three principles. With this mantra, oṃ namo bhagavate śrīcaṇḍamahāroṣaṇāla devāsuramaṇuṣrya trāsanāya sakālamāravala bināśānāya ratnamukuṭakṛtaśirase imi baliṃ gṛhṇa gṛhṇa mama sarbavighnān hara hara caturmārānni vāraya nivarya trāsa trāsa bhrāma bhrāma cchiṃda cchiṃda bhiṃda bhiṃda nāśā śātāpa tāpa śoṣa śoṣa ccheda ccheda bheda bheda duṣṭasatvānmama biruddacitta kanbhaśmī kuru kuru hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ phaṭ svāhā, one should offer the torma to the Blessed One together with his retinue.

Moreover, one sees that the Blessed One and his retinue draw it up through tubes of light rays from the hūṃ on their vajra tongues, partake of it and are delighted. Having satisfied them with the mantra beginning oṃ akāro mukhaṃ, one also offers water to cool the mouth and the rest, worships them and praises them. Reciting the hundred-syllable mantra, one asks their forbearance and requests them to depart. This is the rite of the torma.

Thus, through the yoga that does not block any of the senses and does not abide in anything, the entire three realms, whose nature is the wheel of the maṇḍala, arising in dependence like the moon in water, one should dwell as one pleases.

Furthermore:

Whoever knows that reality, the very self of Acala,
just as it is,
that yogin, bound to nothing fixed,
roams everywhere like a lion.
Without deference in speech, to beings a mantra,
appearing in his natural state, he is like Acala.
Knowing the reality of unborn phenomena,
he comes to have compassion as his enjoyment.
Here he puts an end to the vicious
who do harm to the teaching,
and, seizing their wealth,
uses it for the benefit of beings.
Therefore he never hides with lies
the faults of himself or others.
Likewise, too, he relies upon
the commitments of the five nectars.
One should not make "edible" and "inedible";
"to be approached" and "not to be approached" should be abandoned;
one should not make "to be done" and "not to be done";

The deity is Acala in his form as Caṇḍamahāroṣaṇa. The epithets "Great Fierce Wrathful One", "Great Wrathful One" and "Lord of the Fierce" render the Tibetan descriptive names literally. Mohavajrī is the first of the consorts in his retinue. The phrases "maṇḍala of emission" and "Some read 'flesh'" are rendered as written; their exact sense is unclear. Read literally, the cymbal simile says "like a drunken cymbal". The final sentences, from "As it moves", seem to jump straight from the special joy to the emanation of the goddesses. The Tibetan reads this way, apparently with a gap, and is translated as it stands. "Bakula garlands" renders ba ku li. "Music" renders dbyangs, whose sense in this list of ornaments is uncertain. "Supreme yoga" renders shin tu rnal 'byor (atiyoga), and "Initial Union" renders dang po sbyor ba (ādiyoga). Pātanī, Māraṇī, Ākarṣaṇī, Padmanarteśvarī and Padmajālinī are the goddesses that the Caṇḍamahāroṣaṇa cycle assigns to the elements. "Śāśvata" (literally "the Eternal", rtag pa) is used in this cycle as a name of Vairocana, and "the Lord of Mind" (thugs kyi dbang po) appears to designate Akṣobhya. The opening syllables of the festival verse are kept as written.
