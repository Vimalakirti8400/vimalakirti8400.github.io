About this text

The Sūtra Called the Twelve Eyes, known in Sanskrit as the Dvādaśalocana-nāma-sūtra and in Tibetan as Mig bcu gnyis pa zhes bya ba'i mdo, is a Buddhist sūtra concerned with the interpretation of celestial omens and the divinatory science traditionally known as the Twelve Eyes. The text is set in Rājagṛha, where King Rūpahṛdaya, troubled by alarming signs in the sky — lightning, falling stars, and meteoric iron — is directed by his brahmin astrologers toward this body of knowledge, prompting him to seek clarification from the Buddha himself. It belongs to the sūtra literature of the Kangyur and was translated into Tibetan by the Indian abbot Ajitaśrībhadra together with the great translator Śākyaod. It is catalogued as Toh 359 in the Kangyur, where it is classified among the general sūtras.

Translated by the Indian abbot, the monk Ajitaśrībhadra, and the great translator, the monk Śākyaod.

In the Sanskrit language: Dvādaśalocana-nāma-sūtra. In the Tibetan language: The Sūtra Called the Twelve Eyes. May it be accomplished.

The Blessed One, the Buddha, was dwelling in the Veṇuvana grove in the city of Rājagṛha. At that time lightning appeared, shooting stars fell, and meteoric iron descended from the sky. The king Rūpahṛdaya, together with his retinue, summoned his brahmin astrologers and said to them: "What is the cause, and what are the conditions, for all this agitation in the sky?" The brahmins replied: "Omens such as these arise from the scriptural science called the Twelve Eyes. We are too ashamed to expound it."

Then King Rūpahṛdaya went to where the Blessed One was, paid homage at his feet, and spoke these words: "Blessed One, out of compassion for sentient beings, please teach the scriptural science of omens of the Twelve Eyes." The Blessed One declared: "One who has gone forth into renunciation is not of the sort to teach such worldly scriptural sciences as these, which belong to non-Buddhists."

Then King Rūpahṛdaya again addressed the Blessed One: "Blessed One, out of compassion for me, please resolve this doubt." The Blessed One then granted permission to explain and related the following account of the original occasion. He declared:

"Great King, long ago, in a time that has passed, there dwelt near a Himalayan mountain a sage named Bhagava. Once he saw a young tigress wandering by and desire arose in him. He lay with her, and when the season came twelve sons were born. When the children were seven years old, both parents died. For twelve years the children performed the rites of accomplishment directed to Brahmā, offering prayers to fulfill their wishes and hopes. Then Brahmā regarded them with compassion and, together with Śakra (Indra, lord of the gods) and the four great kings with their retinues, descended to Jambudvīpa. Brahmā spoke to those children: 'Children, what do you desire?' The children spoke to Brahmā: 'O God, we are by nature still children, greatly limited in understanding and without discernment of what is good or bad. Please teach us what the omens of the sun, moon, planets, stars, and maṇḍalas are, and what the scriptural science of omens is like.' Then Śakra, lord of the gods, spoke to Brahmā: 'O teacher of the gods, it is fitting that you grant these children their accomplishment.' Then Brahmā spoke to the children: 'Children, listen. I shall teach you what the maṇḍalas of the twenty-eight lunar mansions and all the stars are like, and what the omens of the sun, moon, planets, the falling of meteoric iron, the shaking and trembling of the earth, and the scriptural science of omens are.

'In the last month of winter, on the waxing and the waning fortnights, on the eighth, the fourteenth, and the fifteenth days, if the sunlight appears dim and turns dark as darkness, great obstacles will arise for the noble and learned among humankind.

'On the day of the lunar mansion Aśvinī, if something resembling smoke appears from the sun, and it revolves and disperses, for twelve years rain will not fall.

'On the day of the lunar mansion Bharaṇī, if the sun takes on an appearance like the color of gold, the lands of the surrounding kingdoms will be destroyed and a great famine will arise in the center.

'In the last month of winter, on the waxing fortnight, on the day of the lunar mansion Kārttikā, if the sun appears especially bright, the border regions will be destroyed and rain will not come to the center.

'On the waxing fortnight of the first month of spring, on the day of the lunar mansion Āgrā, if the sun appears distorted and appears as two suns, disease among humans will arise.'"

Translator's notes

The title "Dvādaśalocana" (Sanskrit) literally means "Twelve Eyes," consistent with the Tibetan rendering. The sūtra is a relatively rare canonical text dealing with astral omens; it is framed as a Buddha-sūtra but its content is worldly prognostication, which explains the Blessed One's initial reluctance. The name "Bhagava" for the sage is likely a Tibetan transcription of Sanskrit Bhṛgu or a similar name; "Bhagava" is also a general honorific, but here it functions as a proper name. The lunar mansion "Āgrā" (Tibetan: ཨཱ་གྲཱ) does not correspond to a standard Sanskrit nakṣatra name; it may be a transcription of Mṛgaśīrṣa or another mansion, and the identification is uncertain. The phrase rendered as "the moon in the sign of the fish" reflects a recurring idiom in this text where the moon's position in a particular asterism or zodiacal sign is described figuratively; "the sign of the fish" likely refers to Pisces or an asterism in that region, and the same rendering has been used consistently with the preceding passage. The term rendered as "disease of the blood" for ཁྲག་ནད is a relatively transparent compound meaning ailments associated with blood, possibly epidemic hemorrhagic disease. The phrase ཡུལ་དེར་ཐབ་མོ་ཆེན་པོ་འོང is rendered as "a great conflagration will come to that land," where ཐབ་མོ can mean a cooking fire or conflagration; given the context of destructive portents, "conflagration" is the most appropriate sense. The phrase rendered "the day of the lunar mansion governed by Indra" corresponds to the mansion Jyeṣṭhā, whose presiding deity is Indra; the text here uses "Indra's day" as a synonym for Jyeṣṭhā, consistent with standard Indian astrological convention. This is the same mansion named explicitly elsewhere in the passage as Jyeṣṭhā, so no inconsistency arises.

The phrase "planet with a long tail" renders the Tibetan term for a comet or the planet with a trailing apparition; in context it most likely refers to a comet as a bad omen, though some traditional readings apply it to particular planetary conjunctions.

The final sentence is incomplete in the source text as given, breaking off mid-passage. The passage lists several planetary and teratological omens drawn from a genre of Indian astral and portent literature closely parallel to Sanskrit nimitta-śāstra material. The lunar mansion names Kārttikā, Maghā, Pūrva Phalgunī, Puṣyā, Śravaṇā, and Viśākhā are transliterated directly from the Tibetan phonetic renderings of the Sanskrit nakṣatra names and are kept as such. The phrase rendered "the red-colored planet Mars" represents the Tibetan mig dmar, literally "red-eye," a standard Tibetan epithet for Mars, consistent with the preceding passage's usage. The term rendered "child of indeterminate sex, possessing both characteristics" translates bu ma ning mtshan gnyis pa, literally "a child that is a hermaphrodite with two characteristics," a recognized category in classical Indian omens literature. The term rendered "great treasury" for གནང་ཆེན is somewhat uncertain; it can also mean "great favor" or "great grant," referring to the king's largesse or authority in dispensing patronage. The interpretation as "treasury" or "royal domain of bounty" best fits the political omen context here.

The city name གཟུགས་ཅན་སྙིང་པོ (Rūpasāraprabha) is taken as a proper royal name or epithet for the king; the Tibetan is literally "essence of form" or "heart of form," likely rendering a Sanskrit name such as Rūpasāra.

The closing title "The Twelfth Eye" reflects the Tibetan མིག་བཅུ་གཉིས་པ, literally "the twelfth eye," likely indicating this sūtra is the twelfth in a series of texts arranged under the metaphor of "eyes" or visionary omens.
