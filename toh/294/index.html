About this text

The Great Sūtra Called The Five-and-Three, known in Sanskrit as the Pañcatriyanāma Mahāsūtra and in Tibetan as Mdo chen po lnga gsum pa zhes bya ba, is a Buddhist sūtra in which the Buddha addresses a gathering of monks at Śrāvastī, in the Jeta Grove. The text concerns the various metaphysical positions held by ascetics and brahmins regarding perception, non-perception, annihilation, and liberation, systematically examining the views through which such thinkers make declarations about existence and the self. Its title appears to reflect a structural arrangement of five and three categories that organize the discourse. It is Toh 294 in the Kangyur, belonging to the general class of sūtra literature.

Homage to the Three Jewels.

Thus did I hear. At one time the Blessed One was dwelling at Śrāvastī, in the Jeta Grove, in the garden of Anāthapiṇḍada.

Then the Blessed One addressed the monks: "Monks, all those ascetics (śramaṇas) and brahmins who make declarations about the many bases of their views, all of them make their declarations in terms of five positions. What are the five? Some declare: 'From this point forward, I shall become one who possesses perception.' Others declare: 'I shall be without perception,' or 'I shall be neither with perception nor without perception.' And there is annihilation. And yet others make the declaration: 'In this very life I shall pass into nirvāṇa.'

"Among those ascetics and brahmins endowed with knowledge who speak of possessing perception, some hold that a self exists, and they declare: 'From this point forward, I shall become one who possesses perception.' Others declare: 'There will be annihilation.' And yet others make the declaration: 'In this very life I shall pass into nirvāṇa.' When these five are present they become three, and when the three are present they become five. This is a brief presentation of the Dharma category called The Five-and-Three.

"Monks, now among those ascetics and brahmins who speak of possessing perception, those who make the declaration: 'From this point forward, I shall become one who possesses a self with perception' — those ascetics and brahmins endowed with knowledge who speak in this way, some hold that a self exists, and they declare: 'From this point forward, I shall become one who possesses perception and has form,' or 'one who is formless,' or 'one who has a single unified perception,' or 'one who has varied perceptions,' or 'one who has a limited perception,' or 'one who has come to have a vast perception,' or 'one who has boundless perception.' And yet others, turning away from all of that, make the declaration: 'That is the sense base of infinite consciousness.'

"The Tathāgata knows correctly and precisely those ascetics and brahmins who speak of possessing perception, those who make the declaration: 'From this point forward, I shall become one who possesses perception' — those ascetics and brahmins endowed with knowledge who speak in this way, some holding that a self exists — who declare: 'From this point forward, I shall become one who possesses perception and has form,' or 'formless,' or 'with a single unified perception,' or 'with varied perceptions,' or 'with a limited perception,' or 'with a vast perception,' or 'with boundless perception' — and those who, turning away from all of that, make the declaration: 'That is the sense base of infinite consciousness.'

"Monks, those with varying perceptions and varying designations declare: 'Having perfectly passed beyond the sense base of infinite consciousness in every respect, there is nothing whatsoever,' and, having established the sense base of nothingness, they abide there — non-dual, unmoving. This, they say, is supreme; this is unsurpassable.

"Monks, that is gross, compounded, intended, and dependently originated. And since formations exist, there is also the cessation of formations. Knowing this to be the case, the Tathāgata turns back from that, beholds its pacification, and dwells beholding its definitive transcendence."

"Monks, in this regard there are ascetics and brahmins who advocate the absence of perception and who make declarations — some declaring: 'Henceforth I shall become one without perception.' These contradict and censure what is asserted by those ascetics and brahmins who advocate the presence of perception and who make declarations — some declaring: 'Henceforth I shall become one with perception.' They censure it, and they think to themselves: 'Perception is a disease. Perception is a boil. Perception is a thorn. This — the absence of perception — is peaceful. This is sublime.' And the Tathāgata fully knows, exactly as it is, those ascetics and brahmins who advocate the absence of perception and who make such declarations — some declaring: 'Henceforth I shall become one without perception' — and who contradict and censure what is asserted by those ascetics and brahmins who advocate the presence of perception and who make declarations — some declaring: 'Henceforth I shall become one with perception.' They censure it, and they think: 'Perception is a disease. Perception is a boil. Perception is a thorn. The absence of perception — this is peaceful. This is sublime.'

"Monks, I do not designate consciousness as coming or going, as persisting, as passing away or being reborn, as growing, increasing, and expanding — as something other than form, other than feeling, other than perception, other than formations. Monks, if anyone says: 'I designate consciousness as coming or going, as persisting, as passing away or being reborn, as growing, increasing, and expanding — as something other than form, other than feeling, other than perception, other than formations' — that is nothing more than words, and since it falls outside the domain of valid experience, if questioned on it, that person will not truly know and will become confused in answering.

The Great Sūtra, the Fifty-Third, is complete.

Translator's notes

The title Pañcatriyanāma Mahāsūtram is a hybrid Sanskrit formation meaning "The Great Sūtra Called the Five-and-Three," corresponding precisely to the Tibetan mdo chen po lnga gsum pa. The sūtra belongs to the category of discourses analyzing speculative views about the self and its posthumous condition, closely parallel to passages in the Pāli Dīgha and Aṅguttara Nikāyas dealing with sassatavāda and ucchedavāda. The phrase rendered "from this point forward I shall become one who possesses perception" (de pha chad bdag 'du shes can du 'gyur ro) reflects the Tibetan rendering of a standard Indic formulation about the post-mortem continuity or non-continuity of the self — a concern of the speculative teachers being described. The final paragraph's characterization of even the attainment of the base of nothingness as "gross, compounded, intended, and dependently originated" is a doctrinal marker that the Tathāgata has insight surpassing all such attainments.
