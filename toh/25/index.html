About this text

Toh 25 in the Kangyur is a short liturgical scripture known in English as "The Noble Perfection of Wisdom: A Text of One Hundred and Eight Names," corresponding to the Sanskrit Āryaprajñāpāramitānāmāṣṭaśatakaṃ and the Tibetan Phags pa shes rab kyi pha rol tu phyin pa'i mtshan brgya rtsa brgyad pa. It belongs to the Perfection of Wisdom class of texts and takes the form of a devotional hymn enumerating one hundred and eight names and epithets of the Perfection of Wisdom, personified as the mother of all buddhas across past, present, and future. Opening with a verse of homage to this "Blessed Lady," the text then proceeds to enumerate her names in rapid succession, covering qualities such as omniscience, suchness, emptiness, and signlessness. As part of the Kangyur, it sits within the vast Prajñāpāramitā scriptural tradition and reflects the deeply devotional as well as philosophical dimensions of that literature.

Homage to the Blessed Lady, the Perfection of Wisdom.

Of all the Victors of past, future, and present,
you, O Virtuous One, are indeed the Mother.
You are the divine lady, daughter of the Victors,
endowed with the intrinsic nature of being without intrinsic nature.
I shall recite the names of her who has become
the Mother of the buddhas.
Those who wish for abundance, listen well.

Whoever recites these one hundred and eight names of the Perfection of Wisdom shall be thoroughly liberated from all lower realms. All buddhas shall regard them with mindful attention. Homage to the Blessed Lady, the noble Perfection of Wisdom, the beloved one devoted to the Perfection of Wisdom, she who is endowed with immeasurable qualities, she whose very omniscient knowing through the primordial wisdom of all Tathāgatas is honored by all buddhas and all bodhisattvas.

Whoever holds this Perfection of Wisdom shall be as one who has grasped the hundred-thousand-verse Perfection of Wisdom. Translator's notes

The passage lists one hundred and eight names of the Perfection of Wisdom personified as a goddess. Several names in the middle section involve subtle philosophical distinctions: "the infallible suchness" and "the non-other suchness" render Tibetan terms that distinguish suchness as free from error and as not being something extraneous, respectively — standard Prajñāpāramitā epithets. The "ten sense bases of exhaustion" renders a technical Abhidharma category referring to the ten sense bases in their aspect of exhaustion or cessation; this is a less common formulation and worth noting for doctrinal specialists. The phrase rendered "she who holds all worlds" could also be read as "she who sustains all worlds," both senses being present in the Tibetan. The one-hundred-and-eight names referred to are those of the Perfection of Wisdom personified as a goddess, recited throughout this text. The phrase rendered "the beloved one devoted to the Perfection of Wisdom" translates a slightly compressed honorific construction; it could also be read as a vocative addressed to a devotee of the Perfection of Wisdom, but the context of a homage formula directed at the deity supports the reading given here. The mantra syllables are rendered in standard reconstructed Sanskrit transliteration following convention for such dhāraṇī passages; individual syllable meanings are not translated, as is standard practice. The final sentence ends mid-passage, indicating the text continues.
