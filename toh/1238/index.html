About this text

The Clear Illumination: A Sādhana of Glorious Hevajra, known in Sanskrit as Prakāśa nāma Śrī Hevajra Sādhana and in Tibetan as Pal Kyé Dorjé Drubtap Rabtu Salwa, is Toh 1238 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the commentaries on the tantras. It is a sādhana, a ritual and meditative manual for practice, centered on Hevajra, opening with homage to the two-armed, white, single-faced innate Heruka and promising to set forth the practices of his sixteen-armed, six-armed and other forms, together with his consort Nairātmyā. Its author presents it as a clarification of earlier Indian works in the same lineage, chiefly the summary composed by Jālandharipāda of Saroruha's Hevajra sādhana and the Dveṣavajra sādhana of Bhadrapāda. The work moves through the generation stage into the completion stage, drawing on the Hevajra Tantra to teach great bliss, the inseparability of emptiness and compassion, and the unity of saṃsāra and nirvāṇa, and its colophon records a translation into Tibetan by the Indian paṇḍita Vanaratna and the translator Gö Shönu Pal.

Translation

In the Indian language: prākāśaṃ nāma śrī hevajra sādhanaṃ
In the Tibetan language: The Clear Illumination: A Sādhana of Glorious Hevajra.

Homage to glorious Heruka.

Having paid homage to glorious Heruka,
The protector whose nature is empty and not empty,
Two-armed, innate,
White in color and with a single face,
I, a mantra practitioner, shall set forth the sādhanas
Of the forms transformed from him,
The sixteen-armed, the six-armed and the rest,
And likewise of Nairātmyā.

Regarding this, glorious Jālandharipāda composed a summary of the Hevajra sādhana of various forms that the glorious master Saroruhapāda composed, called the Vajra Lamp. This work is nothing other than a clarification of the meaning of that and of the sādhana of Dveṣavajra composed by Bhadrapāda. Even so, reflecting that those of weak understanding, having heard it, would not be able to meditate, I have drawn correctly from that sādhana, from the summary, and from the sādhanas of Dveṣavajra, and, in order to give those of weak understanding confidence of their own, following the root tantra, I shall combine the sādhana of the generation stage and the completion stage. As it is truly said in the Hevajra:

The stage of generation,
And the stage of completion as well:
Relying on the two stages in equality,
The Vajra Holder taught the Dharma.

Here, the sādhana of the completion stage should also be understood by following other sūtras, such as the Perfection of Wisdom. Here, Jālandharipāda taught the sūtras of the sādhana in which the thirty-two marks of a great being are purified as thirty-two; these will be explained in order.

First of all, one confesses one's misdeeds, goes for refuge to the Three, takes the vows up to the poṣadha, and trains well in the six perfections. Having pleased the vajra master, one enters the assembly maṇḍala as taught and explained in glorious Hevajra and is fully empowered in the proper order. Again one properly attends upon the venerable master. Having heard this tantra, one attains the awakening mind, the true reality in which emptiness and compassion are inseparable, and receives the master's permission with the words, "You should meditate on Hevajra." With a mind intent on the welfare of all sentient beings, the yogin who wishes to practice glorious Hevajra, in a secluded place agreeable to the mind, should sit in the vajra posture at the center of a comfortable seat marked with a crossed vajra, and should meditate on glorious Hevajra until warmth is attained. When a little warmth has been properly attained, one should rely on the samayas of the five nectars and so on, summon and properly take a consort (prajñā) who has the signs, and accomplish one's chosen deity.

Having recited śrī heruka haṃ, one focuses on one's chosen deity, who will be explained, and on a sun transformed from the letter ra upon a lotus at one's own heart, one should visualize a blue syllable hūṃ possessing five rays of light. With the five kinds of light rays radiating from it, one illuminates the infinite world systems, and invites one's chosen deity surrounded by the circle of yoginīs Gaurī and the rest, together with the master, the buddhas and the bodhisattvas. Imagining them in the sky before one, one should worship them properly with the special outer, secret and reality offerings.

Here, worship by the sixteen goddesses, from Vīṇā up to Dharmā, emanated from their own seed syllables, with their respective forms, hands, emblems and feet that will be explained: this is the outer offering. Meditating on the goddesses Gaurī and the rest, who dwell in his circle, offering samaya substances with their hands: this is the secret offering. The reality of emanating and gathering that comes from experiencing the bliss of oneself entering into union with Nairātmyā: that itself is the reality offering.

Having worshipped properly, one should perform the confession of misdeeds and so on in the presence of the Blessed One:

"Whatever sinful deeds I have done, caused to be done, or rejoiced in, while wandering through the successive births of beginningless saṃsāra, I confess, and from now on I will certainly never do any of them again. I rejoice wholeheartedly in the merit of all people, and likewise of the worldly gods, of the buddhas who live like the rhinoceros, of the śrāvaka buddhas, and of the perfect buddhas, the protectors. Whatever merit arises together with this rejoicing, I dedicate to unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. For the sake of generating the mind set on the awakening of beings, in the mansion of the bliss of innate joy, I go for refuge until the heart of awakening to the Buddha, the hero, glorious Heruka, the single body of the three blessed ones; to the Dharma he taught, the maṇḍala, mantras, mudrās and so on; and to the Saṅgha, the assembly of goddesses Gaurī and the rest who dwell in his circle. I generate the awakening mind in which emptiness and compassion are inseparable, toward perfect and complete awakening, whose characteristic is emptiness endowed with all the supreme aspects, the cause for accomplishing the aims of all sentient beings. I will rely on the unsurpassed path, namely the Vajrayāna. And again, in order to make all those sentient beings happy, I give away myself and all that is mine."

This is the first sūtra, called the Wheel of Offering.

Again it is said in the Hevajra:

Having beheld the Venerable One in the sky,
Vajra-born, of great compassion,
He is to be worshipped by the eight goddesses
Who hold all the ornaments.

Here:

Gaurī holds the deer-marked moon,
Caurī holds the vessel of the sun,
Vetālī holds water in her hand,
Ghasmarī holds medicine,
Pukkasī offers with her hand,
Śavarī holds the elixir,
Caṇḍālī beats the ḍamaru drum,
And Ḍombī displays an embrace.

Again it is said, "Offerings should be made by Rūpavajrā and the others," and so forth, in detail.

After that, having fully considered that, in the wanderings through the beginningless wheel of existence, all sentient beings have by nature been my mother, my brothers, my sisters and so on, one should meditate on great loving-kindness, whose characteristic is delight in them as in an only child.

Then, seeing all of them fallen into the ocean of saṃsāra without a refuge, with tears streaming down, one should meditate on great compassion, whose nature is the earnest wish to draw them out of the causes of suffering.

Then the yogin who has attained joy, through seeing the exalted method of glorious Heruka and the rest, should meditate on great joy, whose nature is the bestowing of all happiness.

After that, having taken on the form of glorious Heruka, the embodiment of accomplishment, and reflecting, "It is not fitting to grasp at gain and the like," one should meditate, fourth, on great equanimity, whose characteristic is freedom from the eight worldly concerns. These are the four abodes of Brahmā.

Immediately after that, seeing that the ordinary body does not exist, through the yoga of the samādhi called the vajra of the light of wisdom, one turns toward and meditates on the meaning of the four syllables śrī heruka. This comes from the sacred instruction. "This itself is free of impurity": that is supreme.

Thus, in the Vajra Lamp, the summary explained by Jālandharipāda, beginning with the practice of the supreme mantra practitioner who has become such as this, it says:

The wise one should first meditate
On the taste whose identity is the dharmadhātu.
Abiding upon that,
The yogin meditates on Heruka himself.

The meaning of this is that, abiding there, one should turn toward the meaning of the six syllables śrī heruka vajra, and then, as the sacred instruction says, "One should meditate on the yoga of the samādhi called the vajra of the light of wisdom, the meaning of the four syllables śrī heruka": that is supreme.

Then, thinking that one arises as the Innate Protector, in an instant, in the center of the mansion of the buddhas, one meditates on the Innate Heruka: Vajrasattva, expressed by the syllable baṃ, white, supremely peaceful, with a crown of matted locks, seated in the vajra posture, two-armed, with the Dharma mudrā, bearing the thirty-two major marks and adorned with the eighty minor marks.

If one asks how his whiteness and the rest are to be understood in his form, it is as said in the eighth chapter:

The intermediate directions, the faces and so on, as before,
With the light of the moon, of water crystal and of jewels.

"As before" means this: just as, from the transformation of the habitual tendencies of ignorance, there arose the forms of the beings of the first eon, subtle and so on, so is it here; that is the innate.

Here, Candrakīrtipāda also said in the sūtra on perfecting Vajrasattva: "Through devotion to emptiness, one should cast off the pride of the ordinary body, and in an instant conceive oneself as the form of the great Vajradhara, white in color." The source for this is the third chapter of the glorious Guhyasamāja:

Abiding in the center of the expanse of space,
One should meditate on the maṇḍala of the buddhas,
A great array of clouds of light rays,
Equal to the blazing light of the buddhas.

Here too, the Blessed One said in the eighth chapter:

The equal union of the vowels and consonants
Is the seat of Vajrasattva.

Here its meaning should be stated. The vowels and consonants are the moon and the sun, that is, the apprehended and the apprehender; this should be known from the master's instruction and from scripture as well. Their equal union is a synonym for ignorance, which grasps them as mingled. By the very force of the words, "Because that does not exist, this too does not exist," and through the operation of a nonimplicative negation, the emptiness of all things is the seat. Seat, basis and place of generation are synonyms.

Again it is said in the Hevajra:

For the body arisen from the unchanging,
Even the syllables hūṃ phaṭ are not to be sought.

The meaning of this is as follows. Change is degeneration. The unchanging is the absence of degeneration: everything empty, the absence of an impure body. That being so, what arises from it is also that, and the body too is that; such it is. For that Vajrasattva arisen from luminosity, even the syllables hūṃ phaṭ are not to be sought, because for him, apart from luminosity, other things such as the syllable hūṃ are not the cause, since he is the form of completion itself. "His" means the Blessed One's; that is the intended meaning.

Again, Jālandharipāda said: "The equal union of the vowels and consonants is the supreme abode that is without intrinsic nature; that is the meaning of 'taste'. The supreme is the vajra seat of Vajrasattva." Thus the noble Āryadeva said:

Mind is to be dissolved into what is self-arisen,
Mental factors into ignorance;
That too goes into luminosity,
And here the three realms of existence cease.
From luminosity comes the great emptiness,

and so on, in detail. It is also said in the noble Eight Thousand: "That which does not apprehend any phenomenon whatsoever is the Perfection of Wisdom; it is that which gives birth to the buddhas." By this the supreme and the innate are also explained.

On the side of the generation stage, the syllable hūṃ and the rest are indeed the cause, for the chapter on the deities says:

Having seen the syllable hūṃ transformed,
One should meditate on oneself as the embodiment of aversion.

Here the completion stage relies on the generation stage, as is fitting; therefore one should have no doubt. The Blessed One taught it in order to keep the instructions hidden from sentient beings without the good fortune for them, by teaching the forms of the sounds and so on in other ways; by this and by that one should understand it.

These six syllables, śrī heruka vajra, are definitively explained by the syllable baṃ: from the supreme, Vajrasattva, the innate, is explained as Heruka. At his heart is the syllable jraṃ: from the transformation of the letter ra comes the sun. From the transformation of its half-moon and of the letter a comes the full disc of the moon, and in its center the syllable ja, the seed of the birth of beings, is taught to be transformed into the syllable hūṃ.

Having seen it black in color and greatly terrifying, from the transformation of the hūṃ there comes a vajra. One should meditate on the reality of the hūṃ abiding at its hub. After that, through the transformation of the sun and the rest and of the hūṃ, one meditates on it as no more than a reflection of the moon; and, by the instruction that will be explained, having seen in the sky Dveṣavajra, the great terrifying one emanated from it, one should, through his entering, abide in the form of one's chosen deity. This is the principal deity.

Thus also Bhadrapāda, drawing from the Hevajra Tantra, said:

At one's own heart one meditates on the repha;
From it arises the disc of the sun.
Upon that is the hūṃ itself,
Whose nature is method and wisdom,
Black in color, greatly terrifying.
From the syllable hūṃ it becomes a vajra.
Abiding at the center of the vajra's hub,
One meditates again on the reality of hūṃ.

Through the transformation of all these, one should meditate on the reflection of the moon, as instructed, by the instruction that will be explained. Emanating from it, having seen above in the sky the Vajra Holder, the great terrifying one, through his entering one becomes the very identity of one's chosen deity. So he said.

Jālandharipāda too, drawing from the root tantra, said:

One should meditate on the lord of the maṇḍala,
Arisen from the reflection of the being.

This is one's chosen deity.

Having thus meditated on the great terrifying Dveṣavajra with eight faces and so on, one should emanate into all the directions, beginning with the east, the ten wrathful ones arisen from hūṃ, amid churning garlands of flame. In the east is Yamāntaka, with Vairocana as his crown ornament, deep black, holding a vajra hammer. In the south is Prajñāntaka, with Ratnasambhava as his crown ornament, brilliant white, holding a white staff. In the west is Padmāntaka, with Amitābha as his crown ornament, deep red, holding a red lotus. In the north is Vighnāntaka, with Akṣobhya as his crown ornament, deep blue, holding a vajra with open prongs. In the northeast is Acala, deep black, holding a sword. In the southeast is Ṭakkirāja, deep black, holding a vajra hook. In the southwest is Nīladaṇḍa, deep black, holding a blue staff. In the northwest is Mahābala, deep black, holding a trident. Acala and the others have Akṣobhya as their crown ornament. Above is Uṣṇīṣacakravartin, yellow, with Vairocana as his lord, holding a yellow wheel. Below is Sumbharāja, smoke-colored, his head adorned with the lord of mind, holding a pestle, terrifying. These should be imagined.

The wrathful ones should be imagined as three-eyed, their tawny hair standing on end, adorned with various snakes, two-armed and single-faced, short and big-bellied, holding in the left hand a noose together with the threatening gesture, with distorted faces. Ṭakkirāja and Acala, however, are at play and adorned with divine ornaments. Further, all of them should be seen standing on variegated lotuses and suns in the posture with the left leg extended.

Thus the ten kings of wrath are set out alike, each in his own place, in the forms of the ten wrathful ones, Yamāntaka and the rest, whose nature is emanation. As they face him, having first been entrusted with the eight-line mantra and with the heart and near-heart mantras, they should be seen joining their palms and saying, "What shall we do?"

Then one gives the command according to the different activities, pacifying and the rest. The command should be given with the mantra taught in the Ḍākinī Vajrapañjara: oṃ sumbha ni sumbha hūṃ gṛhṇa gṛhṇa hūṃ gṛhṇāpaya gṛhṇāpaya hūṃ ānaya hoḥ bhagavān vadyī rāja hūṃ phaṭ.

Having duly received the command of the Vajra Holder, they themselves go here and there, bind by the neck with vajra nooses the hosts of obstructors, Indra and the rest, seize them while terrifying them with their respective weapons, and hand them over to the emanations of the ten directions. One sees them stationed facing the Vajra Holder.

As for these hosts of obstructors: Indra is white, riding a white elephant, thousand-eyed, beautiful in body, with a jeweled crown. Yama is black, riding a buffalo, his tawny hair standing on end, short and big-bellied. Varuṇa is white, the color of the sky, riding a turtle, beautiful in body, with a serpent hood over his head. Kubera is yellow, riding a man, beautiful in body, with a jeweled crown. Īśāna is white, riding a bull, with a crown of matted locks, short and big-bellied. Agni is red, seated on a goat, with a crown of matted locks on his head, short in body. The rākṣasa is smoke-colored, seated on a corpse, with loosened hair, short. Vāyu is pale blue, seated on a yellow deer, with a jeweled crown, beautiful in body. Above is Brahmā, the Grandfather, yellow, riding a goose, with a crown of matted locks, short and big-bellied. Below is Pṛthivī, the earth goddess, yellow, seated on a lotus, beautiful in body, with a jeweled crown. These obstructors, seized by Yamāntaka and the others, are seen trembling with terror and seeking refuge elsewhere.

After that, from the seed syllable at one's own heart, one emanates a wrathful one whose lower part has the form of a spike, black in color, greatly terrifying, as unbearable as the fire at the end of time, whose upper part has the form of Amṛtakuṇḍalin. One imagines him at the heads of the hosts of obstructors, and at the command, one drives in the ritual daggers with the mantra oṃ gha gha ghātaya ghātaya sarva duṣṭān kīlaya kīlaya sarva pāpaṃ hūṃ hūṃ vajra kīla vajra dharo da ajñāpayati sarva duṣṭāṃ vighnāṃ kāya vāga citta vajra kīlaya hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ phaṭ.

After that, one strikes them with the vajra hammer, the transformed emblem of the identity of all the wrathful ones. oṃ vajra muṅgara vajra kīlaya akoṭaya koṭaya hūṃ phaṭ is the mantra of striking. With this mantra the ritual dagger is driven in so that it reaches the very bottom of what holds it. This is the ritual dagger.

Then, together with their respective emanations, the obstructors and their retinues are burned by the fires emanated from the wrathful ones, and one sees them destroyed without remainder.

When they have been struck in this way and destroyed without remainder, and there are no more obstructors, then:

First, from the letter ra, one visualizes a sun;
On that sun, from hūṃ, arises a great crossed vajra.
With that very vajra, a fence
And the binding of a tent should also be visualized.

Here, or alternatively, by means of the syllable hūṃ, one should imagine vajra arrows, a net, a vajra canopy, and a ground whose nature is vajra, reaching down to the floor at the boundary of the ocean. This is the protection wheel.

Then, in the sequence that will be explained, by means of the passage beginning

Since it is not to be cultivated by the mind,
All beings are to be cultivated,

one meditates on the great protection, emptiness, and then one should recite oṃ śūnyatā jñāna vajra svabhāvātmako'haṃ, the mantra that gathers the essence of all things without exception.

Then one should meditate that the protection wheel, the three realms, the fence and the rest no longer appear, and in their midst one should visualize wisdom, white, in the form of the letter e, pervading all of space. In its center, in the middle of a crossed-vajra platform, is a blue wind maṇḍala arisen from the syllable yaṃ, shaped like a half moon. Then comes a red fire maṇḍala arisen from raṃ, triangular. Then a white water maṇḍala arisen from vaṃ, round. On top of that, a yellow earth maṇḍala arisen from laṃ, square. Having recognized the consciousness that enters into its center, one should imagine that all these maṇḍalas of the great elements are completely transformed, and that from them, in an instant, there arises a celestial palace adorned with the eight charnel grounds.

There, in their proper order, I shall describe the eight charnel grounds, each furnished with a tree, a yakṣa, a guardian of the directions, a nāga, a cloud, a mountain, and a stūpa.

In the east is the charnel ground called Caṇḍogra. There stand the śirīṣa tree; the white elephant-faced yakṣa; the white guardian of the direction Śakra (Indra); the yellow nāga Vāsuki; the many-colored cloud called Roaring; Mount Meru, square and made of jewels; and the white stūpa called Vajra.

In the south is the great charnel ground called the Remnant. There stand the mango tree; the black buffalo-faced yakṣa; the black guardian of the direction Yama; the white nāga Padma; the many-colored cloud called Swirling; the white Mount Malaya; and the black stūpa called Vajra.

In the west is the great charnel ground called Jvālākula, Blazing and Turbulent. There stand the aśoka tree; the white makara-faced yakṣa; the white guardian of the direction Varuṇa; the red nāga Karkoṭa; the many-colored cloud called Terrifying; the white Mount Kailāsa; and the white stūpa called Vajra of Passion.

In the north is the great charnel ground called Mountain Cave. There stand the aśvattha tree; the white human-faced yakṣa; the yellow guardian of the direction Kubera; the black nāga Takṣaka; the many-colored cloud called Rumbling; the green Mount Mandara; and the white stūpa called Vajra of Formations.

In the northeast, the quarter of Īśāna, is the great charnel ground called Lakṣmīvana, the Forest of the Goddess. There stand the nyagrodha tree; the white bull-faced yakṣa; the white guardian of the direction Maheśvara (Śiva); the yellow nāga called Śaṅkhapāla; the many-colored cloud called Fierce; the black Mount Mahendra; and the white stūpa called Mind Vajra.

In the southeast, the quarter of Agni, is the great charnel ground called Aṭṭahāsa, Fierce Laughter. There stand the karañja tree; the red goat-faced yakṣa; the red guardian of the direction Agni; the pale blue nāga Mahāpadma; the many-colored cloud called Tiger; the yellow Mount Gandhamādana; and the red stūpa called Body Vajra.

In the southwest, the quarter of Nairṛti, is the great charnel ground called Ghorāndhakāra, Terrifying Darkness. There stand the latāparkaṭī tree; the black bear-faced yakṣa; the black guardian of the direction, the rākṣasa; the white nāga called Ananta; the many-colored cloud called Full; the mountain called Gold; and the white stūpa called Jewel Vajra.

In the northwest, the quarter of Vāyu, is the great charnel ground Kilikilārava, Resounding with Kili Kili. There stand the arjuna tree; the green yakṣa called Deer-faced; the pale blue guardian of the direction Vāyu; the wrathful nāga Kulika; the many-colored cloud called Raining; the blue mountain called Śrīparvata, the Glorious Mountain; and the green stūpa called Dharma Vajra.

These yakṣas possess great magical power. They have one face and two arms, and they emerge from the trees with half their bodies. One should imagine them in this form: in their left hands they hold human skull cups filled with blood, and with their lotus-like right hands they hold various corpses.

These charnel grounds are adorned with green grass, dense thickets of many kinds of trees, and shoots, leaves and yellow blossoms. They are adorned all about with flocks of many kinds of birds, crows, kokilas, owls, parrots, mynahs, potakas, potikās, vultures and others. They are filled with many kinds of wild beasts, lions, wolves, tigers, bears, boars and others. They are suffused with the sweet scent of many kinds of fragrant flowers. They are made terrifying by the tumult of many kinds of emanations. Their various places are adorned, each in its own area, with bones, heads, skeletons, skulls, corpses and the like, and again, each in its own area, they are adorned with shrines, monasteries, meditation huts and pools.

They are attended by avadhūtas and female avadhūtīs, gathered with minds that hold the signs of the sacred commitment, by male and female meditators, by yakṣas and yakṣiṇīs, by male and female pretas, by rākṣasas and rākṣasīs, by kumbhāṇḍas and their females, by male and female bhūtas, by ḍākas and ḍākinīs, and by heroes and heroines. They are filled with hosts of ḍākas and ḍākinīs who have attained every delight: laughing, flirting, amorous play, embracing, kissing, scratching with the nails, and the rest.

Then, with minds full of delight, some dance with vajra steps. Some sing vajra songs. Some engage in the many practices of the sacred commitment. Some eat the five nectars and the five lamps. Some hand out many kinds of garments. Some frighten many kinds of beings. Some are also to be seen here drinking liquor.

Moreover, many kinds of musical instruments are being played: drāka drums, ḍamarus, paṭaha drums, mardala drums, jhajhara drums, vīṇās, flutes, cymbals and others. And again, siddhas, vidyādharas and vidyādharīs, great mahoragas, kiṃnaras and kiṃnarīs, gandharvas and gandharvīs, and many others, together with hosts of gods, asuras and garuḍas, crowd together, making it utterly terrifying with cries of kili kili and with dreadful roaring.

Then too, enchanting the mind with the nine sentiments of the dance, the eight great charnel grounds, furnished with hosts of vetālas and bhūtas, are to be imagined as the purity of the eight consciousnesses. This is written as Jālandharipāda taught it. These are the charnel grounds.

In their center, from the transformation of the four great elements, wind and the rest, that were explained, arises the celestial palace. Its squareness and its vajra lines are the purity of the five faculties. The four doors are the purity of the four applications of mindfulness. The four porticoes are the purity of the four right abandonments. The four platforms are the purity of the four bases of miraculous power. The four corners and the half section are the purity of the five powers. From the purity of the noble eightfold path, the eight pillars are fashioned. From the purity of the seven limbs of awakening come the door panels, the parapets, the parasols, the yak-tail whisks, the canopies, the bells and the banners.

Thus, from the purity of the thirty-seven factors of awakening, one should imagine the dependently arisen city of great liberation, the purity of Vairocana, whose nature is luminosity, upon a twelve-pointed crossed-vajra platform. Of this, the three points in the east are to be imagined as white, those in the south yellow, in the west red and in the north green, and the color of the center of the platform as blue. The squareness, the lines and the rest are to be learned from the commentary composed by Jālandharipāda.

Now I shall describe the arrangement of the sixteen goddesses. On the deity ledge outside the vajra lines and the five walls, beginning from the base of the doors, two on each side, in clockwise order, at the center of seats of moon and sun set in pairs, are the offering goddesses. Each is perfected from a seed, the first letter of her own name, marked with crescent and dot, and each abides in a form that matches her name, as follows: Vīṇā is yellow, Vaṃśā is red, Mukundā is smoke-colored, Murajā is white, Hāsyā is yellow, Lāsyā is red, Gītā is red, Nṛtyā is of many colors, Puṣpā is white, Dhūpā is black, Dīpā is like gold, Gandhā is yellow, Ādarśā is white, Rasā is red, Sparśā is green, and Dharmā is white. All of these, abiding on moon seats, are to be seen as the purity of the sixteen digits of the moon. The palace is adorned by them. This is the celestial palace.

After that, in the center of the celestial palace, upon the anthers of a lotus with petals of many colors, one should see the four māras lying on their backs, heads to the right and to the left, known as the māra of the aggregates, the māra of the afflictions, the lord of death, and the son of the gods. Of these, the māra of the aggregates is Brahmā, the māra of the afflictions is Viṣṇu, the māra who is lord of death is Maheśvara (Śiva), and the māra who is son of the gods is Indra, lord of the gods. The hearts of these four māras are pressed down by a sun.

On the petals of the directions, beginning with the east, are Brahmā, Indra, Upendra and Rudra. On the petals of the intermediate directions, beginning with the northeast, the quarter of Īśāna, are Yama, Kubera, Nairṛti and Vemacitrin. Their hearts too are pressed down, by eight moons. Everything from the lotus with petals of many colors up to Vemacitrin is completed at the very moment the celestial palace is completed. Why is this? Because dependent arising is inconceivable.

At the heart, upon the sun, from the transformation of the vowels beginning with a, doubled, arises the moon maṇḍala that makes the thirty-two major marks manifest; it is the nature of mirror-like wisdom.

Upon that, the thirty-four letters beginning with ka, together with the six letters ḍa ḍha da dha ya la, are doubled by being taken in forward order and in reverse order, and from the transformation of these eighty letters arises the sun maṇḍala, which makes the eighty minor marks manifest. One should see it as the nature of the wisdom of equality.

In the center where these two are joined, the skull cup and the curved knife, transformed from the two seeds hūṃ and aṃ and marked by those very seeds, are the discriminating wisdom.

Then, with the nets of light rays radiating from the seed itself, one illuminates the infinite world systems, draws the rays back again, and they enter into it. From this, all becoming one is the all-accomplishing wisdom.

Through the transformation of that oneness, in an instant, oneself becomes the form of one's chosen deity, glorious Heruka, complete with the color, the emblems and the rest that will be explained: either the sixteen-armed, the six-armed, the four-armed, or the two-armed form, the causal Vajra Holder; and likewise Nairātmyā and the rest, equally complete, arise in his embrace. This is the wisdom of the utterly pure sphere of reality. This is full awakening through the five aspects.

In the directions beginning with the east, in order, on the moons at the heart, and likewise in the center where moon and sun are joined, one should see the eight seeds gaṃ caṃ vaṃ ghaṃ paṃ śaṃ laṃ ḍaṃ. The emblems of Gaurī and the others, transformed from these, are the curved knife, the ḍamaru drum, the tortoise, the snake, the lion, the monk, the wheel and the vajra; one should imagine them blessed by those very seeds. Through full awakening by the five aspects, in which all of these are transformed, Gaurī and the others too are to be completed, with their colors, emblems and the rest, in the sequence that will be explained. This is the causal maṇḍala.

When the form of one's chosen deity has thus been completed, one should also see the circle of ḍākinīs beginning with Nairātmyā; that is, one should see oneself entered into union, in a single taste, with one's own consort.

The gandharva being, drawn in by the light rays of the samaya of samādhi, is of the nature of great bliss. Encircled by two garlands of red hoḥ syllables, it enters by the path of the ḍāka of the wheel. Then the Blessed One, through great passion and attachment together with his own consort, enters the taste of the utterly supreme great bliss and comes to abide in the form of a seed. This is the melting.

Then the four goddesses, Pukkasī and the others, seeing that the lord of the wheel is not there, are tormented by grief. Crying, "We are without a protector!" and overcome by great distress, they rouse the Blessed One with vajra songs that awaken his mind with intense lament.

I shall state the meaning of the four verses that begin, "Arise, Lord, with mind of compassion."

Arise, O Lord, with mind of compassion,
Protect me, Pukkasī;
Casting off the nature of emptiness,
Make love to me in the union of great bliss.

This is understood from the samādhi of having fallen into melting; it means: your great loving-kindness toward me.

Without you I shall die;
O Hevajra, arise!
Casting off the very nature of emptiness,
Accomplish the work of Śavarī.

This means: your great compassion toward me.

Lord of joy, host to the world,
Why do you dwell in emptiness?
Since without you I know no direction,
I, Caṇḍālī, make this request.

This means: arise from that state; your great joy toward me.

I know your mind is a magical display;
Ah, master of illusion, arise!
I, Ḍombī, am a woman of the town;
Do not cut off your compassion.

This means: your abiding in great equanimity toward me.

Now the meaning of the statement, "The Blessed One, through great passion and attachment together with his own consort, melts into the taste of the utterly supreme great bliss and comes to abide in the form of a seed," should be examined. Some reason as follows: "What is it that melts? First one sees that there is no ordinary body; then, through the yoga of the vajra samādhi, there is the appearance of wisdom. Is that the Blessed One, the Innate Heruka, or is it the form with sixteen arms and so on into which he is transformed? In neither case is it reasonable, because the enjoyment body and the emanation body are free of bones, marrow and the like." If they then say, "The melting is the very melting that takes place once the gandharva being has entered. Just as, in dependence on the twelve links, when mother and father unite, the semen and blood mingle, and that is melting, so it is here too. So the expression 'the Blessed One melts' means that, since it is the gandharva being that melts, the Blessed One is said to melt," that is not reasonable. If they say, "By imputation it is made nondual with the Blessed One and spoken of in that way, but in reality it is a melting," that too is not reasonable, because there is no melting or the like. Nor does the expression "falling into melting" express the birth of a being who did not exist before. Therefore, by the expression "falling into melting," one should establish not the sixteen-armed form or the like, but the Innate One. This is what Saroruhapāda stated: "comes to abide in the form of a seed."

Likewise Jālandharipāda says: Through the form of the deity there is no impure body. When there is no impure body, there is merely the imagining of a pure body. When that too is absent, one abides in the form of a consciousness that is clear and like an illusion, like a drop of melted nectar. This is the support of the aspiration of the illusion-like consciousness. Just as an illusion, though it has no knowledge of itself or of others and no correct awareness, still performs a function, so the illusion of consciousness arises through the power of performing the aims of beings, by the momentum of long-standing aspiration, like one who wakes from a dream.

Here, the sequence of arising comes about through the application of the five aspects. There, from the transformation of that illusion-like consciousness, which resembles a drop of melted nectar, arises the nature of the five consciousnesses. In it, the mirror-like wisdom, transformed from the āli, is the moon. The wisdom of equality, transformed from the kāli, is the sun. In their center is the seed that has entered. The emblem transformed from it is to be called the discriminating wisdom. The oneness of all is the accomplishing wisdom. The completed form is the pure nature of reality.

Then, with the light rays rising from the awakening mind whose nature is the five wisdoms, one draws in all that moves and does not move, and, making it of one taste with the deities of the maṇḍala in that very place, one should see the awakening mind in the form of an autumn moon disc. This is the samādhi called yoga.

After that, in an instant, one should see its transformation: the Innate One's image, in the form of the autumn moon, endowed with the Dharma seal that illuminates infinite world systems, the illusion-like, two-armed Innate Heruka whose nature is the five wisdoms. This is the samādhi called anuyoga; it is the arising.

When the anuyoga has been completed in this way, one should place on the aggregates, elements and sense sources the seeds of the goddesses from Vajrā up to Khecarī. That is, on the aggregate of form one should place the oṃ of Vajrā; on the aggregate of feeling, the i of Gaurī; on the aggregate of perception, the īṃ of Vāriyoginī; on the aggregate of formations, the uṃ of Vajraḍākinī; on the aggregate of consciousness, the aṃ of Nairātmyā. On the flesh one places the ū of Pukkasī; on the blood, the ṛṃ of Śavarī; on the awakening mind, the riṃ of Caṇḍālī; on the bones, the ḷṃ of Ḍombī.

The placement on the sense sources is also to be stated. On the sense source of form one should place the līṃ of the second Gaurī; on sound, the eṃ of Caurī; on smell, the aiṃ of Vetālī; on taste, the oṃ of Ghasmarī; on touch, the auṃ of Bhūcarī; on the element of phenomena, the aṃ of Khecarī. In the ninth chapter too the Blessed One taught the passage beginning, "The aggregate of form becomes Vajrā." This is the placement.

This very sequence of realization is also given in the root tantra:

First, emptiness, awakening;
Second, the gathering of the seed;
Third, the image completed;
Fourth, the placing of the letters.

In order to hold a body of nirvāṇa, and in order not to abandon the beings to be trained, one completes the atiyoga of placing the seeds on the aggregates and the rest, and then one should properly begin the mahāyoga. At the navel, the heart, the throat and the forehead there are four lotuses. One imagines them with sixty-four, eight, sixteen and thirty-two petals respectively, arranges aṃ hūṃ oṃ haṃ upon them, and should imagine the blessing of body, speech and mind.

One imagines a white letter oṃ at the head; from it hosts of body vajras fill the sky; they enter that very seed, and from this it becomes the body vajra. One recites oṃ kāya vajrātmakāḥ sarvadharmmaḥ kāya vajrātmako'haṃ.

One imagines a red letter āḥ on the path of speech; from it hosts of speech vajras fill the sky; they enter that very seed, and from this it becomes the speech vajra. One recites oṃ vāka vajrātmakāḥ sarvadharmmaḥ vāka vajrātmako'haṃ.

One imagines a black letter hūṃ at the heart; from it hosts of mind vajras fill the sky; they enter that very seed, and from this it becomes the mind vajra. One recites oṃ catti vajrātmakāḥ sarvadharmmaścatti vajrātmako'haṃ.

In the chapter on mantras, too, the Blessed One said, "oṃ āḥ hūṃ is the mantra that blesses body, speech and mind." This is the samādhi called mahāyoga, and the culmination of these is the placement. For the Blessed Lady, the placement is just as it was done here, with the portions of the five families added besides.

In the sequence stated earlier, from among the four forms of Heruka, one should see one's chosen deity, oneself in union with the wisdom consort, black in color, greatly terrifying, whose nature is aversion. The sequence stated earlier is from the words:

One should meditate on the lord of the maṇḍala
Arisen from the image of the being.

Then, imagining the emblems and the rest in the sequence that will be explained, one should apply the three samayas. Of these, oneself is the very form of the samaya Vajra Holder. At one's own heart, on a sun, one should imagine the wisdom samaya, just like oneself, the samaya being; and at its heart, the samādhi samaya, the letter hūṃ. This is the nature of aversion. Having thus become the nature of the three samayas, one should be endowed with the portions of the five families.

Having found a wisdom consort, unwavering,
Respectful toward the practitioner, gentle and kind,
Seeking the benefit of all beings,
Endowed with all the marks,

one places upon her head the letter oṃ, at her heart the letter hūṃ, at her navel the letter svā, on her two thighs the letter āḥ, and on her feet the letter hā.

The placement on the limbs of the Blessed Lady is also to be stated:

The aggregate of form becomes Vajrā;
Feeling is held to be Gaurī;
Perception is Vāriyoginī;
Formations, Vajraḍākinī;
In the manner of the consciousness aggregate
Abides Nairātmyā Yoginī.
Earth is said to be Pukkasī;
The water element is held to be Śavarī;
Fire is to be known as Caṇḍālī;
Wind is renowned as Ḍombī.
For form, Gaurī is always named;
For sound, Caurī is renowned;
For the portion of smell, Vetālī;
For taste, Ghasmarī is renowned;
Touch is said to be Bhūcarī;
For the element of phenomena, Khecarī.

Having thus properly placed the seeds of these goddesses, one should bless the lotus and the vajra.

Imagining that the lotus, by means of the syllable aḥ, bestows joy, one should visualize the syllable hūṃ in the opening of the anthers of the lotus chamber. After that, one should meditate on a vajra arisen from the syllable hūṃ, imagine the syllable hūṃ in the opening of its jewel, and then once again bless them with the mantras oṃ padma sukhādhara mahārāga sukhandada caturānanda bhaga biśva hūṃ hūṃ karyaṃ kuruṣva me and oṃ vajra mahādveṣa caturānanda dāyaka khagamukhaigaraso nātha hūṃ hūṃ kāryaṃ kuruṣva me.

If there is no outer consort, one obtains a consort emanated by the mind, makes her too, just as at first, endowed with the portions of the five families, and then one should begin the passionate union. Having recited oṃ sarva tathāgatānurāgata vajra svabhāvātmakāssarvva dharmmaḥ oṃ sarva tathāgatānurāgata vajra svabhāvātmako'haṃ, one utters the syllable hūṃ with the pride of the speech vajra and should set the vajra in motion. Everything from the devotion to emptiness up to the bliss of joy is the samādhi called the first union.

Then, imagining the awakening mind, from which arises the great bliss of the single taste of wisdom and means, as radiating in the form of the syllable phaṭ, one should make offering with this: oṃ sarva tathāgata pūjā vajra svabhāvātmakaḥ sarva dharmmāḥ oṃ sarva tathāgata pūjā vajra svabhāvātmako'haṃ. The yogin holding the garuḍa mudrā takes up with the tongue that awakening mind within the lotus chamber, and should make offering to himself as the nature of all the tathāgatas and of Vajrayoginī. If there is no well-trained consort, this is simply not to be done; otherwise it becomes a root transgression.

After that, from the transformation of whatever subtle particles of the awakening mind remain, one should meditate on the Lady together with the circle; this comes about through the practice of churning what is to be churned. One should emit them with these mantras: the hūṃ of the Vajra Holder, the o of the Blessed Lady, the gaṃ of Gaurī, the caṃ of Caurī, the baṃ of Vetālī, the ghaṃ of Ghasmarī, the paṃ of Pukkasī, the śaṃ of Śavarī, the caṃ of Caṇḍālī, and the ḍaṃ of Ḍombī. Once emitted, they act for the benefit of beings; then they are drawn back and enter by the path of the ḍāka of the wheel of the Blessed One, and, like milk mingling with water, the Blessed One abides in the manner that will be explained. One should imagine the Blessed Lady and the Blessed One, and also Gaurī and the others, each on their own seats. This is the emission.

Of this form, the sixteen arms are the purity of the sixteen emptinesses. In the eight lotus vessels held in the eight right hands are, in order, an elephant, a horse, a donkey, an ox, a camel, a human, a śarabha and a cat. Likewise, in the eight lotus vessels of the left hands, in order, are to be known earth, water, fire, wind, the moon, the sun, Yama and Dhanada.

Cough, and likewise labored breath,
Madness, wasting, leprosy and swollen legs,
With the nature of spleen and of liver:
All of these are well known.

The holding of the elephant and the rest comes from the purity of these eight afflictions. That of earth and the rest, up to Dhanada, comes from the purity of the eight masteries, namely:

Mastery of body and mastery of speech,
And likewise mastery of mind,
Mastery of miraculous power and of going everywhere,
Desire, and the agent; the eighth is quality.

An infinite body is mastery of body. Speaking in the languages of all is mastery of speech. Knowing the minds of all sentient beings is mastery of mind. Seeing infinite world systems through miraculous power is mastery of miraculous power. A body that is one with the three worlds is mastery of going everywhere. By the word ācchi desire is meant: producing the great bliss of beings through desire is mastery of desire. The agent, called the great Vajradhara, is mastery of agency. Acting in the form of the deity is mastery of qualities. The lotus vessels come from the purity of the aspect of compassion, and the arms from the purity of emptiness; by this it is shown that emptiness and compassion are inseparable.

From the mind of loving-kindness, the limbs are black.
The eight faces are the eight liberations,

that is, they arise from their purity. In order to accomplish rites of hostility, the first face is black. For the sake of subjugation, the left face is red. The white one on the right accomplishes pacification. In order to destroy aversion and the like once they have arisen, the upper face is grimacing and smoke-colored. In order to conquer the four māras, the two faces on each side, right and left, are again black. All the faces bare their fangs and have crooked brows; they blaze like the terrifying fire at the end of time, have turbulent light, and are greatly terrifying, because he is wrathful by nature. Each face has three red, round eyes. They are red because of compassion. They are three because, with the three eyes of the three vajras, he fully knows the three times. In this way he is anointed with twenty-four eyes.

His hair, bound with silk streamers and standing upright, is tawny, because it burns away all the afflictions, attachment and the rest, without remainder. Because he acts for the benefit of beings, the crown of his head is marked with a crossed vajra. In order to realize the absence of intrinsic nature of the five aggregates, his forehead is adorned with a garland of five dry heads. From the purity of the five tathāgatas he is endowed with the five seals: the wheel, the earrings, the necklace and the girdle. From the purity of Vajrasattva there is also the ash, and so they are well known as the six seals. In order to realize that there is no objectification of any phenomenon, he is naked, from the purity of the wisdom of perfect awakening. His four legs are the four means of gathering disciples. Because he has fully attained the awakening mind, he stands on a sun disc with two of his legs in the half-crossed stance of the tāṇḍava dance.

In order to realize the entire three realms as a single body, he plants only the big toe of his foot. In just that way, because obstructors are destroyed, he holds his left leg drawn in. From the purity of the letters of the vowels and consonants, a garland of fresh heads, adorned with the fifty letters and streaming with blood, is set upon his shoulders; because this indicates the path of union, the garland of heads is on his shoulders.

Graceful, heroic, repulsive,
fierce, laughing and terrifying,
compassionate, wondrous and peaceful:
he is endowed with the nine sentiments of the dance.

Thus it is said. Of these, his being of one taste with Nairātmyā is the graceful. His dwelling in the charnel ground is the heroic. His wrathful frown and bared fangs are the repulsive. His blazing light is the fierce. His wide-open face is the laughing. His garland of fresh heads is the terrifying. His mind, which cares for sentient beings, is the compassionate. His illusory form is the wondrous. His having abandoned the afflictions, attachment and the rest, is the peaceful. He is endowed with these.

One should see oneself as the Blessed One, his body sporting in the form of a sixteen-year-old, in union with Nairātmyā. As the Blessed One is, so too is the Blessed Lady, except that her body is adorned with the five seals and a garland of dry human heads. In her left hand she holds a skull cup filled with the blood of gods and asuras, and in order to cut off all afflictions without exception she holds a curved knife. In order to bring the three realms into a single face, she has one face. She fully savors the taste of the Blessed One's lower body. With her left leg extended, one should imagine her in union with the Blessed One in the joy of supreme great bliss.

From the purity of the six perfections, Hevajra is six-armed and three-faced: his root face is black and his left face red. With two of his left hands he holds a trident and a vajra bell, and with two of his right hands a vajra and a curved knife. With his remaining hands, holding a curved knife and a skull cup, he is in union with Vajraśṛṅkhalā, and he tramples a corpse. So one should see him.

Again, from the purity of the four māras, Hevajra is four-armed: with his right hand he holds a vajra, and with his left a skull cup of blood. With his remaining hands he is in union with Vajravārāhī. One should imagine him one-faced and trampling a corpse.

Again, the two-armed Hevajra holds a skull cup and a khaṭvāṅga with his left hand and a blazing vajra with his right, and tramples a corpse. So one should see him.

The wisdom consort of these, the six-armed one and the rest, is Vajrā. The remainder is as for the sixteen-armed one: their body color, ornaments and so on, and their arising from hūṃ and so on, should be seen in that form. Likewise, Vajraśṛṅkhalā and the others are also like Nairātmyā, differing only in name. Their abiding in an embrace with a khaṭvāṅga should be seen as common to them all.

Having imagined oneself as the Blessed One thus arisen, in the form of one's own chosen deity, through churning, from the practice of churning, the wheel of goddesses is first to be emitted. There, the sequence of their directions, east and so on, their body colors, their emblems and the rest, should be understood by way of their purities.

In the eastern gate, in order to awaken those to be tamed who are māra-like beings, is Gaurī, black, born from the syllable gaṃ. In order to cut off wrong views she holds a curved knife in her right hand; in order to destroy the habitual tendencies of saṃsāra she holds a rohita fish in her left. She tramples Brahmā. One should imagine her as the nature of the aggregate of form.

In the south, in order to awaken beings of attachment, from the syllable caṃ is Caurī, red in color. In order to realize the nature of wisdom and skillful means, she holds a ḍamaru drum in her right hand; for the sake of overcoming delusion, she holds a boar in her left. She tramples Śakra (Indra). One should see her as the nature of the aggregate of feeling.

In the west, for the sake of the flourishing of sentient beings, is Vetālī, born from the syllable baṃ, golden in color. In order to realize emptiness she holds a tortoise in her right hand; in order to realize the nature of compassion she holds a lotus vessel in her left. She rides Upendra (Viṣṇu). One should contemplate her as the nature of the aggregate of perception.

In the north, for the sake of fully accomplishing the fierce rites, is Ghasmarī, born from ghaṃ, like an emerald. From the purity of aversion she holds a snake in her right hand; in order to comprehend the nature of wisdom and skillful means she holds a yogin's begging bowl in her left. She rides Rudra. One should meditate on her as the nature of the aggregate of formations.

In the northeast, Īśāna's quarter, in order to master the rites of stupefaction, is Pukkasī, born from paṃ, blue in color. In order to purify pride she holds a lion in her right hand; in order to vanquish the hosts of Māra and to cut off all enemies without exception, she holds an axe in her left. Trampling Yama, she should be known as the nature of the earth element.

In the southeast, the quarter of fire, in order fully to awaken peaceful beings, is Śabarī, white, born from the syllable śaṃ. In order fully to awaken the five wisdoms, the mirror-like and the rest, she holds a monk in her right hand; in order to comprehend nondual wisdom she holds a ringing staff in her left. She rides Kubera. One should see her as the nature of the water element.

In the southwest, Nairṛti's quarter, for the sake of fully accomplishing paralysis, is Caṇḍālī, born from the syllable caṃ, sky blue. In order fully to awaken arrogant beings she holds a wheel in her right hand; in order to cut off the element of doubting knowledge and for the sake of uprooting the stain of ignorance, she holds a plough in her left. She tramples the rākṣasa. One should meditate on her as the nature of the fire element.

In the northwest, the quarter of wind, in order to discern the various objects individually, is Ḍombinī, born from the syllable ḍaṃ, of various colors. In order fully to awaken people of malicious intent and to realize indivisible wisdom, she holds a blazing vajra in her right hand; in order to threaten all the wicked without exception, she makes the threatening gesture with her left. She rides evenly upon Vemacitra. One should imagine her as the nature of the wind element.

In the position of the wisdom seal, Nairātmyā too, having been emitted, is to be drawn back again into the Lord, and one should imagine her as having entered him.

Because of its close likeness to her emission and reabsorption, one should understand that Gaurī and the others are likewise each in turn emitted and reabsorbed. It is with reference to these that it was said, "They arise for as long as the limbs are being set out." All of them have three round red eyes, tawny hair standing straight up, and faces with bared fangs. Naked and endowed with the five seals, they stand on moon seats in the tāṇḍava posture with crossed legs: fierce women in the form of sixteen-year-olds. One should see them as emitted as the awakening mind, as having come forth properly by the path, and as joined in desire with the Blessed One.

Now I shall state the purity of the eight seats of Gaurī and the others. In order to cut off clinging to attachment, to the consort, to emission, to drinking, to eating, to sleep, to reasoning and to faith, the eight seats were taught, beginning with Brahmā and ending with Vemacitra.

Thus one should properly meditate on oneself encircled by the host of mātṛkās who have entered the wheel of the maṇḍala just described, and on the expanse of the sky pervaded by the mass of light rays radiating from one's own body; and one should summon the wisdom wheel. In the lotus at the heart of oneself as the pledge being, upon a sun resting in a skull cup arisen from hūṃ, is a blue syllable hūṃ. With the hooks of its light rays one illuminates the infinite world systems and imagines the wisdom wheel too as drawn before one. Having offered water for the face, water for the feet and so on, one worships properly and also praises. Then with jaḥ hūṃ baṃ hoḥ one summons, draws in, binds and delights them. Then, with oṃ vajra gaurī ākārṣaya jaḥ oṃ vajra caurī prabeśaya hūṃ, oṃ vajra bettālī bandhaya baṃ, and oṃ vajra ghasmarī toṣaya hoḥ, in due order, one causes the wisdom wheel to enter the pledge wheel. By this one should make firm the pride of being glorious Heruka.

Moreover, just as oneself is the pledge being, so there is the wisdom pledge being. That being so, immediately upon the entry of the wisdom wheel, one should imagine at one's own heart the wisdom pledge being, one-faced, two-armed and red, the wisdom consort of one's own light. At her heart, too, is the lord of samādhi.

Then the Blessed Lady is likewise: at the heart of her wisdom pledge being, on a lotus and moon, is a curved knife born from the syllable aṃ, and on the moon resting upon the curved knife one should see the syllable aṃ. Likewise for the eight yoginīs: at the hearts of their wisdom pledge beings, on lotuses and moons, are their emblems born from their respective seed syllables, and one should see these blessed in turn by their seed syllables resting on moons. This is the wisdom wheel.

At the time when the wisdom wheel and the pledge wheel have been made one, the five tathāgatas, drawn in by the light rays radiating from one's own body and having taken the form of glorious Heruka, together with Vajrayoginī, the great bodhisattvas, the wrathful ones and the rest, are seen directly in the sky, and one requests them to confer empowerment, with abhiṣiñcatu māṃ sarba tathāgata. Those five tathāgatas, with victorious vases and the like filled with the five nectars, say:

Just as, the moment he was born,
all the tathāgatas bathed him,
so with the pure water of the gods
I perform this bathing.

And so saying, they bathe one. This is the empowerment.

Then the pledge vajra yoginīs raise the cry jo jo. The bodhisattvas again look on with joyful nature. The wrathful ones and the rest rejoice. From the sky falls a rain of flowers of many kinds with sweet fragrance. Rains of saffron and sandalwood and rains of many kinds of jewels pour down. With the sounds of dance, music, song and praise, a great clamor arises over the infinite world systems. Upon the empowerment, the lord of the mind appears on the crown of the Blessed One, and likewise for the Blessed Lady. Gaurī and the others should be sealed by Akṣobhya, Vairocana, Ratnasambhava and Amitābha. Again, with those same buddhas, Pukkasī and the others also offer praise in song:

You, encircled by the host of yoginīs, your companions,
you who have found the supreme within your own mind,
by this you adorn all beings, moving and unmoving,
and with a mind of compassion you awaken sentient beings.
Heruka, glorious king, through the display of magical illusion
accomplish the work, and be pleased!

The tathāgatas, bodhisattvas, consorts, goddesses, wrathful ones and the rest, abiding in the sky, also offer praise with the heart mantras, the near-heart mantras and the eight-line garland mantras. Thus is the sealing.

Then, in the midst of the host of mātṛkās, the vajra holder joined with his consort completes the eating of nectar. From the syllable yaṃ comes a blue wind maṇḍala in the shape of a half moon. Above it is a red triangular fire born from the syllable raṃ. Above it, on three heads born from the syllable āḥ, is a lotus vessel, white outside and red inside, blessed by the syllable āḥ. Within it, by the mantras of the syllable buṃ and the rest, the foods, the five nectars and the five lamps are made complete and blessed by the syllable oṃ. Above it one should see a white vajra arisen from the syllable hūṃ. After that, by the very heat of the fire stirred up by the wind, it becomes like quicksilver in its savor.

Having purified it, realized it and made it blaze with the syllables oṃ āḥ hūṃ, one gladdens the infinite and boundless buddhas, bodhisattvas and the rest; the light goes to the world systems and draws in the nectar of wisdom, which enters into it. Once they are made one, it is stirred with the vajra and made of equal taste, and one should see the vajra too dissolving into the nectar. Having become cool through the union of vajra and lotus, the wisdom nectar of the tathāgatas has also entered it. Taking it up with the ladle of one's hand, blessed by the three complete realities, and drawing it in with the light rays of the vajra tongue arisen from hūṃ, one should offer that nectar as a fire offering to the samādhi pledge being, according to the division of rites such as pacifying. One imagines the same for those of the maṇḍala. This is the tasting of the nectar.

After that, the many emanated deities radiated from the lord of the maṇḍala and from those of the maṇḍala go to wherever there are any to be tamed, tame them and come back, and the emanations should be gathered into the respective bodies of the vajra holders. This is the benefit of beings. Everything from the emission onward up to the benefit of beings is the samādhi called the supreme king of the maṇḍala.

After that come the lines of Saroruhapāda:

The colors are to be distinguished
by the distinction of the rites.

This is the six limbs, concealed. Bhadrapāda made it a little clearer, saying, "the colors transformed from hūṃ, by applying them as is fitting." Jālandharipāda explained this in detail, as follows: "In the center of the union of moon and sun, having seen the seed letter of the great universal monarch of mantras, one should meditate on the six limbs." What is meant by this is as follows. In the heart of oneself as the pledge being, a mass of light rays radiates from the syllable hūṃ set in the center of a moon and sun resting on a lotus; following upon this, one should first meditate on the lord of samādhi as black. When he has transformed, one imagines him in the form of a drop, and the light rays are sent out from the pores of the Blessed One's body; having pervaded the whole maṇḍala and all those of the maṇḍala, one should see them abiding expansively. In this way, second there is red, third yellow, fourth green, fifth blue and sixth white; these should be understood as fitting for the rites of pacifying and the rest. This is the six limbs.

Having meditated on the six limbs, one should then begin the luminosity. The breath, expanded into a garland of vowels and consonants, is sent out through the pathway of the breath. Having completely purified the marks and signs of the light rays that go forth, the moving together with the unmoving becomes one. Gaurī and the rest, and Nairātmyā too, by way of entering into oneself, and whatever is imagined as the state of Vajradhara, all of it should be seen as luminosity, apart from all entities. By the stages of entering luminosity which will be explained in this very text, one imagines the coemergent Heruka, one-faced, two-armed and white in color, and one should imagine the seed syllable in his heart.

Then the stages of entering luminosity: having understood that Gaurī and the rest are emanated from the awakening mind, first Gaurī is made to enter the Blessed One's aggregate of form. Likewise Caurī, Vetālī, Ghasmarī and Nairātmyā too are made to enter, as is fitting, the aggregates of feeling, perception, formations and consciousness. Likewise Pukkasī and the rest are made to enter earth and the other elements. Such is the entry into the outer maṇḍala. Then the gradual entry of the five aggregates abiding within the Blessed One is this: the aggregate of form and the aggregate of feeling enter the aggregate of perception; that enters the aggregate of formations; and that in turn enters consciousness, which is Nairātmyā too. There, Bhūcarī and Khecarī too, having reached the two sides, go into luminosity; so it is held, until all is empty, as nirvāṇa.

Bhadrapāda said this: "The mantra practitioner should imagine the five aggregates as luminous by nature, taking in all those of the maṇḍala and withdrawing them again into his own domain. Through this, its seed letter, the moon and the sun also become mixed, and so, in the nature of nectar, in the form of a mass of light rays, like the tip of a lamp flame, one should gradually let it become unapprehended."

Here, in Jālandharipāda's explanation of Saroruhapāda, he says: "By the words 'its seed letter, the moon and the sun also become mixed,' the absence of the three wisdoms is taught. By 'in the nature of nectar' he teaches the coemergent state." This explanation of the text, with what follows, explains luminosity alone; it does not speak of the arising.

Bhadrapāda said this: "After that, from the moon, sun and seed syllable having become one, one imagines in an instant the form of a mass of blazing light rays, which gradually becomes like the tip of a lamp flame; after that, one should imagine it as like the core of a plantain tree, for as long as it takes to become without object."

At this point one should recall this:

There, there is no beginning, middle or end,
no existence and no nirvāṇa,
no self and no other:
here that is supreme great bliss.

This is the luminosity.

Then comes the arising from luminosity. That luminosity is the supreme yoga, the cause of the arising. From this very luminosity, in an instant, arises the wisdom in the form of the coemergent Heruka, bearing the thirty-two major marks and adorned with the eighty minor marks. Having imagined him as like a reflection and an illusion, and peaceful, one should meditate on the appearance of wisdom, the lord of the maṇḍala whose very nature is vajra, gathered into one. Vajradhara too has said:

Arisen from the image of the being,
one should meditate on the lord of the maṇḍala.

As the cause of mantra recitation, one first completes the Blessed One in the form of the sixteen-armed one and the rest, and should then imagine the wheel of ḍākinīs as before. Thus the stages of meditation are taught in the sequence of the four sessions: morning, afternoon and midnight.

If one tires of meditating, one should recite mantra. Its sequence is this. In the center of the wheel, in union, one meditates on oneself in the form of glorious Heruka. The mantra letters of the lord of the maṇḍala and of those of the maṇḍala emerge from the mouth of the Blessed Lady and enter the mouth of the Blessed One; by the path of the avadhūtī and the vajra path they enter the lotus, and the mantra letters, together with their light rays, pass once more from the mouth of the Blessed Lady into one's own mouth. One should recite in this very sequence, applying the colors that are distinguished by pacifying and the rest, until one is no longer weary. This is the palanquin recitation.

One should imagine, and keep imagining, at the heart, upon a sun, the mantra letters together with their light rays, their heads turned upward, encircling it in the form of a ring. This is the cluster recitation.

One should understand with the mind that all those mantra letters of the lord of the maṇḍala and of those of the maṇḍala are sent forth everywhere with a long sound. This is the pledge recitation.

The recitation of the heart mantra, the near-heart mantra, the garland mantra and the rest is also to be done according to the oral instructions. The mantras are these. oṃ deva picu vajra hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā: that of the sixteen-armed one. oṃ kiṭi kiṭi vajra hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā: that of the six-armed one. oṃ jvala jvala bhyo hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā: the four-armed one. oṃ trailokyākṣepa hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā: that of the two-armed one. These are the heart mantras of them all. Further, the near-heart mantra is oṃ vajrāya hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ.

oṃ aṣṭānanāya bidgordhakeśavartmane caturbiśatinetrāya ṣoḍaśabhujāya kṛṣṇajīmūtavabuṣa kapālamālānekadhāriṇe ādhmatakrūricittāya ardhendudaṃṣṭiṇe māraya māraya kāraya kāraya garjaya garjaya tarjaya tarjaya śoṣaya śoṣaya saptasāgarān bandha bandha nāgāṣṭakāna gṛhna gṛhna sarbaśatrūna ha hā hi hī hu hū he hai ho hau haṃ haḥ phaṭ svāhā: this is the root mantra of the Blessed One and his garland mantra.

oṃ aṣṭānanāya hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ oṃ piṃgodhakeśavartmane hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ oṃ caturbiṃśatinetrāya hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ oṃ ṣoḍaśabhujāya hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ oṃ kriṣṇajīmūtavapuṣe hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ oṃ kāpālamālānekadhāriṇi hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ oṃ ādhmatakrūracittāya hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ oṃ adhedaṃṣṭṝne hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ: this is the eight-line mantra of the Blessed One.

oṃ aṃ nairātme hūṃ phaṭ svāhā and oṃ vajraḍākiye hūṃ phaṭ svāhā: these are the heart and near-heart mantras of the Blessed Lady.

oṃ aṃ āṃ iṃ īṃ uṃ ūṃ ṛṃ ṝṃ liṃ ḹṃ e ai oṃ auṃ aṃ phaṭ svāhā: this is the root mantra of the Blessed Lady.

oṃ aṃ āṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ oṃ iṃ īṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ oṃ uṃ ūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ oṃ ṛ ṝṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ oṃ ḷṃ ḹṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ oṃ eṃ aiṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ oṃ oṃ auṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ oṃ aṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ: this is the eight-line mantra of the Blessed Lady.

On this, Jālandharipāda said: "If one is to recite the mantras of Gaurī and the rest, one should at that time embrace her, and imagine the Blessed Lady in her seat. The mantras for this are these: oṃ vajragaurīye hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā oṃ vajracaurīye hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā oṃ vajrabettālīye hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā oṃ vajraghasmarīye hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā oṃ vajrapukkasīye hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā oṃ vajraśavarīye hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā oṃ vajracaṇḍalīye hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā oṃ vajraḍombinīye hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā."

Now I shall state the reality of the torma. Through conviction in emptiness, one meditates in the form of the coemergent Heruka, and then, by the sequence explained before, one should meditate on the lord of the wrathful ones arisen from the image of the being, the eight-faced one and so on, the great terrifying one, as Dveṣavajra. Thus, while the yogin remains in the samādhi of Dveṣavajra, he sends forth the ten wrathful ones arisen from the syllable oṃ, all with wildly blazing garlands of flame, and dispatches Yamāri and the rest to the directions, east and so on. Having summoned the hosts of obstructors, Indra and the rest, he commands them with the mantra beginning oṃ sumbhani sumbha, according to the division of rites such as pacifying.

After that, one should imagine a vajra arisen from the transformation of the elephant vessel, and a vajra bell from the transformation of the earth vessel, and the others too should be purified in the same way. Thus the wise one, holding vajra and bell, should imagine three eight-petaled lotuses, one above the other, the three cities of liberation, completed from three syllables. The obstructors summoned by those very wrathful ones are to be summoned and so forth from their seeds with the mantra oṃ andi jama jāla jakka bhuta bahni bā u rakkha canda sujja māda babpa tala pātālaṃ aṭṭa sa sappa svāhā. Having done so with oṃ vajra gaurī ākārṣaya jaḥ oṃ vajra caurī padmatraye prabeśaya hūṃ oṃ vajra betālī sva sva sthāneṣu krodhabandhena bandhaye oṃ vajra ghaśmarī śaṃ ghaṇḍāya vaśaṃ kuru hoḥ, one should look upon them.

There, in the middle city, on the eastern petal, is Indra, vajra in hand, on a moon seat, radiant as the moon and adorned with all ornaments. In the south is Yama, staff in hand, on a sun seat, radiant as the sun. In the west is Varuṇa, with a water lily, on a moon seat, radiant as the moon and wearing all ornaments. In the north is the yakṣa, holding a bilva fruit in his hand, on a moon seat, radiant as the moon and wearing all ornaments.

On the petal of Īśāna is the lord of bhūtas, three-eyed, holding a trident in his hand and wearing a garment of tiger skin, his body gray with ash, adorned with all the bone ornaments, on a moon seat, radiant as the moon. On the petal of fire is Agni, the god of fire, with a great belly, holding a rosary and a water pot, on a sun seat, radiant as the sun, wearing an upper robe. On the petal of Nairṛti is the rākṣasa, holding a sword and a skull cup, wrathful and with knitted brows, upon a corpse on a sun seat, radiant as the sun. On the petal of wind is Vāyu, god of the wind, holding a banner in his hand and wearing all ornaments, on a moon seat, radiant as the moon. On the pericarp of the lotus is Pṛthivī, the earth goddess, yellow, wearing divine garments and all ornaments, a vase in her hand, on a moon seat, radiant as the moon; and the pretas too abide all around the earth goddess.

The other particulars of Indra and the rest, as explained for the protection wheel, should be understood here, and what is explained here should also be known there. The five, Indra and the rest, hold a snake noose in the left hand. Then one imagines them all standing with the right leg extended, and by the syllable hūṃ one should see them in the form of Akṣobhya.

Above, in the eight petals of the lotus resting in the source of phenomena, on the eastern petal is the Moon, white, riding a white horse, a water lily in his hand, wearing all ornaments, on a moon seat, radiant as the moon. On the southern petal is the Sun, red, holding lotuses in his hands, riding a green horse, with a jeweled crown and fully complete in all ornaments, on a sun seat, radiant as the sun. By the principle of proximity, Mercury and the rest are included with the Moon and Sun. On the western petal is Mercury, yellow, riding a yellow rat, on a sun seat, radiant as the sun, an arrow in his hand and wearing a jeweled crown. On the northern petal is Venus, blue, with a jeweled crown, holding a spear, on a sun seat, radiant as the sun. On the petal of Īśāna is Mars, red, his hair loosed and streaming upward, holding a lotus, on a sun seat, radiant as the sun. On the petal of fire is Jupiter, deep yellow, with a jeweled crown, holding a club, on a sun seat, radiant as the sun. On the petal of Nairṛti is Saturn, black, with tawny hair, holding a trident, on a sun seat, radiant as the sun. On the petal of wind are Rāhu and Ketu, deep black and smoke-colored, their hands, circling round and round, in the manner of drawing in and devouring, on a sun seat, radiant as the sun.

In the center of the pericarp of the eight-petaled lotus is Brahmā, yellow, four-faced and four-armed, holding a triple staff and making the gesture of fearlessness, while with his remaining hands he holds a rosary and a water pot; mounted, on a moon seat, radiant as the moon, wearing the sacred thread, short and stout of body, with matted locks. One imagines the Brahmakāyika gods, the Brahmapurohita gods, the Mahābrahmā gods and the rest, up to Akaniṣṭha, abiding all around Brahmā, and then by the syllable oṃ one should see them completed in the form of the body vajra.

After that, below, in the eight petals of the lotus resting in the source of phenomena, in the order of the directions and intermediate directions, are these: the nāga Vāsuki, yellow; the nāga Padma, white; the nāga Karkoṭa, red; the nāga Takṣaka, black; the nāga Śaṅkhapāla, yellow; the nāga Mahāpadma, blue-green; the nāga Ananta, white; the nāga Kulika, multicolored. These eight too have jeweled crowns and human faces, wear all ornaments and are adorned with hoods over their heads, together with the asuras and the hell beings. On its pericarp are the remaining nāgas, white, with jeweled crowns and human faces with hoods. Having established them firmly in this way, from the transformation of the syllable āḥ one should see them in the form of the speech vajra, and apply the form of glorious Heruka to them all. Or else, for the sake of all the rites, the arms, faces, body colors and so on are taught: in the rites of pacifying and the rest, as one's own form is, so should theirs be.

Having thus imagined everything, one should offer water for the face and the rest to those who have attained the form of body, mind and speech vajra, glorious Heruka. That is: purifying sprinkling on the head with a bundle of kuśa grass; foot water at the feet with the mudrā; covering with the right hand the conch held in the left hand and sprinkling water to rinse the mouth; and the face water on the head with the mudrā, three times. Inserting arghaṃ and the rest between oṃ pravarasatkāra and pratīccha svāhā, one offers everything with this and those, and then with this: idaṃ baliṃ bhuñja jidgha phulla duppa māṃsa piṃgha ambha kājja sāppa sādha khanti khuṇi pheḍa gāda. The first half is the food to be eaten, and the second half is the task as well.

After that, the mantra practitioner holding vajra and vajra bell, with gestures such as the lotus turning, first twirls the vajra and rings the bell in the eight places, and should then give the torma made into nectar according to the oral instructions. Saying oṃ akāro mukhaṃ sarbadharmāṇāmādyanutpannatvat āḥ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā, one satisfies them all with this very mantra; then, between sessions, one should also sing a melodious song, praise with the root mantra, and recite the hundred-syllable mantra.

The root mantra for this is the one beginning oṃ aṣṭānanāya.

For this, the hundred-syllable mantra is this: oṃ śrī heruka samaya manupālaya heruka tvenopatiṣṭha dṛḍho me bhava sutoṣyo me bhava supoṣyo me bhava anurakto me bhava sarba siddhimme prayaccha sarba karmmasu ca me cittaṃ śreyaḥ kuru hūṃ ha ha ha ha hoḥ bhagavān sarba tathāgata bajra mā me muñca heruko bhava mahāsamayasatva āḥ hūṃ phaṭ.

After that, having made the blessing of truth, one should, out of their loving care, make the request for one's desired aims, and then request them to depart. Here the blessing of truth is this:

The goddesses are valid, the pledge is valid,
and what they have spoken is pure and the supreme of valid words:
by this truth, may those goddesses
become the cause of their taking me into their care.

Asking for their loving care for me is this:

Protectors who have destroyed attachment to concepts, devoted to the sameness of existence and peace,
who see all things as like space in their essential nature,
whose minds are vast, moistened by the water of the weightiest compassion:
goddesses, please, please care for me with love.

The departure is this: oṃ bajra muḥoṃ āḥ hūṃ phaṭ phaṭ phaṭ. Of these, the syllable oṃ is aspiration; the syllable āḥ is delight; the syllable hūṃ brings satisfaction; and with the three syllables phaṭ they are to be withdrawn.

O monks, if yogins, with this torma,
make offering to all the bhūtas,
then those become stainless and happy,
among beings there is prosperity, and the gods too rejoice.
For subjugation, fierce rites, destroying hostile armies,
expulsion, killing and summoning,
pacifying, increasing and happiness, for these aims,
always give torma to the hosts of bhūtas.

This is the samādhi called the Supreme King of Activities.

Then the mantrin abiding in the center of the wheel should visualize, at the navel, the heart, likewise the throat, and the forehead, in that order, four lotuses with sixty-four petals, eight, likewise sixteen, and thirty-two, and then visualize the seed syllable at the navel. Caṇḍālī, in form blazing like the tip of a lamp flame, burns the five tathāgatas residing in the wheel of Dharma, and Locanā and the others as well; and from her burning also the emerging and the drawing in of the āli and kāli, the wheel of enjoyment, the wheel of great bliss, and the syllable haṃ whose form is purity, one then takes up the awakening mind that has become the cause of great bliss, makes it thus of one taste with the Blessed One, and again taking up the moon, the sun and the skull cup, one should cause them to enter fully into the seed syllable at the heart. That blazing seed syllable together with the nāda has become the embodiment of the five tathāgatas, as the Blessed One said:

The bindu becomes Vajrasattva;
the half-moon is held to be Vairocana;
the line is the Lord of Jewels himself;
the syllable ha is Amitābha;
and the syllable u is Amoghasiddhi:
knowing this, it is the embodiment of the five Victors.

Again, drawing the nāda out like the tip of a lamp flame, the syllable u, whose nature is envy, is withdrawn into the syllable ha, whose nature is attachment. After that, the syllable ha into the line, whose nature is avarice. Then the line into the half-moon, whose nature is delusion. The half-moon into the bindu, whose nature is aversion; and the bindu should be withdrawn into the nāda, and the nāda should be meditated upon as a subtle tip. When by this the mind has become stable, one should spontaneously emanate the array of the tathāgatas, the net of yoginīs, and the circle of the maṇḍala, extensively pervading the infinite world-systems.

This very point was taught in a hidden way by Jālandharipāda: Rising from this, one should visualize a multicolored lotus in the center of the dharma. On its eight petals are the eight, and the Lord as well; and at his heart one should visualize the seed syllable. From the seed syllable one should meditate, at the last, on the tip of the nāda, and in order to make the mind stable one should contemplate the end of the subtle tip. Have no doubt that by this, through the co-emergent, the mind becomes stable. As soon as the mind is stable, one should emanate the array of the tathāgatas and the net of yoginīs, most extensively pervading the infinite world-systems. From what? It is emanated from one's own sign: "the inconceivable protector, the embodiment of the maṇḍala." The word dharma indicates two meanings; this is to be understood from the view of the oral instructions. The sign should be visualized as about the size of a large mustard seed. This is explained as the samādhi called Subtle Yoga.

Having risen from this, if the yogin practices the yoga of two-armed Hevajra, he should abide having put on the two armors. Here Akṣobhya, in the form of a wheel, is hūṃ on the head. Amitābha, in the form of divine earrings, is hrī on the ears. The Lord of Jewels, in the form of a necklace, is trāṃ at the throat. Vairocana, in the form of bracelets, is oṃ on the hands. Amoghasiddhi, in the form of a girdle, is khaṃ at the waist. Vajrasattva, in the form of ash, is haṃ on all the limbs. Thus one should visualize the armor. On the eyes is Mohavajrā, aṃ. On the ears is Dveṣavajrā, āṃ. On the nose, Mātsaryavajrā is explained as iṃ. On the mouth is Rāgavajrā, uṃ. On the mind is Nairātmyā Yoginī, aṃ. "With the armors, the great being should purify the senses": this is the second placement.

Having donned on the body this armor, accomplished from the tathāgatas and Vajrayoginī, the yogin should abide like a lion through the yoga of two-armed Hevajra, within the celestial palace of the three worlds. So too Nāgārjunapāda said:

These are not the three realms; this is a many-storied mansion; these are not living creatures; these are Victors.
I am not human; I am the lord of the wheel; these are not sense objects, and the faculties are not earth and the rest.
The self is not form and the rest, nor even the nature of reality; therefore they belong to the maṇḍala.
Since all this variety is gathered into the circle of the maṇḍala, O mind, why should you be deluded?

That is the abiding.

After that, whatever food, hard or soft, or drink and other substances the yogin has come by, he should purify as before, bless with the three syllables, and eat by the rite of tasting the nectar. That is eating.

Following the root tantra, it should be stated with regard to staying and wandering, as follows:

Greed, delusion, fear and anger,
and acts done out of shame, are to be abandoned.
Giving up sleep himself,
he should practice the conduct without doubt.
Having first given this very body as a gift,
afterwards he should fully undertake the conduct.
Having discerned the fortunate and the unfortunate,
he then gives, or does not give.
Food, hard and soft, and likewise drink,
should be eaten just as it is obtained.
Because desirable and undesirable are concepts,
he should not take the side of grasping.
The five families, in union,
should be regarded as a single family.
Because of the multiplicity of families,
he should not produce distinctions accordingly.
At a solitary tree or in a charnel ground,
likewise at night in a shrine of the mātṛkās,
or else in a place without people
or in a remote region, meditation is declared virtuous.
If one wishes to practice the conduct,
it is once a little warmth has been obtained.
If one has the wish that accomplishment should come,
the conduct is to be practiced by this.

So it says at length. "Practice" means conduct and thorough conduct. The expression "staying" is a synonym for "thorough conduct." With reference to having obtained a little warmth, it is also said: All yogins should practice intensively at night in their own dwelling, until something has been obtained. From what is this known? From the words, "he should not produce distinctions accordingly." Once it has been obtained, doing or not doing is up to oneself. This is the conduct.

Then, wishing to sleep in the interval between sessions, one should turn one's attention to the yoga of glorious Heruka and lie down like a lion. That is sleeping.

At the time of rising, too, when one has risen, roused by the songs of Pukkasī and the others, then by the very sequence explained before, everything from the wheel of offerings and so on up to the end of the conduct is to be done in three sessions, until the aspect of the maṇḍala circle has been made directly perceptible. From that, the beginner yogin, as lord of the eighth ground, goes to and comes from the buddha fields. The Blessed One also said:

As long as one is engaged in meditation,
so long is one a beginner.
Even through the practice of a beginner
the eighth ground will be attained.

That is rising again.

By these, the thirty-two topics of the sādhana have been explained. What are these topics? They are these: the wheel of offerings, the four abodes of Brahmā, the supreme, the co-emergent, one's chosen deity, the protection wheel, devotion to emptiness, the charnel ground, the many-storied mansion, the causal maṇḍala, the falling of the melted drop, the arising, the placement, the nature of aversion, the emanation, the wisdom wheel, the empowerment, the sealing, the tasting of the nectar, the benefit of beings, the six limbs, luminosity, the arising, mantra recitation, the reality of the torma, the subtle yoga, the second placement, the abiding, eating, conduct, sleeping, and rising again.

Next, the completion stage is to be written. This is the meaning of what was said: "The word dharma indicates two meanings; this is to be understood according to the oral instructions." The instruction is this: in order to stabilize the mind through the yoga of the generation stage, one should visualize at the aperture of the vajra a sign about the size of a large mustard seed, and contemplate the lord of the maṇḍala. By this very sequence, the meditator on the completion stage, without depending on the moon, the sun, the skull cup, the seed syllable and so forth, completes himself in an instant into the complete form of his chosen deity, and then should visualize a mustard seed above the dharma, at the tip of the nose. "Mustard seed" refers to the conceptual activity of the breath, which by the sequence of its movement through day and night comes to twenty-one thousand six hundred. By that very sequence of meditating on it until it reaches its utmost culmination, in order to stop the movement of the breath, one stops it, together with its eighty natures, by way of holding and so on, through the mode of a non-affirming negation. Then, again, there arises Vajradhara, the body of emanating and gathering; this is the embodiment of non-affirming and affirming negation. Through that and this, one is fully established as the lord of the eighth ground.

Jālandharipāda said this: After that, one should imagine it going upward from the gaze. As one wishes, one should imagine drawing it in through the nose, without an object of focus. Just so, when letting it out, it goes downward for as long as there is no object of focus. The words "as one wishes" mean realization; such an instruction is extremely secret. That is vajra recitation.

By this the following is taught: through the generation and completion stages, relying on the realization that body, speech and mind are false, one enters the awakening mind in which emptiness and compassion are inseparable. By that, self-blessing too is to be understood. In the Hevajra it is also said: Vajragarbha asked, "By what means is the awakening mind to be generated?" The Blessed One said:

By the means of the maṇḍala circle and so on,
and by the stage of self-blessing.

That is vajra recitation, the second stage.

Now the stage of self-blessing is to be understood. Here, once one has understood the means of the maṇḍala circle and so on, the false body, speech and mind are what the word "self" expresses; and their blessing is this: the inseparable body and the rest, arisen in a way in which they are not separate from one another; the form adorned with the thirty-two major marks and the eighty minor marks; the house of bliss without an object of focus; that which is characterized by special joy; illusion-like wisdom; the pure wisdom of co-emergent joy. That is called self-blessing, the complete enjoyment.

If one asks how its body and the rest are inseparable: it is said in the fourth chapter of the second part:

If it is set apart, the body abandoned,
the mind does not become beautiful;
and if it is set apart, the mind abandoned,
the body is not seen to abide.

It is also said in the Glorious Samāja: "You should generate mind in the aspect of body, body in the aspect of mind, and mind through the utterance of speech."

Āryadevapāda too said, in the Heart of the Path:

Whatever comes about through one's own self,
that here is self-blessing.
Definitively liberated through nonduality itself,
it is expressed by the word "self."

Jālandharipāda too, drawing it from the chapter on the families, called it "complete enjoyment":

Great wisdom abides in the body,
having abandoned all conceptual thought.
It is what pervades all things;
though it abides in the body, it is not born of the body.

In the eighth chapter the Blessed One said:

This self-awareness is wisdom,
the domain beyond the path of speech.
Because by this comes the stage of blessing,
it is the nature of omniscient wisdom.

Self-blessing itself the Blessed One taught under other names, in view of the variety of those to be taught. In the tenth chapter:

That itself, in the mode of consciousness,
the Person, the Ancient One, the Lord,
the self, the life principle, the being,
time, and the individual as well:
this, the nature of all things,
truly abides in illusory form.

In the noble Laṅkāvatāra it is also said:

The person, the continuum, and the aggregates,
conditions, atoms, and likewise
the Principal, the Lord, and creators:
these I declare to be mind only.

Here it is said: "Great wisdom abides in the body." The body here is the emanation. Abiding in that body: the wisdom characterized by self-blessing comes to abide in the body. Because it fully realizes all objects of focus, its true aspect is this. From not fully knowing it, one takes birth in the aggregates. Why? Because when the aggregates of earlier and earlier habitual tendencies have ceased, one grasps the habitual tendencies through co-emergent joy, and there arises the wisdom characterized by special joy, the antidote to the intermediate existence. Then, seeing the union of the lotus and the vajra of wisdom and method, birth comes from taking up desire: this is supreme joy, whose nature is without a grasper. Then, by being grasped, there is the joy of the empty as well. Then wind arises; then fire; then earth. Then again the five aggregates, those that come first, and the sense objects arise in order.

This is said in the Glorious Hevajra Tantra. Vajragarbha asked, speaking thus: "O Blessed One, in that body, how does the great wisdom, pure by nature, come to have the nature of the elements?" The Blessed One's answer: it comes from the joining and touching of bola and kakkola. Pure and from the very beginning without intrinsic nature, here the Blessed One, the Vajra Holder, spoke it, giving bliss to the ḍākinīs:

The self, whose nature is free of shyness,
all of it abides in the body.

"O Blessed One, why are there the aggregates of the great elements?" The Blessed One said:

From bola joined with kakkola,
by touch, through the quality of hardness,
earth comes to arise from that.
From the awakening mind in the form of water
the water element too arises.
From the friction, warmth arises;
by movement, it is renowned as wind;
by bliss, the space element too.
It is surrounded by the five.

The Blessed One stated this clearly as follows:

From the nature of the five elements
the union, kunduru, becomes fivefold.
The single great joy itself,
by division, goes into five.
From bola joined with kakkola,
from the habitual tendency of hardness through touch,
because hardness is the nature of delusion,
delusion is Vairocana.
Because from the moisture of the awakening mind
moisture is held to be the water element,
and because water is the very form of Akṣobhya,
aversion is the guide Akṣobhya.
From the union of the movement of the two,
warmth constantly arises.
Attachment is Amitavajra;
attachment comes about from warmth.
Whatever mind is in the kakkola,
that has the nature of wind.
Envy is Amoghasiddhi;
Amogha arises from wind.
Bliss, attachment, turns into blood;
joy is characterized as space.
Space becomes Mātsaryavajra;
avarice has arisen from space.
Mind, which is one and great,
is indicated by the forms of five.
From those very five families
many thousands have arisen.
Therefore these are of a single nature,
great bliss, supremely beneficial.
The five minds, attachment and the rest,
come to be divided into five.

Then, furthermore, the elements, aggregates, sense objects and so on are without intrinsic nature, that is, from the very beginning unarisen in their essential nature. In the Hevajra too it is said:

By nature unborn from the very beginning,
neither false nor true, and likewise
all are like the moon in water:
through desire, yoginī, know this.

It is like this: from the fire drill, the base, and the effort of a person's hands, fire suddenly comes to arise. This fire does not abide in the drill, nor in the base, nor in the person's hands either; searched for in every way, it is not found in any single one, and so that fire is neither true nor false. In that way, yoginī, contemplate all phenomena. Therefore, when it is purified: for it is said in the chapter on empowerment,

Afterwards its reality is rightly explained,
having the nature of pure wisdom.

Again, in this same text it is said:

First, joy is the form of beings;
likewise supreme joy is of beings;
the joy of cessation too is of beings;
in these three there is no co-emergent.

The explanation of this by the oral instruction is: because it lacks characteristics it is false; that is, the wisdom of beings arises. Regarding the first, this is said by taking it as distinctive. The wisdom of supreme joy is the meaning of grasping, focusing on things as object grasped and grasper. The joy of cessation too is stated by this and that, because it is the very side opposed to mind, mental factors and ignorance.

This awakening mind is the self-aware wisdom of the yogin. If one asks, "If it belongs to the yogin endowed with wisdom, then how are ordinary people to know it? Is there anywhere it can be realized?", it shall be stated: having made directly perceptible, through serving the guru, the aspect of the joy of entering into union, one should know it elsewhere too, in the objects such as form. This is said in the Hevajra:

The syllable e, of lovely shape,
adorned in its center with vaṃ,
the abode of all bliss,
is the jewel casket of the buddhas.
From the half divided by the moments,
joy comes to arise in it.
Knowing bliss through knowing the moments,
it abides in the syllables evaṃ.
Variety and maturation,
consummation, and likewise freedom from characteristics:
relying on the four moments,
thus the yogin comes to know.
Embracing, kissing and the like
are explained as variety, the various aspects.
Maturation is the reverse of that:
the eating of the wisdom of bliss.
The view, "I have eaten bliss,"
is declared to be consummation.
Freedom from characteristics is other than the three,
having abandoned both passion and passionlessness.
Variety is the first joy;
in maturation there is supreme joy;
in consummation, the joy of cessation;
co-emergent joy is in freedom from characteristics.
Master, secret, and wisdom,
and the fourth is likewise.
By the count of the four empowerments
joy and the rest are to be known in order.
Laughing is purified as the master;
gazing, likewise, as the secret;
in holding hands there is wisdom itself;
in the embrace of the pair, suchness itself.
For the accomplishment of sentient beings
empowerment is explained as fourfold.
Because of what is called sprinkling and washing,
by that it is called empowerment.

Now the characteristics of joy and the rest are to be stated:

The first is the wish for touch;
the second is the wish for bliss;
the third, because it destroys passion;
and the fourth is not to be meditated upon.

This itself:

Through joy there is a little bliss;
supreme joy exceeds it;
the joy of cessation becomes free of passion;
the remainder is co-emergent joy.

Thus Āryadevapāda:

Oh, bliss! How wonderful, bliss!
However one enjoys it,
the mind supremely desires;
it is merely the joy of cessation.

By this the following is taught. By the very bliss that is perceived in the joy of cessation, characterized as the side opposed to ignorance, the bliss of the example, which follows somewhat like a shadow, makes manifest, in just the way the form of an image is perceived in a mirror, the one who bears the thirty-two marks and is adorned with the eighty minor marks, as the wisdom of complete enjoyment. This is to be learned from the mouth of the vajra master. Then the Blessed One said:

The Teacher with the thirty-two marks,
the all-pervading lord endowed with the eighty minor marks.

So too Nāgārjunapāda:

In a stainless mirror
one should look clearly at the reflection.
Liberated from entity and non-entity,
the vajra mind, well drawn forth:
one should rely on an excellent guru;
that is to be called self-blessing.

So too Āryadevapāda said it, and Jālandharipāda also wrote it in his commentary, the Vajra Lamp, as follows:

Just as in a perfectly clear mirror
a reflection appears as a form complete in all its limbs,
so the body of Vajradhara, rich with the marks
and the eighty minor marks, is permanent itself.

That is self-blessing, the third stage.

Now the stage of purification is to be stated. Again, by the sequence of the empowerments of the garland, the water and so on, by these words,

With thumb and ring finger
one should let it fall into the disciple's mouth,

one confers the secret empowerment and the others, and then the third, through special joy, causes the aspect of the joy of blessing to be realized. By the words, "and the fourth is likewise," its purification is through the mode of a non-affirming negation. The purification of suchness is twofold: the purification as deities of the aggregates and so on, both form and what is not form; and the other is the co-emergent form whose nature is self-awareness, which is the purification of the three joys, antidotes to the grasped object and so on, that have attained the characteristic of self-blessing; that is, suchness, whose nature is co-emergent joy. The Blessed One said this:

All that is known as entities
is declared to be pure as suchness.
Afterwards, by individual division,
it is to be expressed through the deities.

It is said:

The five aggregates, the six faculties,
the six sense sources and the five great elements
are pure by nature in their characteristics;
the obscurations of unknowing and the afflictions are to be purified.
Self-awareness itself is purity;
one is not liberated by any other purity.
Because the objects are the very nature of purity,
supreme bliss is self-awareness.

By this the following is taught: relying on the generation stage, the aggregate of form and the others are said to be purified by the goddesses Vajrā and the rest. The purification of the three joys that have attained the characteristic of self-blessing is the meaning of the words, "of self-aware nature, likewise fully entered from that"; by this all nirvāṇa is said to be empty. The chapter on reality also says, "The glorious one, gone as suchness," and so on.

Again:

In essence there is no form and no seeing,
no sound and no hearer either,
no smell and no smeller either,
no taste and no taster either,
no mind and nothing arisen from mind.

In the chapter on empowerment it is also said:

In supreme joy there is no meditation and no meditator either;
there is no body, no object grasped, and no grasper either;
there is no flesh or blood, and no excrement or urine either;
no vomit, rage or stupor, no hindrance for the sake of purity or worship;
no passion, no hatred, no delusion, no envy;
no avarice, no pride, and nothing to be seen;
no meditation and no meditator, no friend and no foe:
the co-emergent, free of shyness, is variety itself.

If one asks how this too is to be known, in this same text it is said:

The co-emergent is not told by another;
it is not found anywhere at all.
It is to be known from attending on the lineage of gurus
and from one's own merit.

Even so, in what is one to have confidence regarding the co-emergent? It shall be stated: because it follows like a shadow the co-emergent of entering into union, the co-emergent is to be known from the guru's instruction. The Blessed One said this: "There its reality is to be taught: the supreme end, cessation first, having the supreme end." That is: that of which cessation is first is "cessation first," and that is the co-emergent. "Ender" means "having an end." The supreme is the utmost end; that is what it is. That which is both "cessation first" and "having the supreme end" is "cessation first, having the supreme end."

Wholly hidden in all the tantras,
the end of ends is made fully clear.
Having fully performed the vajra worship,
the goddess then asked him:
"How is the moment of that god?
Great Lord, please explain."
"In it there is no beginning, middle or end,
no existence and no nirvāṇa,
no self and no other."
Here too the Blessed One said:
"I shall explain the conduct of the disciple
who requests empowerment.
Seeing the guru endowed with the mudrā,
the one under vows should praise and worship him:
O Blessed One, great Peaceful One,
devoted solely to vajra yoga,
accomplisher inseparable from the mudrā,
arisen from vajra yoga:
just as you are great of soul,
do the same task for me as well.
Within the mass of saṃsāra's mire
I am sinking without a refuge: protect me!
Delicious food and drink,
bala, the supreme mahā, and madana,
incense, food offerings and garlands,
bells, banners and unguents:
with these offerings the excellent disciple
worships the vajra holder."

Without interval after the offering, the one who teaches should by this make co-emergent joy understood:

Because it is not to be meditated on by the mind,
all beings are to be meditated on.
When all phenomena are fully known,
meditation itself is not meditation.

This and this are also the reality of the stable and the moving. That is: "all, all is empty." The Blessed One said this:

Whatever things are stable and moving,
grasses, shrubs, creepers and the rest,
from the nature of one's own being
one should surely meditate on the supreme reality.
They are one alone; there is no other.
Great bliss is self-awareness.
Supreme joy is truly obtained
in the moment that has abandoned variety.
The Teacher said: "Great being,
take hold of great bliss."

So too Āryadevapāda said:

Through joy and the rest, in order, relying on the subtle object of focus,
the moments come to be exhausted; at the time of the fourth,
ah, what is this thing called an object of focus?
Whatever bliss is free of shyness is merely to be measured as an aspect of experience;
that too is perishing, and in it
there is only expansion, no object of focus.
Oh, filling the three realms of existence,
great bliss is graceful:
it is the moment in which self and other
are not divided.

By this the following is taught: from the example of the third, the joy of cessation, having realized that the bliss of self-blessing arises, from its very purification the co-emergent is to be realized in the aspect of suchness. This is the meaning of the words, "and the fourth is likewise." What wisdom there is when there is union was stated at the beginning; likewise here too, from realizing the co-emergent, that becomes the fourth wisdom. Therefore one should have confidence in whatever bliss and whatever wisdom come from the example of the co-emergent awakening mind. Because it follows like a shadow of the co-emergent, it is not explained as reality itself, but as belonging to the great elements. For the Blessed One too said:

Because it is the bliss of a great embrace,
therefore that bliss is not reality.

These are the disciple's words of question:

If the awakening mind,
the treasure of all accomplishments, falls,
then since the aggregates and consciousness swoon,
how can there be irreproachable accomplishment?
From the perfect union of the space sphere and the vajra,
whatever bliss arises from touch,
of great wonder,
that is the agent of supreme joy.

Here, "how can there be irreproachable accomplishment?" refers to the accomplishment whose nature is the Great Seal, arising from co-emergent joy, which is explained as that very supreme joy. It is irreproachable because all objects of focus are pacified; therefore it is the understanding of the co-emergent. If there is that swooning of the aggregates and the rest, what wisdom is experienced? Therefore self-blessing has been classed as impure. When its meditation has reached the utmost culmination: as long as the aggregates and the rest have not become nonexistent, so long one is unable to make the accomplishment of the Great Seal directly manifest. The Blessed One too said, "Supreme bliss is without aggregates." This very thing is called death by the yoginīs; and the Blessed One too said:

Just as bliss is obtained from the father,
his bliss is excellent of itself;
the bliss by which there is death,
that bliss is called meditative concentration.
The concept called death...

This is set out at length in the Perfection of Wisdom and elsewhere.

What use is there in saying much? From the reality of the Vajra Vehicle:

Even what is said to exist as a substantial entity
is, in dependence on its intrinsic nature, that very reality.
The imagined entity does not exist;
the dependent does exist.

So it is said, and by the words "and the rest" the dependent nature is included. Because it relies on other causes, it is dependent and established. The dependent has the characteristic of the self-blessing of beings. The nonexistence of these entities, which is explained as the coemergent form of the perfected nature, is also called nirvāṇa; for an emptiness of everything in every respect is the portion of the non-Buddhists (tīrthikas). That very thing is the Perfection of Wisdom, the mother who gives birth to the tathāgatas. This is purification, the fourth stage.

Now the stage of arising from purification will be fully taught. On this it is said: If one asks, "Since arising is the absence of a self of the three joys that have the characteristic of self-blessing, to whom would that, just as it is, belong?", it should be answered: the absence of the characteristic of self-blessing is not something born. Rather, as long as one does not make cessation directly manifest through meditating on the coemergent joy together with the illusion-like consciousness accompanied by its habitual tendencies, so long there is arising; but it does not become generation.

Here too it is said:

Afterward that reality is correctly explained,
having the nature of pure wisdom.

"Afterward" means that what is born from self-awareness immediately after self-blessing is the accomplishment. As it is said:

From self-awareness comes accomplishment;
because of self-awareness there is meditation.

And again it is said:

Free from mudrā, from cause, and from union,
yoga becomes compassion, the method.
Emptiness and compassion, inseparable,
are declared to be the awakening mind.

This very thing the Blessed One taught: union as the path, and union as the result. As he said:

Self-aware wisdom becomes that reality,
free of the correct knowing of self and other,
equal to the sky, stainless, emptiness,
the supreme nature of entity and nonentity.
Method and wisdom thoroughly blended,
attachment and freedom from attachment mingled,
it is the life of living beings;
it is not that which is unchanging.
It is the agent of all things,
and it abides in all bodies.

And in this very text it is said:

The Glorious One has gone to suchness,
and likewise has come back again;
by this, through the reasoning of wisdom,
he is designated the Tathāgata.

Then Vajragarbha asked, "By what means is the awakening mind to be generated?" The Blessed One said:

Through means such as the maṇḍala circle,
and through the stage of self-blessing as well,
generate fully the awakening mind
that has the forms of the conventional and the ultimate.
The conventional is like the kunda flower;
the ultimate has the form of bliss.
The woman's kakkola is Sukhāvatī,
in the nature of the syllables evaṃ;
because it guards bliss,
it is called Sukhāvatī, the Blissful.
It is the abode of buddhas, bodhisattvas
and the Vajra Holders.
This very thing is called saṃsāra;
this very thing is nirvāṇa itself.
Apart from saṃsāra, elsewhere,
nirvāṇa is not to be conceived.
Saṃsāra is form, sound and the rest;
saṃsāra is feeling and the rest;
saṃsāra is the sense faculties themselves;
saṃsāra is aversion and the rest.
These phenomena are nirvāṇa;
because of delusion they have the form of saṃsāra.
Without delusion, by the purifying of saṃsāra,
saṃsāra passes into nirvāṇa.

By this, from fully knowing the non-abiding nirvāṇa of these phenomena, the supreme Victors have taught their own birth. If others, through the succession of the lineage of gurus, come to know this nature fully, and it belongs to ordinary beings, then it is not the exclusive nature of those Victors alone. The Blessed One too said:

The birth of beings without exception,
that which is to be spoken of,
because the emanation abides firmly,
is the emanation body itself.

And so on at length:

What is spoken of as the monastery
is to be declared the belly.
The place of birth becomes free from desire.
The caul of the womb is the monastic robe;
likewise the mother is the preceptor.
Palms joined at the head is the salutation;
the activity of beings is the basis of training.
Likewise the mantra recited is a and haṃ:
the syllable a is the cakra of the place of birth,
and the syllable haṃ is that of great bliss.
Naked, with hair and beard shaven,
reciting mantra, a monk is born.
Through this gathering of conditions a being
is a buddha, without doubt.
The ten months are the grounds;
the being is lord of the ten grounds.
This is great wisdom,
abiding in all bodies,
in the manner of duality and nonduality,
the lord whose nature is entity and nonentity.
Pervading the stable and the moving it abides,
appearing in the form of an illusion.
Through means such as the maṇḍala circle
it is always certainly accomplished.
Great wisdom abides in the body,
having abandoned all conceptualization.
Pervading all entities,
though it abides in the body it is not born of the body.

Therefore one should understand the stage that is born immediately after one has fully entered the suchness of self-blessing; otherwise there would be merely the exhaustion of saṃsāra. And for as long as one remains in saṃsāra, the means of fully realizing the ultimate meaning, and the result characterized by supreme great bliss endowed with qualities such as the ten powers and the fearlessnesses, would not be obtained naturally. As it is said:

One bound by the discipline of the generation-stage yoga
should meditate on elaboration.
Having made elaboration like a dream,
make that very elaboration free of elaboration.
Like an illusion, like a dream,
like the intermediate existence:
from constant cultivation of familiarity
the maṇḍala appears in just that way.
The empowerment of the Great Seal,
the great bliss as it is known:
it is from experiencing this very thing;
the maṇḍala arises from nothing else.

And likewise:

The aggregates themselves are the stainless maṇḍala;
all is ascertained and realized as many goddesses.
It is also the hero Heruka:
the body without leaking, the taste of suchness.

In the Hevajra Tantra too it is said:

Black bliss, yellow bliss,
red bliss, white bliss,
blue bliss, green bliss,
black bliss, the moving and the unmoving.
Bliss is wisdom, bliss is method;
likewise the bliss born of kunduru.
Bliss is entity and nonentity;
Vajrasattva is declared to be bliss.

Vajragarbha asked:

This yoga of the completion stage,
its bliss is held to be great bliss.
Without meditation on completion,
what purpose is served by generation?

The Blessed One said:

Oh, great being of the awakening mind,
listen well, with the strength of faith.
Without a body, from where would bliss come?
One could not even speak of bliss.
In the manner of the pervaded and the pervader,
bliss pervades all beings.
Just as the fragrance that abides in a flower
could not be known were there no flower,
so, without form and the rest,
bliss itself would not be perceived.
I am entity and I am nonentity;
because I realize entities, I am the Buddha.
Those who are afflicted by laziness,
and those who are deluded, do not know me.
In the bhaga of the venerable goddess,
in the guise and form of the shape of e,
the jewel casket of the buddhas,
I move always in Sukhāvatī.
I am the expounder, and I am also the Dharma;
I am the listener, endowed with my own assembly.
I am what is to be accomplished, the teacher of beings;
I am the world, and the worldly as well.
I am the nature of coemergent joy,
possessing the first, the supreme, the final, and the joy of cessation.
Like a lamp in the darkness:
in the same way, son, you too should have faith.
The Teacher with the thirty-two marks
abides in the queen's bhaga, in Sukhāvatī,
under the name of semen.
By these two stages that have been taught,
in the mantra tantras and the rest, and in yoga,
whoever does not know Heruka
toils in vain.
Without that, there would be no bliss;
without bliss, that would not be.
Because each is powerless alone, they are mutually dependent;
bliss comes from the yoga of the deity.
Because it arises from the body,
it is to be called a deity.
Therefore the Buddha does not become an entity,
nor does he become one in the manner of a nonentity.
He has form, with faces and arms,
yet through supreme bliss he is without form.
Therefore all beings are coemergent,
and their nature is to be declared coemergent.
Through the purified mind,
the intrinsic nature itself is nirvāṇa.
From face, hands, color and shape,
in the form of the deity's aspect,
it abides merely by arising;
but from ordinary habitual tendencies...

Therefore it is not liberation through the absence of an enjoyment body; that is not to be accepted anywhere. Nor is it that liberation through an entity which is explained as the coemergent arising from emptiness in every respect. Therefore it is certain that the liberation of those views is not the awakening mind, whose form is the culmination of the inseparability of emptiness and compassion. Likewise Nāgārjunapāda says:

One is liberated through entity itself;
it does not come about through nonentity.
The nature of entity is liberation,
through fully knowing entity and nonentity.

The Blessed One too said in the tantra:

Vajragarbha, of great compassion,
by entity itself one is liberated,
and by the bondage of entity one is bound.
By fully knowing it, there is liberation.
By fully knowing nonentity as well,
the one possessed of wisdom meditates on entity.
By fully knowing nonentity as well,
meditate thus on the glorious Heruka.

And again it is said:

The syllable śrī is nondual wisdom;
that is emptiness of cause and the rest.
The syllable ru is free of arrangement,
and ka is not abiding anywhere.
The yogin who, understanding it thus,
meditates in meditative equipoise,
even a person of weak merit,
will accomplish it without doubt.

The yoga of meditative equipoise is the yoga of the lowest practitioner.

While eating, drinking and bathing,
whether waking or asleep, one should contemplate.
One who longs for the Great Seal to become manifest
will always proceed from that.

This meditative equipoise belongs to the middling yogin. Those who practice the conduct of the great one:

Food and drink, just as they are found;
not avoiding what may or may not be approached;
not performing ablutions or purity;
village practice is not to be abandoned.
The wise one does not recite mantra,
nor make meditative concentration an object.
Sleep is not to be abandoned;
the senses are not to be restrained.
One should eat every kind of bala, flesh;
one should conduct oneself equally toward all castes.
With a mind free of misgivings,
one should delight all women.
One should not delight in friends,
nor have aversion toward the vicious.
One should not bow down to these gods
made of stone, wood, or clay.

And so on at length. And likewise:

There is no mantra recitation, no austerity, no fire offering;
there is no master of the maṇḍala, and no maṇḍala either.
That is mantra recitation, that is austerity, that is the fire offering;
that is the master of the maṇḍala, and that is the maṇḍala too.
In brief, it has the form of what is gathered into the mind.

And elsewhere it is said:

Just as the hero, free of conceptualization,
having overcome worldly states,
should engage in every conduct:
then he sees the Buddha.

The unsurpassed five stages, taught by the glorious Heruka in the king of tantras,
arranged by the glorious Jālandhari and Bhadrapāda, and the secret intent of the guru:
by the supreme merit gained in making a true treatise of it for the fortunate one who receives it,
may all sentient beings, the whole multitude of beings, certainly attain the unsurpassed five stages.

Here ends the Sādhana of the Glorious Hevajra called Clear Illumination, composed by the great maṇḍala master, the glorious Rāhulaguptapāda.
