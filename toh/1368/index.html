About this text

The Commentary on the Six-Limbed Yoga, known in Sanskrit as the Ṣaḍaṅgayogaṭīkā and in Tibetan as Sbyor ba yan lag drug pa'i 'grel pa, is a tantric commentary by the Indian paṇḍita Raviśrījñāna on the six-limbed yoga of the Kālacakra tradition, the system of withdrawal, meditative stabilization, breath control, retention, recollection and absorption that leads to the "empty forms" and to undiminished bliss. It opens with the story of the master Anupamarakṣita, who after years of fruitless practice was sent by Vajrayoginī to Vikramapura, where the realization behind his own teaching on the six limbs arose, and it then explains that teaching, describing the signs that appear in the night and day yogas, such as smoke, mirage, fireflies and the cloudless sky, and how the yogin sees emptiness directly without conceptual imagining. It was translated into Tibetan by the eastern Indian paṇḍita Vibhūticandra. The work is Toh 1368 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the tantra commentaries associated with the Kālacakra cycle.

Translation

In the language of India: ṣaḍaṅgayogaṭīkā. In the language of Tibet: Commentary on the Six-Limbed Yoga.

Homage to glorious Kālacakra.

Bowing to the lord of Kālacakra
and to the guru of great qualities,
I compose this commentary
that brings to completion the qualities of the sixfold yoga.

Here, the master Anupamarakṣita, whose mind was trained in the outer and inner stages, was meditating on reality as an object of focus. Although he stayed for twelve years in the presence of glorious Khasarpaṇa, he did not attain any special realization, and so, disheartened, he fell asleep. Then Vajrayoginī spoke to him: "Son, go to Vikramapura! There a special realization will arise in you." Placing her command upon the crown of his head, he went to Vikramapura together with his disciple, the great paṇḍita Śrīdhara, son of the overseer. There, at an auspicious time, while he was engaged in virtuous practice, at midnight Vajradhara, in the form of an avadhūti, instructed him in person. Merely by receiving that instruction, he entered into the equipoise of meditative absorption. Then the Blessed One vanished right there. When dawn broke, he too arose from meditative absorption, and that great being gave the instruction to Śrīdhara, who had been the guru's companion. He in turn, out of compassion, gave it to the fortunate great paṇḍita Bhāskaradeva. He in turn explained it to the guru of great paṇḍitas, the omniscient one of the degenerate age, Dharmākaraśānti. And from him I, Raviśrī, who strive to please the guru, a source of inexpressible qualities, obtained reality. This is the lineage of the gurus.

Thus, at the request of a disciple, the master Anupamarakṣita, wishing to set forth the six-limbed yoga, which is the cause of attaining the level and state of a vajra holder, states the six limbs by way of praising his own chosen deity. This is the verse beginning "Obscuration."

Because it veils reality, obscuration itself is a mass of clouds. Being free of it, he realizes all things, leaving nothing uncounted; and rising in the ultimate sky through the cessation of the breath and wind, Avalokiteśvara is the Buddha. Because he is precisely the full moon, he delights beings, and so he is beautiful. In the definitive sense, this means being free of the superimposition of apprehended and apprehender. This explains the limbs of withdrawal (pratyāhāra) and meditative concentration.

The conceptions in all their number are characterized by the eighty-four thousand moments of mind accompanied by passion. And the variegated form, seen in every aspect in the sky where the outgoing and the indrawn winds have ceased: by this, breath control, retention, and recollection are explained. It comes from the wheel of great bliss, through contact with the rising caṇḍālī. Because he holds with the crown of all the buddhas and with the tip of the vajra, he is the Lord of the World. He is beautiful like a moon full with the sixteen joys. This explains the limb of meditative absorption.

Now the activity of the six limbs is explained in terms that seem contradictory. This is the verse beginning "Existence and peace." Existence is the conventional, the continuum of birth and death; peace is the ultimate truth. Although it is free of any reference to these two, it is not different from them, and so it is union. Because it pervades the three realms, it is the pervader. Because it is empty, very empty, great emptiness, and empty of everything, it is emptiness. It possesses all aspects, like a reflection in a mirror. It is the state that is the culmination of nonconceptual wisdom, that is, the lord of the twelfth stage. "Utterly nonabiding," because it does not cling to any object of focus. "Unwavering," because it does not decline. Being free of duality, it is "nonconceptual." "Wise" shows that in realizing nonconceptual wisdom, the bliss of the five sense pleasures does not diminish. "Not mind" is the mind in which conceptions have ceased. Because it arises from that and is to be relied upon by beings, it is "protector." With this he prays that it bless the minds of beings.

Preceded by a statement of the qualities that are actually present, homage to the guru is explained. This is the verse beginning "Conceiving existence." If one conceives anything at all to exist, one falls into the extreme of eternalism; and if one discards it without examination, that is the extreme of annihilation, which is the cause of experiencing suffering in the Avīci hell for many eons. When the sublime path had thus been obliterated, the glorious guru showed the excellent disciple the nature of mind, which transcends mind and is not mind: empty, yet possessing all aspects such as suffering; an image in the sky, a variegated form. To that guru, with great delight, I bow with body, speech, and mind.

Now, if one asks, "If the sublime path is obtained solely through the kindness of the guru, what is the use of praising oneself by stating the supreme six limbs?" he says: the verse beginning "Thus." To state the qualities of the guru who teaches the path as it is, is simply to state one's own understanding; for just as, without a blazing fire, it is impossible to see smoke clearly arising, so it is here.

Then, if one objects, "Realizing the path of reality is itself the cause of pacifying pride, so the pride of thinking, 'I know reality,' is not fitting," he says: the verse beginning "Declaring realization." This pride in my realization does not become a fault, because it causes those who desire liberation to enter the path, and so it is a cause of benefit for others; it is not for the sake of gain and the like. For people do not set about accomplishing a desired aim in a matter the teacher has not ascertained.

Now the origin of the tantra is explained. Mantra practitioners puffed up with having understood the provisional meaning of all the tantras through commentaries written by ordinary beings, not knowing the distinction between the conventional and the ultimate, go astray on the path. In passages such as "the aggregate of form is Vairocana," the word "Vairocana" teaches that the aggregate of form is empty; so in order that they might realize reality, without imagining that form is thereby negated, the King of Śambhala, an emanation of the bodhisattva, the great being Mañjuśrī, at the command of glorious Vajradhara, by way of condensing the teaching, set forth the later Kālacakra Tantra. This names the teacher, the compiler, and the one who requested its meaning, King Sucandra, an emanation of Vajrapāṇi; and so the greatness of the tantra is declared.

This is the tantra on which Puṇḍarīka, an emanation of the Lord of the World, composed the commentary called Stainless Light (Vimalaprabhā), which illuminates every meaning. What he illuminated is the mind of the precious Buddha, the most sublime of sublime paths. Those who have gathered merit obtain it from the guru, and because it teaches the nectar of the definitive meaning, the smoke of existence is cleared away by the nectar of the four joys, and they revel in undiminished bliss. With this the greatness of the one who explained it is declared.

Again he speaks the praise of the king of tantras: the verse beginning "In which." It is the support of the entire collection of knowable things, the outer and inner fields of knowledge, for it is said, "Whatever is not taught in the Ādibuddha does not exist." It is the king of tantras in which the Buddha, the Blessed One, is shown to bear the name "Omniscient" in its true meaning. Again, it is the source of the jewels of reality, beside which the words of Īśvara (Śiva) and the others, having no essence, are like grass. The Perfection of Wisdom, because it realizes phenomena as empty, is "born from space," that is, born from the variegated form; and what is born of this Mother of the Buddhas destroys the whole host of conceptions in such a way that they never arise again.

Now he explains the guru's instruction, which is the cause from which the signs such as smoke arise and the basis for overcoming the malicious: the verse beginning "In which." The tantra in which it is taught that when, with attention, one fixes the half-opened eyes steadily on the expanse of the sky free of stains, that is, of clouds and the like, then from the mind moistened with compassion and free of conceptions, signs such as smoke arise, free of the four alternatives. "In pitch darkness one should not think of anything at all": this is the instruction. Elsewhere too it is said:

Having torn out the root of the mind,
if one becomes habituated to emptiness,
for those blinded by the cataract of ignorance
what eye medicine is there other than this?

And Saraha too has said:

Making all phenomena equal to space,
holding the mind to be equal to space,
if one makes that mind no-mind,
by that one attains unsurpassed awakening.

Now, what clarifies the completion stage is that the conventional and the ultimate have come to a single taste. In the Wisdom chapter of the Kālacakra, after one hundred and twenty-six verses, comes the verse beginning "Undiminished." By negating, for the undiminished innate body, the phenomena that are mere conceptions, namely the ā li and kā li, the seats of lotus, moon, and sun, the transformation of the syllable hūṃ, a form arisen from a hand emblem, shape, arms, and figure, the Blessed One states the practice of undiminished wisdom. ā li is the series of vowels beginning with ā, which is the moon. kā li is the series of consonants beginning with kā, which is the sun. The seat formed by these two becoming one, the lotus from paṃ, the moon from a, the sun from raṃ: these are not the seat of Vajradhara, who possesses undiminished wisdom. Nor is it held that the deity's body rests upon the transformation of a vajra arisen from hūṃ, for that is characterized by fixation, and so it is subject to decline. Because it produces undiminished bliss and destroys decline, it is free of the letters such as a imagined by ordinary people, and it possesses the divine faculties. The words beginning "That" are easy to understand.

The sphere of reality is the variegated form in space, possessing all aspects. It is like the divinatory image called down into an empty mirror and held there by the adept. Just as a young girl sees in it a thief and the like who are not there, so one sees, without conception, the unborn phenomena. It is so because there are no external objects of the kind imagined by the childish, and so, even while one has eyes, it appears like a dream. Because there is no inert matter, there are no infinitesimal particles. Because it does not enter into the system of the materialists (Cārvākas), it keeps the emptiness of annihilation far away. "Inner bliss," because it delights in its own self-awareness. "Outer" and the rest, because one does not rely on an action seal. "Instruction," because it will be explained below. "The Victors" and the rest: since it is not attained as a path that, as will be explained, is four when condensed and sixteen when divided out, it is not attained through a conceptual path. "hūṃ phaṭ and so forth": the words "and so forth" include the mudrās. Being free of them means that the generation stage is only an entry into the path; what goes beyond it is the completion stage, which is to be realized by meditating on the sky. Its sequence is the yoga of smoke and the rest; that alone is the cause, and not other realizations such as the lotus.

Next the method for seeing the awakening mind is explained: the verse beginning "Fire." Leaving aside fire that has no support, even the fire present in wood is not seen when one cuts the wood with an axe or the like or splits it with a saw, yet it is seen through the use of a fire drill and the like. In the same way, by habituating oneself again and again to conceptual thought, one does not see the way the mind appears; but through the yoga in which the rasanā and the lalanā are not two in the central channel, one's own mind comes to appear just as it is. This is explained in the Paramārthasevā:

As fire comes from wood, water from the water crystal,
butter from curd, gold from stone, and so on,
so undiminished bliss, shown through a woman,
is to be grasped by the fortunate through applying its method.

In your mind nothing whatsoever is accumulated;
there is nothing to set in place, no increase and no decrease.
In one's own mind there is merely mind itself appearing,
like the sun in water throughout the three realms.

The intention is that the one mind alone becomes many through the reflection of wisdom.

That very point is explained: the verse beginning "Smoke." Those of which smoke is the first are the mirage and the rest. "Signs" are what give confidence. Breath control is the vase breath, whose nature is to block the three channels above and below. "Moving in the central channel" means that the life wind enters the channel of the mind and moves there. "The observance of the vidyā" is celibate conduct in which the vidyā, the consort, is made primary. "Undiminished" means remaining erect. "Moving upward" refers to the jewel, the lotus, and so on. "By this path" means by the path characterized by the six limbs, applied to smoke and the rest. The bliss of all the buddhas, free of the sense faculties, is the supreme of the undiminished. "All the messengers" are the innate bliss of the ḍākinīs, whose nature is the stopping of the seventy-two thousand channels. "Form and so forth": among form and the rest, meditating on a form is the action seal; by the words "and so forth," the mental seal is meant. The maṇḍala is the support, and the wheel is what is supported, the assembly of deities. By the words "and so forth," mantras and mudrās are meant.

By meditating on these, one gains the world, that is, the three realms, and its accomplishment is lordship as far as Akaniṣṭha; but where in this is the accomplishment of the great seal (mahāmudrā), which transcends the world, accomplished?

Then does this instruction on the path arise in everyone? It does not; rather, see the verse beginning "Beings." Through the kindness of the holy guru, this path arises in a disciple who has been purified, the stains of ordinary pride having been cleansed by empowerment and the like. As it is said in the Mahāmudrātilaka:

Just as poison, when purified
by mantras and the like, becomes an elixir,
so through empowerment and the like
an impure disciple becomes pure.

It arises in those beings who are compassionate, devoted to the benefit of others, and always diligent in virtue.

Now he explains that even when the path has been obtained, carelessness is the cause of being parted from it: the verse beginning "The supreme path." The path is that by which buddhahood itself is attained without hardship. Because it increases joy, it is precious. Because it is hard to find and grants the perfect fulfillment of one's desired aims, it is like a wish-fulfilling jewel. The careless are those who have no regard for benefit and happiness, who wander in saṃsāra among the six classes of beings, who lack qualities such as compassion, and who do not strive for the benefit and happiness of others. If it is given to such a one, then his own household, that is, his wife, is itself the forest, for it is the haunt of savage creatures. There, because the senses are attached to it, there are the objects: the enjoyment of external objects such as form is base, for it is contrary to the path, and it is the deluded who indulge in it. These are the thieves, and by them the path is stolen.

Not by these alone, but also by doubt about the precious path in which the guru, scripture, and oneself have confidence; and by laziness, which is lack of enthusiasm for virtue. Sleep is the unfitness of body and mind for work. Regret takes the form of thinking, "Because I have done base deeds, I cannot make the path directly manifest." The kin of these thieves, children, wife, and the rest, are themselves the forest, and the supreme path of liberation of one who has entered there will be stolen.

Not only these obscurations of meditative absorption, but also killing, taking what is not given, lying, slander, harsh speech, sexual misconduct, idle talk, covetousness, malice, and wrong view: by these too the precious path will be stolen. Not by these alone; it is also stolen by food and drink taken utterly without measure. Here food means meat and the like, and drink means liquor and the like. "Utterly" is judged by the Dharma of the Victor. Enjoyments are the various things such as garlands, sandalwood, and women. If even one possessed of diligence is robbed by these, what need is there to speak of one without diligence? Carelessness includes keeping company with bad friends and the like; it is as if one were stunned by it.

The examination of the precious path is explained: the verse beginning "A jewel." If, not knowing oneself whether something is a jewel or not, one shows it to another who is sinful and unskilled, asking, "Is this a jewel or not?", he says, "It is not." Because of his words, one who lacks good fortune throws it away. This explains the fault of keeping company with sinful friends.

Then, if one asks, "How can one who does not know it through his own examination recognize the precious path?", he says: the verse beginning "A jewel." One should rely only on someone skilled in examining jewels, and through his undeceiving words one should know it to be priceless. This explains relying on a spiritual friend.

It is explained that if Māra appears to one who has entered the path, he must be rejected: the verse beginning "Father." Because there are those who steal the path, Māra the evil one creates obstacles in the form of the Buddha and others, saying, "This is the path to awakening," and so forth, as explained in the chapter on Māra.

Summarizing, he explains: the verse beginning "Thieves." Because there are those who steal the path, one should abandon kin, doubt, and the rest, and with effort make the precious path granted by the guru exceedingly clear.

If one says, "There is another path for attaining liberation," he says: the verse beginning "Self." The stage of self-blessing is focusing on peaceful deities. The stage of the hero is focusing on wrathful deities. These were not taught by the Buddha for the sake of liberation; rather, the utterly pure stage is the completion stage alone.

If one asks, "How is that known?", he says: the verse beginning "By which, mother." "By which" means for which reason. Even those who have committed the five deeds of immediate retribution, namely killing father and mother, killing an arhat, causing schism in the Saṅgha, and drawing the blood of a Tathāgata with evil intent, are gathered blissfully and without effort into the pure stage through the meaning of empowerment. Otherwise, those who had committed the deeds of immediate retribution would not be turned back from fierce deeds by the stage of the hero, and through self-blessing too one does not attain liberation from the pleasures of saṃsāra.

Then, if one asks, "If these two stages are not the path, why did the Blessed One teach them?", he says: the verse beginning "Hero." The stage of the hero is not something external; it is so called because it is this that exhausts the life wind in the body. Self-blessing is the appearance of one's own mind, and that is the appearance of the three realms in the sky. Self-blessing is the appearance of one's own mind; because it is the appearance of the three realms, once it has arisen it does not turn back, and so saṃsāra does not turn back.

If one asks, "How then does one become a buddha?", he says: the verse beginning "Saṃsāra." After the three realms, which are without essence and false, have been exhausted, the fruit arises that possesses qualities such as a buddha's omniscience; just as the plantain, which has no core, ripens its fruit and the fruit comes forth when the plant is spent.

Then, if one asks, "Since this illusion-like appearance is ignorance, how can the awakening mind that arises from it be the cause of awakening?", he says: the verse beginning "The she-mule." The she-mule, the scorpion, and the like kill the mother from whom they were born. In the same way, the mind of the yoga of method and wisdom, the fourth, kills that illusion, the conventional from which it arose.

By way of the undiminished nature of this yogic mind, the four and the sixteen divisions are explained: the verse beginning "Nondual." "Nondual" is that in which saṃsāra and nirvāṇa do not exist. It is "unmoving" because it does not move from the jewel and the lotus. It is "causeless" because there is no cause of method or of wisdom. It is the "chosen deity" because it grants unsurpassed bliss: this is the fourth yoga. It is "undiminished" through stopping the twenty-one thousand six hundred breaths. It is "great passion" because it abides between passion and freedom from passion.

Because it is freed from the range between having aspects and having no aspects, it is "neither entity nor nonentity." Because it sees all knowable things without remainder, it is "wisdom": this is the third yoga. Because, preceded by making beings joyful, it is devoted to the welfare of sentient beings, it is "being" (sattva). Because it is virtuous in the beginning, the middle, and the end, it is "the All-Good" (Samantabhadra). Because conceptions concerning Māra and the afflictions have been abandoned, there is no sound of utterance, outer or inner, and so it is "soundless." Because its own sound has no intrinsic nature, it is "great sound": this is the second yoga. Because birth from a womb has been abandoned, it is "innate." Because the obscurations of the four bodies are exhausted, it "holds the drop of the awakening mind." Because emptiness and compassion are not different, it is "the vajra of glorious Kālacakra." Because the accumulation of moral discipline is complete, it is "the yoga endowed with method and wisdom": this is the first yoga.

In order to teach it, the characteristics of the guru are explained: the verse beginning "Thus." Having entered the Vajra Vehicle, he meditates on reality by meditating on undiminished wisdom. Because he guards the awakening mind, he keeps the commitments. Because he is without attachment and the like, he is unsullied. Because he has no regard for children, wife, his own body, and the like, he is without desire. He has pleased a guru who abides in the Buddha's path and is endowed with patience. He teaches the path to those who are weary with dread of the hells. Having attained undiminished bliss, he lives the pure life within it. That guru who conquers the four māras is blessed by the vajra holder and so is his emanation; therefore he is Vajrasattva.

Now, for the sake of accomplishing the mundane and the supramundane, the visualization of the deity is explained: the verse beginning "Of the deity." "Its yoga" means the yoga of meditating on the variegated form. "Like a wheel of stars": preceded by seeing, within a drop shaped like a blue jewel or a moon disk, the enjoyment bodies of infinite buddhas, one sees with the fleshly eye, which sees things a hundred leagues away, the vases and other things of the three realms; and with the buddha eye, with knowledge of others' minds, with the wisdom eye, which is characterized by not conceiving any phenomenon, and with the eye of primordial wisdom, one sees all things in all their aspects. Other, ordinary images, made of clay and the like or cast in brass and the like, are seen as like corpses, since they lack emanation and the like.

The five nectars are feces, urine, marrow, blood, and semen. By "and the like" the five lamps are meant: the flesh of cow, dog, elephant, horse, and human. The inner five nectars are the aggregates, and the five lamps are the five senses. One is without dependence on these. "Food" is turning toward objects; turning back from turning toward ordinary forms and the like is withdrawal. "Other forms and the rest," because they are not made of particles, are supramundane forms and so forth. The vajra lotus is the lotus at the crown of the head, and it arises there. "By nectar" means by the undiminished awakening mind. "By seven months" implies nine months. "By the six aggregates" means by the aggregates of form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness, and primordial wisdom. The attainment of the grounds through the division into lesser and so forth comes from applying the fourfold division beginning with the lesser to the three. From the Very Joyful up to the Eye of Primordial Wisdom there are twelve grounds. The meaning of the twelve aspects of truth is the stopping of the twelve transits through the nostrils, which are the purity of the twelve houses, the Ram, the Bull, and the rest. "Knowing reality in sixteen aspects" means being undiminished through the exhaustion of the sixteen parts of saṃsāra, the digits of the moon. "Awakened through twenty aspects" means through meditating on the six yogas, purified by the pure central channel, through the purified conceptions of the five aggregates, the five elements, the five faculties, and the five sense fields. "Fully awakened," through the stopping of ignorance. "All-knowing," through realizing all things as the nature of buddha. For that very reason it is "supreme." "In a few days": in three years and one and a half months, reckoned in Kālacakra days, one will attain the accomplishment of the great seal.

Now, with the verse beginning "Vajra mind," the six yogas are explained. Withdrawal is tenfold, by the division of the signs such as smoke. Meditative concentration is so because, in the variegated form, object and subject become eleven. Breath control is so because the left and right maṇḍalas become eleven. Retention is tenfold because the life wind neither goes nor comes at the navel, heart, throat, forehead, and crown. Recollection is by division into the ten states of desire:

Thinking, longing, fever arising in the body,
the mouth going dry, no wish to eat,
trembling, intoxication, the body reeling,
the mind confused, and utter swooning:
these states in the bodies of beings
are the states of Vajradhara.

Meditative absorption is the stopping of the ten winds:
the life wind, the downward-clearing, the equalizing,
the upward-moving, and the pervading,
the nāga, the tortoise, the kṛkara,
the devadatta, and the dhanaṃjaya.

Through passion and freedom from passion, it is nonabiding nirvāṇa. "By the other five, the eye and the rest": the fleshly eye and the rest see the variegated form. "Certainty" is certainty in emptiness. "Clinging to the variegated form": whatever mental happiness there is from entering, through mind alone, with regard to objects and the rest, into the reality in which unsurpassed wisdom and method have become one taste, that is joy. "Unmoving bliss" is through the bliss of bodily pliancy and the like. "The two paths entering the central channel": because the five beginning with space do not move in the left nostril, and the five beginning with earth do not move in the right, the rasanā and the lalanā are blocked, and the subtle breath, characterized by entering, abiding, and emerging in the path of the central channel alone, is breath control. "Free of the two movements," because there is neither going nor coming at the navel, heart, throat, and crown. "The ten aspects explained earlier": when the mind abides in retention, there arises of itself, preceded by the appearance of the ten signs such as smoke, the appearance of the deities of the complete maṇḍala, like a reflection, free of conception; then flashes of light radiate; then, preceded by the emanation of maṇḍalas of deities throughout the three realms and the like, comes recollection, characterized by the ten states of desire beginning with thinking. "Through the non-movement of the life wind and the rest": through the blazing of caṇḍālī, since it is the undiminished awakening mind abiding at the tip of the vajra jewel, by stopping, stage by stage, the twenty-one thousand six hundred breaths in the six wheels, meditative absorption is the nature of Vajradhara, the primordial wisdom of union, whose nature is nonconceptual, undiminished bliss.

"One becomes a bodhisattva" means that one does not perform the arising of the mind set on nirvāṇa, the awakening, the turning of the wheel of Dharma, and the rest; the intention is that if one did perform them, one would be renowned in a buddha field simply as a perfect buddha. The ten powers are power over life, over karma, over inclination, over requisites, over aspiration, over miraculous powers, over the Dharma, over mind, over birth, and over primordial wisdom, and so forth. "Through habituation to nāda" means through habituation to the joy of innate primordial wisdom.

As has been explained, one inserts the vajra into the lotus of the young woman and rests without moving, pressing her lotus with the jewel of the vajra; through joy and supreme joy one remains unmoving for a moment, and when the joy that is subject to loss arises in both, then, preceded by uttering hūṃ, one turns one's mind toward the kindness of the holy guru, and through experiencing the fourth, the innate joy, the awakening mind is held back and one attains the undiminished moment. One goes to the state without an object of focus.

"At the end of twelve" refers to the left nostril, from the space maṇḍala to the earth maṇḍala, from sixteen finger-widths to the end of twelve. "At the end of sixteen" refers to the right nostril, from the earth maṇḍala to space, from twelve finger-widths to the end of sixteen. By uttering the sound hūṃ, the life wind and the downward-clearing wind are joined and dissolved at the navel, then inserted into the central channel; and when what is like the fire of lightning is driven upward, it is like snow. If one is unable to enter another body, one performs transference. "The vajra standing up" means after generating the supreme primordial wisdom of bliss. "Moving in the sky" means having power as far as Akaniṣṭha. The five superknowledges are miraculous power, the divine eye, the divine ear, knowledge of others' minds, and recollection of former lives. As the nature of the five superknowledges, Viśvamātā arises; through attachment to the great seal, the nonconceptual caṇḍālī blazes.

The three undiminished things are the stopping of the drop of mind at the secret place, the stopping of the drop of speech at the navel, and the stopping of the drop of body at the heart. "Through undiminished bliss": because the collection of drops at the tip of the vajra is undiminished, the drop of primordial wisdom is stopped. The passage up to "stopping the two winds" teaches the practice of moving in the sky, and the passage up to "Viśvamātā" teaches the practice of the great seal.

"The great seal like an illusion" is like a reflection in a clear mirror. "In the sky, without mind" means in the mind that is not mind. "In the order of wisdom and primordial wisdom": realizing all phenomena as empty is wisdom; primordial wisdom is the apprehending mind. The appearances of smoke and the rest are what it apprehends. Just as one sees one's own eye reflected in a mirror with one's own eye, so with one's own mind, whose nature is emptiness, one sees one's own mind, whose nature is emptiness, possessing all aspects.

In this characteristic of the apprehended mind accompanied by smoke, the yogi must place the life wind into the central channel; through that the signs are seen. This is the guru's first face, the vajra of body. Then, by restraining the life wind that moves to the sun and the moon, there is the second, breath control, the vajra of speech. "Awakening mind": the awakening mind attached to the variegated reflection is the third, the vajra of mind. "With supreme bliss raise the vajra": if joy does not arise by way of the variegated form, then one should slowly make the sound of the vajra. If one does not find a wisdom consort, then one arouses the vajra with one's own hand, in order to increase bliss, not in order to let it fall. Thus this fourth, the vajra of primordial wisdom, which does not cast away the awakening mind, is endowed with bliss, and protects from the fear of death. This is what the yogi is to meditate on with certainty.

In the Ādibuddha, in the section that summarizes the empowerment, is the verse beginning "This alone." Having the firewood of the afflictive and cognitive obscurations, the fire of omniscient primordial wisdom, through smoke and the rest: this alone is to be accomplished by the yogi. How? As is renowned from the root tantra, the later tantra, and the sūtras of definitive meaning such as the Perfection of Wisdom: appearance in emptiness, because it is free of all conceptions, is inconceivable; because the fruit, reality, becomes manifest, it is free of signs and the like, of apprehended and apprehender; it is the reflection of wisdom, empty by nature; because it is not covered by outer and inner obscurations, it is equal to space; because it is without conceptions of existence, nonexistence, and the like, it transcends existence and nonexistence; because it is self-awareness and is not made into something experienced by one's own mind, it is wondrous; because it has no intrinsic nature, it is empty of any aggregation of infinitesimal particles; it is like the bliss that arises in a dream from meeting the daughter of a barren woman.

Relying on the reflection that arises from space,
so it is within oneself.

"The aspect of earth and the rest" is the characteristic of the reflection. Here the appearance of emptiness appears as what is not an entity and so forth; it is not solid. Having the aspect of liquid, it is not water, like the water of a mirage, because it is not an entity. Having the aspect of fire, it is not fire, because it does not burn. It appears as movement, like wind, yet it is not wind, because it is not an entity. It is like space, because space is not seen. Like a city of illusion, though it has colors such as white, it is not color, because it is not an entity. Though it possesses all aspects, it cannot be seen by the childish, because of the power of the afflictions and of Māra, that is, because the life wind moves to the left and to the right.

Now the manner in which the five maṇḍalas dissolve is explained: the verse beginning "Earth wind." When the yogi, with steady eyes and the wrathful gaze, fixes the mind on emptiness, then, as space and the rest, or earth and the rest, move in the left or the right nostril, the earth wind dissolves into the water wind; and likewise from the left, space and the rest, up to the maṇḍala of consciousness. Thus emptiness becomes the ten signs such as smoke. The signs in turn become a reflection possessing all aspects. The reflection becomes undiminished bliss. That itself is the primordial wisdom of the Perfection of Wisdom. From primordial wisdom arise miraculous powers such as moving through the sky and the accomplishment of lordship over the three realms, and these are accomplished in this very birth. The yoga is summarized and explained: the verse beginning "The central channel."

Here, first, the grasping mind dissolving into emptiness is its becoming of one taste. "Child," "come of age," and "emitting" refer to the bliss of method, which declines, and which is twofold: the changing and the sluggish. Wisdom does not emit, because it does not quickly decline. The supreme of these, the transmundane, the one that does not emit, is just that which arises from the Great Seal (mahāmudrā).

Now he explains the entry into the appearance of one's own mind, which is like a reflection in a mirror: the verse beginning "Space." "Night" indicates darkness. "Attached" means the mind dissolving into space. "The appearance of form": because, like a reflection in a mirror, it appears free of turbidity and unobstructed. "The appearance of sound": it does not arise from the effort of the throat, the palate and so on; rather, by the power of great bliss, mere self-arisen sound is experienced. This verse explains the two bodies.

"There is no knowing of another's mind": because there is no other mind, distinct from one's own mind, to be known. It is said:

Unborn and unceasing,
whatever knowable is seen here
is one's own mind and not another,
since it is other than an external knowable.
Therefore one's sealing of oneself
by oneself exists nowhere,
just as a great sword
is unable to cut itself.

This verse explains the accomplishment of the Dharma body.

The aspect of smoke, pervading outside and inside, comes after the cloud-like appearance. It is said:

At the very first it appears like cloud;
appearing as smoke is the second.

A mirage is like shimmering water. Fireflies and the aspect of a lamp are as commonly known. These four signs arise out of darkness in a house without openings; hence the night yoga and the yoga of space. In the Guhyasamāja the first and the second are taught in reverse order:

When the mind is stopped and becomes vajra,
the signs will arise.
These signs are of five kinds,
as Bodhivajra has taught.
First, having the aspect of a mirage;
like smoke is the second;
fireflies are the third;
like a lamp is the fourth;
like a cloudless sky,
the constant appearance is the fifth.

Preceded by the appearance of the cloudless sky, the blazing in the aspect of fire and the appearances of the moon and the sun are as commonly known. Rāhu appears like a black jewel. Lightning and the great drop, which illuminate the stable and the moving, appear blue in color, in the shape of the disc of the moon. These six signs, arising from the appearance of the cloudless sky, are the day yoga and the yoga free of imagining.

Then, preceded by seeing in the midst of that the image of the Buddha whose nature is the enjoyment body, in a single instant one sees luminous forms of every variety, such as vases and cloth, possessing all aspects, clear like water lying in the palm of the hand.

"Closed" and so on: with both eyes half opened toward the sky, gazing at emptiness, that is, at what is free of apprehended and apprehender, one should constantly meditate on the reflection, the smoke and the rest, in the direct perception of a yoga that does not imagine. Both yogas are taught in the Kālacakra with the words "attached to space" and so on. They are also taught in the Perfection of Wisdom: the meditative absorption called Jewel Light, the one called Jewel Lamp, the one called Moonlight, the one called Sun Lamp, the one called Seeing All Aspects, and so on. Therefore those who wish to awaken should, without doubt, meditate on the path of smoke and the rest.

Someone may ask: "But the Great Seal is free of conceptual thought, while meditation is a superimposition; so how can it be said that one should meditate?" He replies with the verse beginning "Meditation." Whatever meditation the yogin performs on that very reflection, which in accordance with the instructions is meditated upon as having neither outside nor inside, is not meditation, because it is not an entity.

Someone may ask: "But whether it concerns an entity or a non-entity, there is no difference as far as conception goes; so how does one meditate on a non-entity?" He replies with the verse beginning "Mind." The reason is that the yogin has no conception of entity or non-entity regarding the reflection of emptiness, because the reflection of emptiness is seen directly in the unimagined smoke and the rest. The intent is this: if it were imagined, it would become an entity, like a cast image and so on. If it were not seen, it would be a non-entity, like the son of a barren woman and so on. Here, since it is seen directly without imagining, everything stands well.

He gives an example of the seeing: the verse beginning "Casting a prasenā." A prasenā divination is performed by a practitioner. In a mirror, a sword, a thumbnail, a lamp, the moon, the sun or water, thieves and the like are seen as objects of the eye that do not arise from an entity, that do not arise from material things; and in this seeing without imagining there is neither entity nor non-entity, because, though there is no entity, a mere capacity to perform a function is observed. In the world itself, what is not an entity is seen as an entity, as in illusions, dreams and the like.

"Possessing qualities" and so on: the nonconceptual wisdom that arises accomplishes the welfare of beings in every way, and so it is like a wish-fulfilling jewel. He gives an example: the verse beginning "Previously." Thieves and the like that were not seen before are seen when a young girl, with eyes blessed by mantra, casts the prasenā. The practitioner then goes to the place the girl described and sees them with limited, ordinary eyes. The thought is this: with the eye of intellect blessed by the mantra of the instructions for the practice, the yogin, like the young girl, because his awakening mind is undiminished, sees in space the transmundane suchness that has not been shown and is not a place ordinary people can go.

That too is explained as free of all elaboration: the verse beginning "By another." It is not seen with another's eyes, because the smoke is not seen anywhere else. It is not seen with one's own eyes, because the eyes are covered with cloth. Through this example of the prasenā divination, yogic direct perception, being entirely without intrinsic nature, is unborn; and, like a young woman perceiving a son in a dream, the wisdom of omniscience is accomplished.

Whatever virtue I have gained
by explaining the six-limbed yoga,
by it may the entire world
attain undiminished bliss.

This completes the Commentary on the Six-Limbed Yoga, composed by the great paṇḍita Raviśrījñāna.

The great paṇḍita of eastern India, Śrī Vibhūticandra, translated it, edited it and established it at the glorious temple of Gyatak, in the district of Dringtsam in the heart of Rulak.

Translator's notes

The author of this commentary names himself Raviśrī (Tibetan Nyima Pal), that is, Raviśrījñāna; the root text is the Six-Limbed Yoga of Anupamarakṣita, whose opening verses are cited here only by their first words. The limb rendered "withdrawal (pratyāhāra)" is written in the Tibetan as so sor rtog pa, literally "individual discernment," rather than the usual so sor sdud pa; it is taken as the first of the six Kālacakra limbs since the commentary maps the verse onto all six in order. In the first verse gloss, the Tibetan reads "realizing" (rtogs pa) where the following gloss speaks of "conceptions in all their number" (rtog pa); the verse may originally speak of freedom from countless conceptions. "Son of the overseer" renders tshod dpon gyi bu; this probably reflects the epithet Sādhuputra borne by Śrīdhara(ānanda) in the Kālacakra lineage, but the identification is not certain. "The lord of the twelfth stage" renders "its twelfth lord," taken as the twelfth ground of the Kālacakra system. Evident spelling slips read as intended: sbrul ba for sprul pa "emanation" and phyi for phyir "because." The "later Kālacakra Tantra" spoken by the King of Śambhala refers to the abridged (Laghu) Kālacakra Tantra. The phrase rendered "the basis for overcoming the malicious" reads literally "the support of the malicious" (gdug pa'i rten); its sense in this context is unclear. In "destroys the whole host of conceptions" the Tibetan writes rtogs pa ("realization"), read here as rtog pa ("conception"), the only reading that fits the verb. "The words beginning 'That'" renders de bsan la sogs pa; the second syllable is obscure, and only the opening word "that" is clear. In the gloss on "hūṃ phaṭ," the word sgos pa is unclear; it is taken as referring to the mudrās and similar features from which the completion stage is free. The divinatory image in the mirror (Tibetan phra phab, Sanskrit prasenā) is a well-known Indian rite in which a young girl sees hidden things such as a thief in a mirror; the commentary uses it as an image of seeing the empty forms. The "four when condensed and sixteen when divided" probably refers to the four joys and their sixteen subdivisions. The verses quoted from the Paramārthasevā are attributed by the tradition to Puṇḍarīka. "Being parted from it" reads me dang 'bral ba, literally "parted from fire," taken as a slip for de dang 'bral ba, "parted from it," which the context requires. In the gloss on the five deeds of immediate retribution, the syllable pa after "by which" is read as pha, "father," completing the list "father and mother." The plantain (Tibetan chu shing) is the classic image of a plant without a core that dies after bearing fruit; the she-mule and the scorpion are traditional Indian examples of offspring that cause their mother's death. "Three years" reads log sum, taken as a slip for lo gsum. "Arises of itself" reads rang gi dang gis, taken as rang gi ngang gis. "Flashes of light" renders 'od dum, literally "fragments of light." The "Very Joyful" is the first ground (pramuditā); in this system the twelfth is named the Eye of Primordial Wisdom. The ten winds are the five principal winds (prāṇa, apāna, samāna, udāna, vyāna) and the five secondary winds (nāga, kūrma, kṛkara, devadatta, dhanaṃjaya). The five nectars and five lamps are the standard tantric substances, here also read inwardly as the aggregates and the senses. "Not in order to let it fall" renders ltung ba'i phyir ma yin; in this context ltung ba refers to the emission or loss of the awakening mind (seminal essence), not to a moral transgression. "Like snow" is the traditional image of the awakening mind melting at the crown when the caṇḍālī fire is driven upward. "Enter another body" renders grong 'jug, the yoga of transferring consciousness into another body. "The section that summarizes the empowerment" may refer to the Sekoddeśa portion of the Ādibuddha. "With certainty" reads des pa, taken as nges pa; "in this very birth" reads dug, taken as du. The two short verse lines beginning "Relying on the reflection" are given as verse since they are set off in seven-syllable lines. The phrase rendered "twofold: the changing and the sluggish" (གཉིས་འགྱུར་བ་དང་བུལ་བ) is obscure as transmitted and the rendering is tentative. In "the yoga free of imagining," the reading བླགས་བ་མེད་པ makes no sense and is taken as a slip for བརྟགས་པ་མེད་པ ("unimagined"), matching the commentary's repeated "unimagined" (akalpita) seeing just below. In the quoted verse on the sword, རང་སོགས is taken as a slip for རང་ཉིད or the like ("itself"). A prasenā is a mantra-empowered divination in which a young girl sees hidden things in a mirror, sword blade, thumbnail or similar surface; the Kālacakra tradition uses it as the model for the yogic vision of empty forms.
