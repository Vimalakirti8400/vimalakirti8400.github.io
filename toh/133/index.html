About this text

The Noble Great Vehicle Sūtra called The Samādhi in Which the Buddhas of the Present Stand Directly Before One, known in Sanskrit as the Pratyutpannabuddhasaṃmukhāvasthitasamādhi Sūtra and in Tibetan as Phakpa Datar Gyi Sangye Ngönsum Du Zhukpé Tingngé Dzin, is a Mahāyāna discourse on a meditative absorption through which a practitioner comes to behold the buddhas of the present age as if they were standing right before them. Set at Rājagṛha in the Bamboo Grove at the Kalandaka Feeding Place, where the Buddha dwells with a great assembly of arhat monks, the sūtra explains how this samādhi is cultivated, the qualities and conduct that support it, and the insight into emptiness that accompanies such visionary encounters. It is regarded as one of the earliest Mahāyāna scriptures and is especially remembered for its teaching on visualizing the Buddha Amitābha, which made it influential in later devotional and meditative traditions. In the Tibetan canon it is Toh 133 in the Kangyur, where it belongs among the general sūtras of the Great Vehicle.

Bhadrapāla, these qualities will give rise to that samādhi. And what, Bhadrapāla, is the samādhi to which these qualities give rise? It is the samādhi called Direct Encounter with the Buddhas of the Present. And what, Bhadrapāla, is this samādhi called Direct Encounter with the Buddhas of the Present? Bhadrapāla, suppose a monk, a nun, a layman or a laywoman keeps moral discipline perfectly. Going alone to a solitary place and sitting there, that person should give rise to this thought: "In which direction does the Blessed One, the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha Amitāyus dwell, living and sustaining himself, and teaching the Dharma?" Then, in the way that he has heard it, he brings to mind: "Westward from this buddha field, past a hundred thousand koṭis of buddha fields, in the world realm Sukhāvatī, the Blessed One, the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha Amitāyus now dwells, surrounded and attended by a host of bodhisattvas. He lives, he sustains himself, and he teaches the Dharma." And with an undistracted mind he brings that Tathāgata to mind.

Bhadrapāla, it is like this. Suppose some man or woman, while asleep, sees forms in a dream. They see silver, or gold, or friends, or relatives, or blood kin, or companions, people who are pleasing, dear and not at all disagreeable. They dream that in the dream they play with them, rejoice, enjoy themselves to the full, and talk and converse with them. When they wake, they tell others everything just as they saw it, heard it, discerned it, cognized it, and spoke and conversed, and as they recall the images of the dream, they shed tears. In the same way, Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva, whether a householder or one who has gone forth, goes alone to a solitary place and sits. Bringing to mind the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha Amitāyus in the way that he has heard, with no flaw in his body of moral discipline and with undistracted mindfulness, he should keep him in mind for one day and night, or two, or three, or four, or five, or six, or for seven days and nights. If for seven days and nights he keeps the Tathāgata Amitāyus in mind without distraction, then when the full seven days and nights have passed, he will see the Blessed One, the Tathāgata Amitāyus. If he does not see the Blessed One during the day, then in a dream while he sleeps the Blessed One, the Tathāgata Amitāyus, will show him his face.

Bhadrapāla, it is like this. A woman or a man, in a dream while asleep, does not know whether they are in a house, nor know whether they are out in the open, and their eyes are not obstructed by walls, nor dimmed by darkness or haze. In the same way, Bhadrapāla, for that bodhisattva great being too, the spaces between world realms do not obstruct him; Sumeru, king of mountains, the Cakravāla, king of mountains, the Great Cakravāla, and likewise the other black mountains do not obstruct his eyes. Giving rise to his thought in just this way, that bodhisattva does not see the Tathāgata by having attained the divine eye; he does not hear the true Dharma by having attained the divine ear; he does not go to that world realm in an instant by having attained the power of miraculous abilities. Rather, Bhadrapāla, while remaining in this very world realm, that bodhisattva sees the Blessed One, the Tathāgata Amitāyus, and knows himself as though he were present in that world realm, and he also hears the Dharma. Having heard it taught, he retains all those teachings, masters them and holds them. He honors the Blessed One, the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha Amitāyus, esteems him as supreme, reveres him and makes offerings to him. And when that bodhisattva has arisen from that samādhi, he explains to others too, extensively and correctly, those teachings just as he heard them, just as he held them and just as he mastered them.

Fascicle two.

Bhadrapāla, it is like this. Suppose a man living in the great city of Rājagṛha heard that in the city of Vaiśālī there was a courtesan called Sumanā. A second man heard that there was a courtesan called Āmrapālī. A third man heard that there had once been a courtesan called Utpalavarṇā. Having heard of them, each of them became attached to one of them; yet those men did not see those courtesans. Merely by hearing of their names, their figures and their complexions, they gave rise to thoughts of desire. As they lay down to sleep while thinking of them again and again, they felt in their dreams as though they had gone into the presence of those courtesans. Just as those men, staying in the great city of Rājagṛha, had given rise to such thoughts of desire before they slept, so too, once asleep, those men dreamed that they saw those courtesans in their dreams, came together with them, engaged in intercourse, and were then freed from that craving for intercourse. When they woke, they recalled what they had experienced in the dream, just as they had seen, heard, known and understood it, and they went to where you were, Bhadrapāla, and told you about these things. Having heard about these things, you used this very teaching to teach them the Dharma in such a way that they would by all means become irreversible from unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening. And I prophesy that in a future time those men will become tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly complete buddhas, each called Suprabuddha.

Even though those persons have attained acceptance, they still see and recollect those former signs.

Bhadrapāla, in the same way, the bodhisattva who possesses this samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present, while still remaining in this very world-system, hears of the Blessed One, the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha Amitāyus. Having heard no more than the name, the form and the qualities of that Tathāgata, he recollects that Blessed One, the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha Amitāyus correctly, with undistracted mind. By attending to him again and again, he sees that Tathāgata.

Abiding in the bodhisattva's samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present, having seen that Tathāgata, he puts this question: "Blessed One, possessing what qualities are bodhisattva great beings born in this world-system?" Likewise, whatever buddhafield he wishes to be born in, he then asks that Tathāgata about it. When he has asked this, the Blessed One, the Tathāgata Amitāyus, says to that bodhisattva: "Son of good family, if one practices, fully practices, cultivates and makes much of the recollection of the Buddha, one is born in this world-system. If one practices, fully practices, cultivates and makes much of the recollection of the Buddha, one will be born in this buddhafield.

"Son of good family, what then is this recollection of the Buddha? It is attending to the Tathāgata in this way: he is the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha, endowed with knowledge and conduct, the Sugata, the knower of the world, the charioteer who tames persons, the unsurpassed one, the teacher of gods and humans, the Buddha, the Blessed One. He possesses the thirty-two marks of a great person and a body the color of gold. Like a golden image, shining, radiant and well established, he is beautifully adorned like a jeweled offering pillar. In the midst of the Saṅgha of śrāvakas he teaches the Dharma, and he teaches it in such a way that nothing is lost.

"If one asks what is not lost: he does not lose earth. He does not lose water, fire, wind, bhūtas, gods, brahmins or Prajāpati. He does not lose form. He does not lose feeling, perception, formations or consciousness.

"He does not take that Tathāgata as an object of conceit. He does not apprehend him, does not cling to him, does not fully know him, does not conceive of him, does not discriminate him, and does not fully see him. When, attending to the Tathāgata in this way without apprehending him, one attains the samādhi of emptiness, that is called the recollection of the Buddha."

That bodhisattva cultivates that samādhi, settles evenly in that samādhi, and rises from that samādhi. Then, Bhadrapāla, he goes to where you are, and when he has arrived he speaks of this samādhi. Then, Bhadrapāla, you teach him the Dharma in just such a way that the bodhisattva becomes irreversible from unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. And I too prophesy of that person: "In the future he will become the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha called Pratibhānaprāpta."

Bhadrapāla, you yourself, the elder Mahākāśyapa, the bodhisattva Indradatta, the bodhisattva Susīma, and any other bodhisattvas who have attained this samādhi have gained mastery over this samādhi.

Bhadrapāla, long ago in the past, a certain man went into a wilderness desert. Hungry and thirsty, he was overcome by torpor and sleep, and he lay down. In a dream he found plenty of food and drink. Having found it, he ate as much as he wanted, and his hunger and thirst disappeared. When he woke up, his body and belly were full. He thought, "There is indeed such a thing as this, which is like a dream." By understanding it in this way, he attained acceptance of the unarisen nature of dharmas, and he also became irreversible from unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening.

Bhadrapāla, in the same way, a bodhisattva, whether a householder or one who has gone forth, who hears that a Tathāgata dwells in this or that direction, and who attends to that Tathāgata facing that direction, will attain the vision of the Buddha. He should not form the notion of a thing; he should form the notion of space. Firmly abiding in the notion of space and attending closely to the notion of the Buddha, that bodhisattva will see the Tathāgata directly, like a fine image of beryl. That bodhisattva will see the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha in just this way.

Bhadrapāla, it is like this. Suppose a certain man leaves the land of his birth and goes to another region. When he has arrived there, he recollects his native land, and as he attends to the things he saw, heard, knew and understood there, he is overcome by torpor and sleep. He lies down, and in a dream he goes to his native land and believes that he is there himself. He sees again those things he had seen, heard, distinguished and cognized before. There he goes about and comes back. When he wakes up, he recounts these matters among his kinsmen, friends, relatives and blood relations: "I went to such and such places. I saw these things. There I experienced these things."

Bhadrapāla, in the same way, a bodhisattva great being, whether a householder or one who has gone forth, who hears that a Tathāgata dwells in this or that direction, should attend to that Tathāgata facing that direction, mindful and with undistracted mind, in order to attain the vision of the Buddha. In this way that bodhisattva will see the Tathāgata abiding as a fine image of beryl.

"Bhadrapāla, it is like this. Suppose a monk who cultivates the meditation on the unattractive sees bloated forms before him. He sees before him corpses that are bluish, festering, reddened, gnawed by animals, with the flesh stripped away, without flesh and blood, white, the color of a conch shell, or a skeleton. From the bluish corpse up to the skeleton, these have not come from anywhere, and they have not gone anywhere. No one made them, and no one stopped them. Nevertheless, Bhadrapāla, through the power of that monk's one-pointed mind, he sees a skeleton lying before him.

"In the same way, Bhadrapāla, bodhisattvas who are upheld by the buddhas and who abide in this samādhi turn their minds toward whatever direction a tathāgata, an arhat, a perfectly complete buddha is dwelling in. They do this, toward each of those directions, in order to gain the vision of the Buddha. Because they have turned their minds toward each of those directions, they see those tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly complete buddhas in each of them. Why is that? Bhadrapāla, this gaining of the vision of the buddhas is the natural outcome of this samādhi. Three things come together and combine for a bodhisattva who abides in this samādhi: the might of the Buddha, the strength of his own roots of virtue that he has generated, and his having attained the samādhi. Because of this, he sees the tathāgatas, and they appear to him.

"Bhadrapāla, for example, suppose there is some woman or man who likes to wash their hair and adorn themselves. Wishing to look at themselves, they gaze into a vessel of clear oil, or a vessel of clear water, or a well-polished mirror, or a patch of ground smeared with blue pigment, and there they see their own form. Bhadrapāla, what do you think of that? When the form of the man or woman appears in the vessel of clear oil, the vessel of clear water, the well-polished mirror, or the ground smeared with blue pigment, has that man or woman gone into it, or entered into it?"

Bhadrapāla replied, "Venerable Blessed One, that is not so. Rather, Venerable Blessed One, the reflection appears before them because the oil and the water are clear and free of murk, because the mirror is well polished, and because the great ground smeared with blue pigment is clean. The man's or woman's body has not emerged from the water, the oil, the mirror, or the great ground. It has not come from anywhere, it has not gone anywhere, it has not arisen from anywhere, and it has not ceased anywhere."

The Blessed One said, "Excellent, Bhadrapāla, excellent! You have done well, Bhadrapāla. So it is, Bhadrapāla. It is just as you have said: when forms are thoroughly pure, reflections appear. In the same way, because that bodhisattva has cultivated this samādhi well, he sees those tathāgatas with little difficulty. Having seen them, he asks them questions, and he rejoices when his questions are answered.

"He thinks, 'Where have these tathāgatas come from? Where have I gone to?' He then understands clearly that the tathāgata has not come from anywhere. Recognizing that his own body has not gone anywhere either, he thinks: 'These three realms are mind only. Why is that? Because however I conceive of things, that is how they appear. That mind is not inside, and it is not outside, nor is it found apart from both. Rather, it arises in dependence on an object. Whatever arises dependently is without real entity. Whatever is without real entity is unborn. Whatever is unborn cannot be apprehended. Whatever cannot be apprehended is empty of intrinsic nature. Whatever is empty of intrinsic nature cannot be designated. Whatever cannot be designated cannot be seen, cognized, clung to, shown, destroyed, or established.'"

Bhadrapāla, therefore a bodhisattva should give rise to enthusiasm and great diligence in order to attain this samādhi, that is, in order to accomplish it fully. Bhadrapāla, it is like this. Suppose a great ocean-going ship filled with jewels were to cross the great ocean, escaping every danger, and then break apart at the near shore. The people of Jambudvīpa would raise a great clamor, a great outcry and wailing over it, and in great grief they would lament, "We have lost these jewels!"

Bhadrapāla, in the same way, suppose this precious samādhi reaches the ears of any son of good family or daughter of good family, any monk, nun, layman or laywoman, and having heard it, they do not copy it into a book, do not teach it, do not recite it, do not retain it, do not read it, do not explain it, do not meditate on it, and do not earnestly put it into practice. Then, Bhadrapāla, the world with its gods will raise a great clamor, a great outcry and wailing over that.

For when they hear this kind of profound and precious samādhi, which the buddhas have praised, which the buddhas have extolled, which the buddhas have caused others to extol, and through which all the qualities of a buddha are attained, they do not copy it in writing, do not teach it, do not recite it, do not retain it, do not read it, do not explain it, do not meditate on it, and do not earnestly put it into practice. Overcome by pride, they do not understand. One should even lament with great sorrow, saying, "Those beings who do not hold to complete and extensive learning have fallen away entirely from this kind of precious samādhi!"

Bhadrapāla, I declare that those beings who become unwilling to listen to this samādhi, and who do not take up this samādhi, are, Bhadrapāla, of a nature to fall away entirely. Bhadrapāla, see how greatly those beings fall away who, having heard this kind of precious samādhi, do not copy it in writing, do not teach it, do not recite it, do not retain it, do not read it, do not explain it, do not meditate on it, and do not earnestly put it into practice. Bhadrapāla, I declare that for such beings, teachings like these are of no great purpose.

Bhadrapāla, it is like this. Suppose some person were to give a piece of fresh red sandalwood to a man of childish nature and corrupt understanding, and he were to conceive of it as unclean, to conceive of it as filthy. Then a sandalwood merchant of wise nature would say to him, "O man, do not conceive of this fresh sandalwood as unclean! Do not conceive of it as filthy! O man, just smell how delightful its fragrance is! O man, look too at how beautiful its color is!" Yet that man of childish nature and corrupt understanding, seeing the red sandalwood, would not take it himself, because he did not want to smell it, and would shut his eyes, because he did not want to look at it.

Just so, Bhadrapāla, in the future there will be monks, bodhisattvas full of conceit, undeveloped in body, undeveloped in mind, undeveloped in moral discipline, undeveloped in wisdom, of corrupt discipline, who do not take up the precious true Dharma of definitive meaning, who reject the precious true Dharma of definitive meaning, of corrupt wisdom, fallen entirely away from what is excellent, overweeningly proud because they have gained meditative concentration, relying on a person, viewing things in terms of a person, abiding in a person, clinging to the apprehension of phenomena as objects, and frightened by the teaching of emptiness. When they hear this samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present being explained, they will not lend an ear and listen to it, will not have faith in it, will not take it up, will not master it, will not keep it, and will not even read it, let alone explain it extensively to others. Because of this they do not even attain vast loving-kindness and joy, let alone practice in accordance with suchness; and on top of that, having heard it, they reject it. They have no faith in it. They do not accept it. They take no delight in it. With a mocking attitude, a scornful attitude, and a contemptuous attitude, they even say, "How these scriptures shine! How marvelous that someone like the monk Ānanda appears in the world, and that a sūtra like this appears!" And going off in secret, they say to one another, "Sūtras like these are self-made, fabricated. This was not spoken by the Buddha; it was not sanctioned by the Buddha." Saying such things, they revile it. Such foolish people should be known to have fallen entirely away from the precious true Dharma and to have rejected the precious true Dharma.

Bhadrapāla, it is like this. Just as that man of childish nature, seeing the red sandalwood, blocks his nose so as not to smell it and shuts his eyes so as not to see it, just so, Bhadrapāla, when those foolish people hear this kind of precious samādhi, they do not think to listen to it again and again, nor do they think to take it up, to master it, to keep it, or to read it; and on top of that, having heard it, they will leave that place because they do not wish to listen.

Bhadrapāla, it is like this. Suppose some jewel merchant thought to show a priceless precious beryl gem to some people of foolish nature. Those people of foolish nature, seeing the precious gem, would say to him, "O man, what is the price of this precious gem?" The jewel merchant would say to them, "Give me as much land as the light of this precious gem pervades, in all four directions, completely filled with the seven precious substances. And why? Because that is the full price of this precious gem." When those beings of foolish nature heard from him the price of the precious gem, they would burst out laughing, scoff, and sneer, and would weigh the precious gem in their two hands.

Just so, Bhadrapāla, in the future there will be monks with many conceptual thoughts and little merit, who have not generated roots of virtue, who have not rendered service to the Victors of the past, tormented by pride, tormented by jealousy, tormented by gain, honor, and renown, whose moral discipline is not pure, who have not attained the samādhis, who have not attained wisdom, of little learning, who do not keep company with teachers, who view things in terms of a person, rely on a person, abide in a person, and cling to the apprehension of phenomena as objects. They will be frightened by the teaching of emptiness. Having heard this samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present, they will not reflect on it, will not have faith in it, will not trust it, and will not take delight in it; on top of that, even when they hear it, they will burst out laughing, will scoff, and will sneer. And going off in secret, they will revile it and reject it among themselves, and they will even say, "These monks are brazen.

These monks talk nonsense. These things were never spoken by the Buddha. They are their own making, their own fabrications, assorted words and letters, mere talk, and yet they explain them under the name of sūtras. How astonishing!" And saying, "These sūtras were not spoken by the Buddha," they will lead other people to understand them in that way too. Thus those foolish people of little merit, who have not generated roots of virtue, who have not served the victors of the past, who have utterly rejected the unsurpassed precious Dharma and have become outsiders to the precious Dharma, will resort to their very poverty of merit, cultivate it, and make much of it.

Bhadrapāla, at that time there will also be certain other beings who have served the victors of the past and have generated roots of virtue. Having heard this samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present, they will take it up so as to spread it. They will master it, write it down, have it written down, expound it, and recite it, and they will also teach it extensively and correctly to others. They will have heightened faith, will reflect on it much, and will have much devotion. Their conduct will be serene. They will have a sense of shame and remorse. They will long for the training and be devoted to the profound. They will be endowed with wisdom and will be very learned. They will abide in loving-kindness and attain compassion. So that this samādhi may be heard, wherever they go they will teach it there and teach it correctly. They will form this intention: "May this samādhi of the bodhisattvas, spoken by the Buddha, by all means long endure! May it spread!"

Bhadrapāla, before this assembly with its gods, humans, and asuras, I declare this to you, I make you understand it. Compare a son or daughter of good family who fills this great trichiliocosm with the seven precious substances and offers it to the tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly complete buddhas, with a son or daughter of good family who listens to this bodhisattva samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present, and having heard it, reflects on it, has faith in it, trusts it, is convinced, "This is the very truth," and also declares in words, "It was spoken by the Buddha." The merit of the latter son or daughter of good family will increase far more.

The third fascicle.

Bhadrapāla, as for those who please me now, while I am teaching this samādhi: that in the future, in the latter time, in the final five hundred years, they should hear this samādhi and reject it or revile it is impossible; there is no possibility of it, except for those who have fallen into the hands of evil friends or are separated from spiritual friends. Bhadrapāla, if even some foolish people who have come into the hands of spiritual friends hear this samādhi and do not have faith in it, do not trust it, and are not devoted to it, what need is there even to mention those who have fallen into the hands of evil friends? And why is that, Bhadrapāla? Great learning is just that hard to accomplish. Seeing the buddhas is just that hard to obtain. Bhadrapāla, devotion to the qualities of a buddha is just that difficult. Bhadrapāla, this bodhisattva samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present, which gives rise to all the qualities of a buddha, is likewise hard to accomplish.

Bhadrapāla, consider any bodhisattva, whether a householder or one who has gone forth, who hears a samādhi such as this and is not afraid, not frightened, not terrified. He does not laugh at it, does not scoff, does not sneer, and does not reject it. Beyond that, having heard it, he rejoices in it, has faith in it, trusts it, and aspires to it, and he gives rise to the wish to teach this samādhi, to take it up, to master it, to retain it, to read it, to write it down, to explain it, and to cultivate it. Bhadrapāla, all such sons or daughters of good family are seen by the Tathāgata. They are known by the Tathāgata.

"Bhadrapāla, how does the Tathāgata see such holy persons, and how does the Tathāgata know them? Bhadrapāla, such holy persons will take up this meditative absorption, master it, retain it, recite it, write it down, teach it, and cultivate it. They will not be of corrupt moral discipline. They will not be without faith. They will not be averse to the Dharma. They will not hold wrong views. They will not be attached to objects of focus. Rather, Bhadrapāla, such sons or daughters of good family will be endowed with faith. That is to say, such sons and daughters of good family who take up teachings such as these will be given to much reflection and much devotion. They will have conviction, they will love the Dharma, and they will be drawn to the profound.

"Bhadrapāla, such sons or daughters of good family will not be of little merit or few roots of virtue. Rather, Bhadrapāla, such sons or daughters of good family have done service to the Victors of the past, and their roots of virtue are blazing. Bhadrapāla, such sons and daughters of good family have not honored and served only a single buddha. They have not generated roots of virtue under only one, two, or three buddhas. Rather, Bhadrapāla, such sons of good family will have honored and served a hundred buddhas. Such sons of good family will have generated roots of virtue under a hundred buddhas. From those tathāgatas, those sons or daughters of good family will have heard this meditative absorption, rejoiced in it, and been devoted to it. In the later time, in the later age, even in the final five hundred years, when they hear this meditative absorption they will not reject it. More than that, on hearing it they will rejoice, they will offer the words 'Excellent!', they will retain it, recite it, receive its transmission, explain it, and strive in the practice of cultivating it.

"Bhadrapāla, suppose some son or daughter of good family hears this bodhisattva meditative absorption in which the buddhas of the present stand directly before one, and is not frightened, not terrified, not filled with dread, and does not reject it. More than that, on hearing it, they rejoice, they have firm faith, they have firm conviction, they are devoted, and they offer the words 'Excellent!' Having heard it, they take it up, master it, retain it, recite it, write it down, have it written down, and teach it, and even for just a few days they diligently strive in the practice of cultivation in order to cultivate this meditative absorption. Then, Bhadrapāla, those sons or daughters of good family will generate much merit on that basis. They will generate a heap of merit that is immeasurable and incalculable. Those sons and daughters of good family will become irreversible from unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. They will also attain complete fulfillment, just as they intend.

"Nevertheless, Bhadrapāla, in order to make that very point still more complete, I shall give you an example. Bhadrapāla, it is like this. Suppose a certain person came along and ground this great trichiliocosm, this world system of a billion worlds, into particles of atomic dust. Suppose he also ground into particles of atomic dust all the grasses, branches, leaves, and petals of this great trichiliocosm, down to those as small as four finger-widths. Then that person took a single particle of atomic dust from among them and divided it into as many parts as there are particles of atomic dust in all, and in that manner divided every one of those particles of atomic dust into that many parts. Bhadrapāla, what do you think? Would those particles of atomic dust be many?"

The bodhisattva Bhadrapāla replied, "Venerable Blessed One, they would be many. Venerable Sugata, they would be many."

The Blessed One said, "Bhadrapāla, suppose some son or daughter of good family were to fill as many buddha-fields as there are parts of those particles of atomic dust with the seven precious substances and present them as an offering to the tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly and completely awakened buddhas. Would they generate much merit on that basis?"

He replied, "Venerable Blessed One, it would be much. Venerable Sugata, it would be much."

The Blessed One said, "Bhadrapāla, have faith in this. You should understand it. Bhadrapāla, there is a son or daughter of good family who fills that many buddha-fields with the seven precious substances and presents them as an offering to the Tathāgata. But, Bhadrapāla, suppose a son or daughter of good family hears this bodhisattva meditative absorption in which the buddhas of the present stand directly before one, and examines it, has firm faith in it, has conviction in it, and is devoted to it; and having examined it, having firm faith, conviction, and devotion, takes it up, masters it, retains it, recites it, writes it down, and also explains it extensively to others, teaches it, and correctly expounds it. The heap of merit of this son or daughter of good family grows far greater than those earlier roots of virtue. What need then to mention one who abides striving in the practice of cultivation even for as long as it takes to milk a cow?

"Bhadrapāla, I do not even speak of the measure of the heap of merit of those sons or daughters of good family who hear this meditative absorption, take it up, master it, retain it, recite it, write it down, and also make it extensively understood by others, teach it, and correctly expound it. What need then to mention, Bhadrapāla, the son or daughter of good family who accomplishes this meditative absorption, trains in suchness, and applies themselves diligently to suchness?"

This concludes the seventh chapter, the chapter on the samādhi requested by Bhadrapāla.

Bhadrapāla, how, then, should a bodhisattva great being cultivate this samādhi? Bhadrapāla, just as I am now seated before you teaching the Dharma, in the same way, Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva should bring to mind those tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly complete buddhas as seated on the seat of a buddha and teaching the Dharma. He should bring the tathāgatas to mind as endowed with the supreme of every aspect, of excellent form, beautiful, lovely to behold, and possessed of a perfect body. He should regard each one of the major marks of a great being on the body of a tathāgata, arhat, perfectly complete buddha as produced by a hundred merits. He should also take hold of their features. He should also inquire thoroughly into the crown of the head, whose top cannot be seen. Having inquired, he should take hold of the features of the major marks of a great being. Having taken hold of them, he should train in this way: "Ah, how wondrous is the beauty of those tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly complete buddhas! I too, in a future time, will come to possess such a perfect body as this. Such marks as these will be complete in me. I too will come to possess moral discipline such as this, and likewise samādhi, likewise wisdom, likewise liberation, and likewise the seeing of the wisdom of liberation. I too, in the same way, will fully and perfectly awaken to unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening. Having fully and perfectly awakened, I will teach the Dharma to the fourfold assembly and to the world with its gods." Thus he should train.

Having thus brought this to mind as endowed with the supreme of all aspects, he should further train in this way, thinking: "What is that thing called 'I'? And what is that thing called 'mine'? What, too, is awakening? And who is it that fully and perfectly awakens to awakening? Does one fully and perfectly awaken to awakening with the body, or does one fully and perfectly awaken to awakening with the mind? If one were to say that one fully and perfectly awakens to awakening with the body, the body is inert matter, motionless, without agency, without volition, like grass, wood, stone, a wall, or a mirage, while awakening is formless, indemonstrable, invisible, and cannot be made known. How then could one, with a body that is inert, motionless, without agency, and without volition, fully and perfectly awaken to an awakening that is formless, indemonstrable, invisible, and cannot be made known? And if one were to say that one fully and perfectly awakens to awakening with the mind, the mind is formless, indemonstrable, invisible, cannot be made known, and is like an illusion, while awakening is formless, indemonstrable, invisible, and cannot be made known. How then could one, with a mind that is formless, indemonstrable, invisible, cannot be made known, and is like an illusion, fully and perfectly awaken to an awakening that is formless, indemonstrable, invisible, and cannot be made known?" In this way he should train.

Bhadrapāla, in this regard the bodhisattva should train in this way, thinking: "One does not fully and perfectly awaken to awakening with the body. One does not fully and perfectly awaken to awakening with the mind. Nor does what is without mind fully and perfectly awaken to awakening by means of mind. Nor does what is without form fully and perfectly awaken to awakening by means of form. Nor does mind fully and perfectly awaken to awakening by means of mind. Nor does form fully and perfectly awaken to awakening by means of form."

Bhadrapāla, why is that? The body of the Tathāgata has completely abandoned all grasping at extremes. Therefore the Tathāgata dwells contemplating the body in the body, and dwells contemplating the mind in the mind.

Bhadrapāla, the wisdom that fully knows all phenomena, a wisdom that is formless, indemonstrable, invisible, cannot be made known, and is undefiled, neither fully and perfectly awakens nor fails to fully and perfectly awaken. Why is that? Since the bodies of the tathāgatas are undefiled, the minds of the tathāgatas are undefiled, the form of the tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly complete buddhas, is also undefiled, and the feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness of the tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly complete buddhas, are also undefiled, what need is there even to mention that the moral discipline, the meditative absorption, the wisdom, the liberation, or the knowledge and vision of liberation of the tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly complete buddhas, are undefiled? Bhadrapāla, all the qualities of the tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly complete buddhas, are undefiled. Whatever true Dharma the tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly complete buddhas, have taught, teach, or will uphold, that Dharma too is undefiled. Childish and unskilled people do not know it, but the wise fully realize it.

Bhadrapāla, at that time the bodhisattva should train in this way, thinking: "Does one awaken to awakening with the body, or does one fully and perfectly awaken to awakening with wisdom?" Bhadrapāla, in this regard the bodhisattva, the great being, should train in this way, thinking: "One does not awaken to awakening with the body, nor with wisdom either." Why is that? That wisdom is undefiled, and though it is thoroughly sought in every way, it is not found. For that reason he becomes equanimous with regard to the search for awakening. Why is that? Awakening, too, is undefiled, and though it is thoroughly sought in every way, it is not found. Though he thoroughly searches his own body and his own mind, he does not find them. In the same way, though he thoroughly searches all phenomena, he does not find them. He does not find any "who fully and perfectly awakens to awakening." He does not find any "by which one awakens to awakening." He neither finds nor sees any "what is to be fully and perfectly awakened to." When he examines all phenomena in this way, he does not truly see them. Although he does not truly see all phenomena as being by nature at peace, they are not by nature at peace. Why is that? Because what is unborn is not something that has been pacified. With regard to all phenomena that are unborn and at peace, he conceives of them as peaceful phenomena. With regard to all peaceful phenomena, he conceives of them as free from peace. Having examined in this way, he should not conceive of phenomena as "peaceful," nor should he conceive of them as not peaceful. Bhadrapāla, why is that? Because all phenomena are unborn and not fully established.

Bhadrapāla, suppose, for example, that some person, when no fire had been kindled and none was blazing, were to say, "I shall put out this fire." Bhadrapāla, what do you think of that? Would that person be speaking truthfully?

Bhadrapāla replied, "No indeed, venerable Blessed One."

The Blessed One said, "Bhadrapāla, it is the same, because all phenomena are not found. Whoever says, apart from worldly convention, 'I shall fully know all phenomena. I shall abandon all phenomena. I shall directly realize all phenomena. I shall cultivate all phenomena. I shall pacify all phenomena. I shall attain all phenomena. I shall attain the fruit of stream-entry.

"I will attain the fruit of once-returner. I will attain the fruit of non-returner. I will attain arhatship. I will attain solitary awakening. I will fully and manifestly awaken to unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. Having fully and manifestly awakened to unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening, I will teach the Dharma. I will completely free sentient beings from saṃsāra." If he were to say this, would he be speaking as one who speaks truly?

Bhadrapāla replied, "No indeed, Venerable Blessed One."

The Blessed One said, "Therefore, Bhadrapāla, sons or daughters of good family who wish for perfect and complete awakening, sons or daughters of good family who wish for solitary awakening, and those who wish for arhatship should examine those phenomena in that way. Having examined those phenomena in that way, they should not conceive of them as 'phenomena are at peace,' nor should they conceive of them as 'phenomena are not at peace.'

"Why is that, Bhadrapāla? If anyone conceives of all phenomena, which are nothing whatsoever, unborn and not established at all, as 'all phenomena are at peace,' that is one extreme. If one conceives of them as 'all phenomena are not at peace,' that is the second extreme. Bhadrapāla, not apprehending, not conceiving, not establishing, not attending to and not engaging in these two extremes of peace and non-peace is called the Middle Way, by way of enumeration and in accordance with the conventional teaching of the world. Ultimately, however, neither extreme nor middle can be apprehended in it.

"Why is that, Bhadrapāla? Because all phenomena are like space and equal to nirvāṇa. They are not annihilated, not destroyed, impermanent, and not everlastingly abiding; they do not abide in any place, they do not abide in any direction, they are without signs, they are incalculable. Since even the wise do not approach them through calculation and do not apprehend them, all phenomena are called incalculable.

"Bhadrapāla, in this way a bodhisattva who sees those tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly and completely awakened buddhas should not become attached to them. Why is that? Bhadrapāla, all phenomena are not something to be attached to; in them there is no attachment. They are called root-severed, rootless and without support.

"Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva should cultivate this bodhisattva samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present in such a way that, whenever he sees those buddhas, those blessed ones, he does not hold them as supreme, does not apprehend them, and does not grasp them wrongly. Why is that? Because, Bhadrapāla, the Tathāgata has declared, 'All phenomena are ungraspable, utterly at peace, like space, and equal to nirvāṇa.'

"Bhadrapāla, it is like this: the wise do not take hold of an ingot of gold that has been thoroughly heated and thoroughly smelted until it is like a glowing lump of iron. Why is that, Bhadrapāla? Because it is by nature hot. Bhadrapāla, although it is gold, the finest of precious things, still, Bhadrapāla, because it is so very hot, they do not take hold of it. In the same way, Bhadrapāla, when a bodhisattva, a great being, sees those tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly and completely awakened buddhas, he should not become attached to them. Nor should he become attached to form, feeling, perception, formations or consciousness. Nor should he become attached to moral discipline, samādhi, wisdom, liberation, or the knowledge and vision of liberation. He should not become attached to any of the qualities of a buddha. He should not become attached in any way at all. Why is that, Bhadrapāla? Because attachment produces all the phenomena of suffering, whose very nature is saṃsāra.

"Yet, Bhadrapāla, when a bodhisattva, a great being, sees those tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly and completely awakened buddhas, he should aspire to their surpassing qualities in this way: 'How marvelous are the great wisdom, the wisdom of a buddha, the wisdom of a tathāgata, the self-arisen wisdom, the wisdom equal to the unequaled, the wisdom that surpasses all three worlds, of the tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly and completely awakened buddhas, who are endowed with such wondrous qualities!' Yet the bodhisattva, the great being, should not become attached to that wisdom to which he is to aspire here. Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva, a great being, should not become attached even to this samādhi. Why is that? Bhadrapāla, these phenomena become directly manifest as free of attachment."

Then, at that time, the Blessed One spoke these verses:

In a polished mirror or a vessel of oil
an adorned woman gazes at her face,
and desire for it arises in her;
seeking pleasures, she runs about everywhere.
Deluded about a thing that does not exist,
the woman is unbearably tormented, afflicted by desire,
the woman deceived by her own body,
like one who does not know these phenomena to be hollow.
"I will awaken to awakening and come to know the deathless,
and I will free the beings tormented by suffering," he thinks:
the bodhisattva's mind is just like that.
It is through not knowing that one speaks of "sentient beings."
Ultimately, no being at all is found
who is born, who dies, or who passes into nirvāṇa.
Phenomena cannot be grasped; they are like the moon in the sky.
Though one seeks awakening, it is not found;
it is like a reflection, like the moon in the sky,
like an illusion, empty, like a mirage.
Phenomena are without substance, empty of intrinsic nature,
yet the childish always perceive them as existent.
The person wise in awakening who knows
that in this there are no afflictions whatsoever,
and who knows ultimately that the world is a delusion,
will become a buddha, chief of the world.
Buddhas and awakening are imputed by the mind,
and the mind too is pure, luminous by nature,
untainted, unmixed with any beings.
Whoever knows this awakens to supreme awakening.
All phenomena are free of defilement and formless,
isolated and empty, beyond conceptual thought,
free of attachment, the mind fully liberated.
One who knows this attains this samādhi.

Taking the body of the Victor as your object,
listen to the Dharma that is pure by nature.
Do not engage here in even the least meditation or contemplation;
then this samādhi will not be hard to find.
If one abides in the perception of space,
the perception of atoms will cease.
One who knows phenomena as free of thought, unmade and indestructible,
attains this samādhi.
Knowing all forms to be free of conceptual thought,
wherever the eye may look, one is not attached.
Like seven suns risen in this world,
so will he see the buddhas.
The eye is pure, the ear is perfectly pure;
having undertaken diligence, mindful and with vigilance,
having cultivated this samādhi well,
one retains inconceivably vast learning.
That which is the samādhi of not seeing,
that which is the samādhi of all beings,
by which one does not see, by which one does not perceive:
here many other non-Buddhists (tīrthikas) come to ruin.
When the perceptions of signs have been dispelled,
his mind is pure, and he sees the buddhas.
Having seen, he once again does not see,
and he will become a buddha, chief of the world.
Before him there is no earth, nor any water,
no fire, no wind, and no space.
Yet he perceives the buddhas present
and teaching the sacred Dharma.
Just as those who desire the Dharma
look upon me as I sit teaching the Dharma,
so for him, apart from the buddhas and the supreme Dharma,
no other object arises.
For those endowed in this way,
apart from the many buddhas teaching
this stainless samādhi they have well upheld,
no other hearer or teacher at all is perceived.
There has been no buddha in the past
who did not teach this peaceful, noble samādhi,
nor will there be any in the future,
nor is there any present who will not teach it.
I too, who have now appeared in the world
as the highest of humans for the benefit of living beings,
recollecting these buddha guides,
teach this peaceful samādhi, so hard to see.
One who, heedless of body and likewise of life,
wishes to seek the qualities of the buddhas,
by abiding in this samādhi
will not find buddha awakening hard to attain.
The wise one who wishes to see
the many supreme humans of a single nature and to question them
should hurry, hurry, and swiftly
cultivate the stainless, pure samādhi.
Whoever has set out for the benefit of beings
and seeks vast, inconceivable learning
should hurry, hurry, and swiftly
cultivate the stainless, pure samādhi.
In this there is no attachment, no aversion;
in this there is no delusion, no envy;
in this there is neither knowing nor not knowing.
Therefore, come to know this peaceful samādhi.

This concludes the eighth chapter of the Samādhi Requested by Bhadrapāla.

When the Blessed One had spoken thus, the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, said to the Blessed One, "Venerable Blessed One, it is wonderful that the Blessed One has taught this profound samādhi in this way. Venerable Blessed One, if bodhisattva great beings who have gone forth from the household wish, having heard this samādhi, to receive its transmission or to cultivate it, then, Blessed One, abiding in which qualities should such a bodhisattva receive the transmission of this samādhi and cultivate it?"

When he had asked this, the Blessed One said to the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, "Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva who has gone forth from the household and who, having heard this samādhi, wishes to receive its transmission or to cultivate it, should make his moral discipline completely pure. He should keep his moral discipline free of fault."

The bodhisattva Bhadrapāla asked, "Venerable Blessed One, how is the moral discipline of a bodhisattva who has gone forth from the household completely pure and free of fault?"

The Blessed One said, "Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva who has gone forth from the household and who, having heard this samādhi, wishes to receive its transmission or to cultivate it, should be restrained by the vow of individual liberation (prātimokṣa). He should be perfect in conduct and in his sphere of activity, see danger in even the slightest misdeed, and keep his deportment completely pure. Having properly taken them up, he should train in the bases of training. He should have devotion to the profound and acceptance of what cannot be objectified. On hearing the teachings of emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness, he should not be afraid, he should not be terrified, and he should not become frightened. Bhadrapāla, it is in this way that the moral discipline of a bodhisattva who has gone forth from the household is completely pure and free of fault."

The bodhisattva Bhadrapāla asked, "Venerable Blessed One (the Buddha), in what way is the moral discipline of a bodhisattva who has gone forth from the household life not completely pure, and in what way is that moral discipline faulty?"

The Blessed One said, "Bhadrapāla, some bodhisattvas who have gone forth from the household life live the holy life while abiding in form, and live the holy life while abiding in feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness. They say, 'Through this moral discipline, austerity, and holy life of mine, may I become a god, or one among the gods.' This, Bhadrapāla, is the moral discipline of a bodhisattva gone forth from the household life that is not completely pure; it is moral discipline that has faults. That is to say, because they hold the notion of existence to be supreme, wish to indulge in sense pleasures, and wish to take up a place of birth in existence, their moral discipline is not completely pure.

"Therefore, Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva who has gone forth from the household life and who, having heard this samādhi, wishes to explain it or to meditate on it, should make their moral discipline completely pure. They should make their moral discipline faultless. They should keep their moral discipline unadulterated. They should keep their moral discipline unbroken. They should keep their moral discipline unblemished. They should make it moral discipline without reliance, moral discipline without holding it as supreme, moral discipline without any objectifying reference, moral discipline that is not destroyed, moral discipline praised by the wise, and moral discipline praised by the noble ones.

"They should delight in giving. That is, they should apply diligence to the gift of the Dharma, the supreme gift, the unsurpassed gift. They should establish mindfulness. They should be endowed with faith. They should be gentle, endowed with a sense of shame, and perfect in conscience. They should not be excessively attached to gain, honor, and verses of praise. They should be without stinginess and without jealousy, should possess the qualities of ascetic practice, and should keep few belongings. They should not pursue worldly talk; giving up such talk entirely, they should pursue talk that transcends the world. They should be grateful and repay kindness. They should be quiet and speak little. They should be respectful and reverent. That is to say, since a spiritual friend is hard to find, they should be eager to serve their master and their preceptor.

"Toward the person from whom they will hear such teachings as these and receive them, and have them written down in volumes, and on whose verbal teaching they depend, and toward the person from whom they master such a sūtra as this, or will master it, or copy it in volumes, or have it written, they should generate the notion that this is their father. They should generate the notion that this is a spiritual friend. They should generate the notion that this is the Teacher. For the sake of the meaning of such teachings as these, and in order to bring awakening to full maturity, they should generate toward that person still greater joy, faith, and respect.

"Bhadrapāla, if sons or daughters of good family belonging to the bodhisattva vehicle, the śrāvaka vehicle, or the pratyekabuddha vehicle do not generate joy, faith, and respect toward a monk who expounds such teachings as these, and do not generate the notion of a spiritual friend nor the notion of the Teacher, then, Bhadrapāla, it is impossible, there is no occasion, that those sons or daughters of good family of the bodhisattva vehicle, the śrāvaka vehicle, or the pratyekabuddha vehicle will master such teachings that they have not yet mastered, or that the teachings they have already mastered will remain, will not go to waste, and will not disappear. It is also impossible that a son of good family of the bodhisattva vehicle will attain this samādhi. And why is that, Bhadrapāla? Because through disrespect the true Dharma will decline.

"Bhadrapāla, if sons or daughters of good family of the bodhisattva vehicle, or of the śrāvaka or pratyekabuddha vehicle, generate joy, faith, and respect toward a monk who teaches such teachings as these, and toward those from whom they will master such a Dharma discourse as this, who have mastered it, and who have written it down in volumes, and if they establish the notion of a spiritual friend and the notion of the Teacher, then, Bhadrapāla, it is possible that those sons or daughters of good family of the bodhisattva vehicle, the śrāvaka vehicle, or the pratyekabuddha vehicle will master teachings they have not mastered, and that the teachings they have mastered will remain, will not go to waste, and will not disappear. That is indeed possible.

"Therefore, Bhadrapāla, you should have faith in this. You should understand it. In this way, you should generate joy, faith, and respect toward the monks who teach such teachings as these. You should generate the notion that they are spiritual friends and the notion that they are the Teacher. This is my instruction.

"Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva who has gone forth from the household life and who wishes to receive the transmission of this samādhi and wishes to meditate on it should be free from remorse. They should delight in the wilderness, incline toward the wilderness, tend toward the wilderness, and settle in the wilderness. They should take no delight in the settled countryside. Not wanting the houses of friends or the houses of those who give food, they should have no regard for their own lives. Giving up the body, they should not be attached to the body, and should attain the perception of the wilderness. They should not rely on gain, honor, and verses of praise, and should firmly uphold the true Dharma. They should not be attached to their bowl and robes, and should not hoard them. They should go for alms without needing to seek invitations as a guest. They should be scrupulous and conscientious. They should not take gold as their own. Being free of the outbursts of affliction, they should be free from remorse. Having abandoned anger, they should abide in loving-kindness. Having abandoned harmfulness, they should abide in compassion. Having abandoned discontent, they should abide in joy. Having abandoned all signs, they should abide in equanimity. Having undertaken diligence, they should aspire to the trainings.

Not overcome by torpor and sleep, one should engage in walking meditation. Therefore, Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva who has gone forth from the household life, has heard this samādhi, and wishes to take it up and cultivate it should abide in these qualities. He should take up this samādhi, and he should cultivate it.

When he had said this, the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, said to the Blessed One (the Buddha): "Venerable Blessed One, it is wonderful that the Tathāgata has taught and spoken these vast and excellent teachings. Venerable Blessed One, the lazy bodhisattvas who will appear will hear this samādhi and be frightened, be terrified, and become utterly terrified. Even when such excellent teachings are explained, they will not gain vast joy, delight, and faith. They will think, 'We shall accomplish this samādhi under some other tathāgata, arhat, perfectly complete buddha. For now our bodies are emaciated and we have many illnesses.' When they hear this samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present, they will become discouraged and utterly discouraged, and they will not even exert diligence to accomplish this samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present.

"Then again, Venerable Blessed One, there will appear bodhisattva great beings who abide in mindfulness with diligence undertaken; who seek the Dharma, respect the Dharma, uphold the Dharma, teach the Dharma, speak the Dharma, and take up the Dharma; who practice the Dharma in accordance with the Dharma; who give up body and life; who do not rely on gain, honor, and verses; who do not seek renown for their good qualities; who are not attached to bowl and robes; who take no delight in villages, towns, market towns, regions, kingdoms, provinces, and royal courts; who incline toward solitude, tend toward solitude, and have arrived at solitude. When they hear this bodhisattva samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present, they will not become discouraged, will not become utterly discouraged, will not be frightened, will not be terrified, and will not become utterly terrified. More than that, they will gain vast joy, delight, and faith. They will also undertake great diligence in order to grasp, master, retain, read, write out, teach, and cultivate this samādhi. Venerable Blessed One, these sons or daughters of good family will not seek great learning from buddhas who have not yet come. Nor will they put off their striving to accomplish the distinctive mantras, qualities, and teachings.

"That being so, Venerable Blessed One, in the future there will appear sons or daughters of good family who have served former victors and have undertaken diligence. When they hear such a samādhi as this, spoken by the Tathāgata, they will think, 'Even if right here our bodies, skin, flesh, blood, bones, sinews, and marrow dry up and wither away completely, that is better. We will not become lazy like this and die. We will not give up diligence without having mastered, explained, and cultivated such vast and excellent teachings as these.' Thinking this, they will undertake diligence. Such holy persons, who constantly undertake diligence, upon hearing a sūtra such as this spoken by the Tathāgata, will gain exceedingly vast joy, delight, and faith the moment they hear it."

When he had said this, the Blessed One said to the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla: "Excellent, Bhadrapāla, excellent! Moreover, Bhadrapāla, the teaching in accordance with the Dharma that you have explained is unerring; it is just so. Excellent! I too rejoice in it, and in whatever I rejoice, the buddhas, the blessed ones of the past, future, and present, also rejoice."

Then, at that time, the Blessed One spoke these verses:

"Whatever training of the prātimokṣa I have taught,
Train in all of it and resort to the wilderness;
If you always practice the qualities of purification,
This samādhi will not be hard to find.

Give up all seeking to be invited as a guest,
And completely give up attachment to tastes;
Regard the one from whom you hear it as the Teacher,
And cultivate the perception of equality and sameness.

Give up jealousy, desire, and pride,
Give up attachment, aversion, and delusion,
Properly generate a mind devoted to meditative concentration,
And enter into this samādhi.

Free of jealousy, having given up attachment,
Fully restrained, having given up anger,
Having made the Victor's body your object of focus,
Enter into this samādhi.

Not giving up diligence, not overcome by sleep,
Not keeping company with relatives and friends,
Devoted to walking meditation, shunning crowds and companions,
Cultivate that stainless samādhi!"

This is the ninth chapter of the samādhi requested by Bhadrapāla.

The fourth section.

When he had said this, the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, said to the Blessed One: "Venerable Blessed One, if some nun who has properly entered the Great Vehicle hears this samādhi of the direct presence of the buddhas of the present and wishes to take it up or to cultivate it, Venerable Blessed One, abiding in what qualities should she take up this samādhi and cultivate it?"

When he had asked this, the Blessed One said to the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla: "Bhadrapāla, a nun who has properly entered the Great Vehicle, has heard this samādhi, and wishes to take it up or to cultivate it should always be respectful. She should always be free of jealousy and free of anger. She should destroy pride, be without arrogance, and undertake diligence. She should be without laziness and give up torpor and sleep.

She should strive in the practice of not lying down to sleep. She should give up all seeking after gain, honor, acclaim, robes, food, bedding, medicine for the sick, and requisites. Her livelihood should be completely pure, and she should not cling even to body and life. She should always long for the Dharma and strive to seek much learning. Her mind should be free of attachment, free of aversion, and free of dullness. She should abandon the side of Māra. She should give up massaging, adorning, and applying cosmetics to the body. She should not be attached to her bowl and robes. She should not be attached to fame, should be free of slander, and should give up all fickleness.

Therefore, Bhadrapāla, a nun who has truly entered the Great Vehicle and who, having heard this samādhi, wishes to receive it or to cultivate it, should regard the master as the Teacher and receive this samādhi. Abiding in these qualities, she should cultivate this samādhi.

Then the Blessed One spoke these verses:

A nun who longs for this samādhi,
If she is always respectful and gives up jealousy, anger,
Pride, and likewise conceit,
Will not find this samādhi hard to obtain.

If you seek this samādhi,
Take up diligence and give up sleep,
Give up every kind of seeking,
And, not clinging even to life, long for the Dharma!

Whoever wishes to hold this samādhi
Should not dwell with a mind tormented by afflictions,
Possessed of attachment and aversion;
Never let yourself be caught by the noose of Māra!

Whoever wishes to hold this samādhi
Should not behave with deceit,
And should give up massaging, ornaments, slander,
And all fickleness.

For the sake of a bowl or for the sake of praise
She gives not even a moment's thought.
Regard the one from whom you hear it as the Teacher,
And hold the perception that they are equal and alike.

This was the tenth chapter of The Samādhi Requested by Bhadrapāla.

When the Blessed One had said this, the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla, said to the Blessed One, "Venerable Blessed One, a bodhisattva who is a householder, wearing white clothes and living at home, who, having heard this samādhi, wishes to receive it or to cultivate it for even one day or two days: Venerable Blessed One, abiding in what qualities should he receive this samādhi and cultivate it?"

The Blessed One said, "Therefore, Bhadrapāla, if a bodhisattva who is a householder, wearing white clothes and living at home, having heard this samādhi, wishes to receive it or to cultivate it for even one day, or two days, or even just the time it takes to milk a cow, that householder bodhisattva, wearing white clothes and living at home, should be endowed with faith. He should be free of stinginess and give freely. He should delight in distributing gifts, and give away all his possessions without hoping for any karmic ripening. He should go for refuge to the Buddha. He should go for refuge to the Dharma. He should go for refuge to the Saṅgha. He should keep his moral discipline completely pure. He should take up the five bases of training. He should have no other god, and his intention should follow the Buddha. Being faultless in moral discipline, properly adopting and practicing the ten paths of virtuous action, and leading others to adopt them as well, he should turn away from drinking alcohol. He should not be haughty. Speaking to others of the faults of alcohol, not giving alcohol to others, and disparaging sensual desire to others as well, he should practice celibacy. He should be free of jealousy. His livelihood should be completely pure. He should not crave sons. He does not give rise to craving for daughters. Do not give rise to craving for your wife! He should not crave wealth. He should not crave a home. He should delight in going forth, and his mind should be inclined toward going forth. Keeping the fast of eight branches, he should be inclined toward this samādhi and should stay at a monastery. He should have a sense of shame and of modesty, his mind should be inclined toward awakening, and he should not wish for any other vehicle. He should not disparage monks who possess moral discipline. He should be respectful and courteous toward those who lead the pure life. Toward the master from whom he received this samādhi he should be deeply devoted, holding him extremely dear, with faith and respect. He should regard him as a spiritual friend. He should also regard him as the Teacher. He should offer him every requisite for his comfort. He should live with gratitude, repaying kindness, and with respect. Bhadrapāla, in this way a householder bodhisattva living at home should apply himself to this samādhi. Abiding in these qualities, he should cultivate this samādhi."

Then the Blessed One spoke these verses:

A bodhisattva living at home
Who wishes to hold this samādhi
Should always keep his moral discipline completely pure,
Firmly give rise to the mind of renunciation,
Take up the five bases of training,
Observe the eight-branched poṣadha,
And, without having left his home, stay at a monastery;
Thus should he enter into this samādhi.

He should not pour alcohol for anyone,
And should give up drinks the Buddha has not praised;
Abiding in these trainings,
He should receive this samādhi.

Not craving sons, not craving daughters,
Not craving a wife, nor craving a home,
A layman possessed of a sense of shame,
He should enter into this samādhi.

Keep yourself free of sickness, and with no one
Keep company or talk; give up all idle chatter.
Abiding in faultless patience,
Enter into this samādhi.

When entering this noble samādhi,
Always be respectful toward the Buddha and the Dharma,
With a mind clear, unwavering, and free of jealousy,
Be eager to serve and honor the Saṅgha.

This was the eleventh chapter of The Samādhi Requested by Bhadrapāla.

When he had spoken these words, the bodhisattva mahāsattva Bhadrapāla said to the Blessed One (the Buddha), "Venerable Blessed One, if a laywoman who has correctly entered the Great Vehicle hears this samādhi and wishes to receive its transmission or to cultivate it, Venerable Blessed One, in what qualities should she abide in order to receive the transmission of this samādhi and to cultivate it?"

When he had asked this, the Blessed One said to the bodhisattva mahāsattva Bhadrapāla, "Therefore, Bhadrapāla, a laywoman who has correctly entered the Great Vehicle, and who hears this samādhi and wishes to receive its transmission or to cultivate it, should take up the five precepts.

"Bhadrapāla, the laywoman should go for refuge to the Three and should have no other gods.

"Bhadrapāla, the laywoman should completely give up auspicious signs and omens, and should be without deceit.

"Bhadrapāla, the laywoman should keep her conduct free of pretense and should be without stinginess.

"Bhadrapāla, the laywoman should delight in giving out gifts and should long for the Dharma.

"Bhadrapāla, the laywoman should be inquiring by nature, respectful, and courteous. When she sees monks and nuns, she should offer them a seat.

"Therefore, Bhadrapāla, if a laywoman who has correctly entered the Great Vehicle hears this samādhi and wishes to receive its transmission or to cultivate it, that laywoman should abide in such qualities as these, and so receive the transmission of this samādhi and cultivate it."

Then, at that time, the Blessed One spoke these verses:

"The laywoman who seeks this samādhi,
Approved by the buddhas and praised by the Sugatas,
Should take up the five precepts
And so enter into this samādhi.

Having no other gods than the Buddha,
The Dharma, and likewise the Saṅgha, being respectful,
And giving up every wrong path,
She should enter into this samādhi.

Not killing living beings, not taking what is not given,
Not speaking any falsehood,
Never engaging in sexual misconduct,
She should receive the transmission of this samādhi.

Without stinginess, not hoping for a ripened reward,
Having no pretense in her conduct,
Giving up envy, pride, and anger,
She should receive the transmission of this samādhi.

When she sees monks and likewise nuns,
She rises respectfully and offers them a seat;
Longing for the Dharma, she seeks to know what is virtuous;
She should receive the transmission of this samādhi."

This concludes the twelfth chapter, on the laywoman, from the Samādhi Requested by Bhadrapāla.

When he had spoken these words, the bodhisattva mahāsattva Bhadrapāla said to the Blessed One, "Venerable Blessed One, it is wonderful that the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha, has taught these vast and excellent qualities, abiding in which one may receive the transmission of this samādhi or cultivate it, for the sake of bodhisattvas who have gone forth from the household, bodhisattvas of great aspiration, those of excellent superior intention, those who have taken up diligence, those who do not abandon effort, and those who have correctly set out for unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening.

"Venerable Blessed One, in the future, when the Tathāgata has passed into nirvāṇa, will this samādhi be practiced here in Jambudvīpa? Will it be spread?"

The Blessed One said, "Bhadrapāla, after I have passed completely into nirvāṇa, this samādhi will be practiced in Jambudvīpa for four thousand years. It will be spread. After that it will go into a hollow in the earth.

"Then again, in the last five hundred years, in the later time, in the final five-hundred-year period of that later age, when the holy Dharma is being destroyed, when renunciants are being destroyed, when the holy Dharma is being rejected, when beings are being destroyed, when kingdoms are in turmoil, when the time has come for the side of those with moral discipline to decline, when the time has come for the side of those of broken discipline to increase, when the time has come for the side of the holy Dharma to decline, when the time has come for the dharmas of the unholy side to increase, when the time of exhaustion has come, then a few beings will appear whose roots of virtue blaze so that they will obtain a sūtra such as this, who have done service to the victors of the past, who have generated roots of virtue, whose roots of virtue have ripened, and who have planted the seeds. For the sake of such beings as these, through the power of the Buddha, this samādhi will be practiced in Jambudvīpa. It will be spread.

"And when they hear this samādhi, they will obtain vast joy, delight, and faith. Having heard it, they will take it up, they will hold it, they will read it, they will master it, they will write it down, they will teach it, they will spread it, and they will strive in the practice of meditation. At the very least, they will write it in a volume and keep it."

Then the bodhisattva mahāsattva Bhadrapāla, the bodhisattva mahāsattva Ratnākara, and the Licchavi youth, having heard from the Blessed One of the changing and destruction of this holy Dharma in the later time, wept from the force of the Dharma and shed tears. Then the bodhisattva mahāsattva Bhadrapāla, the bodhisattva mahāsattva Ratnākara, and the Licchavi youth wiped away their tears, rose from their seats, draped their robes over one shoulder, and placed their right knees on the ground. Bowing with palms joined toward the Blessed One, they said to the Blessed One, "Venerable Blessed One, even in the final century after the Tathāgata has passed into nirvāṇa, in the later time, in the final five-hundred-year period of that later age, when the holy Dharma is being rejected, we will take up sūtras such as these. We will master them. We will write them down. We will hold them. We will read them. We will spread them. We will strive in the practice of meditation.

Why? Because, Venerable Blessed One (the Buddha), we can never have enough of listening to this profound sūtra spoken by the Tathāgata, of questioning it, taking it up, mastering it, keeping it, reading it, explaining it, spreading it widely, and meditating on it.

Then the bodhisattva great being, the householder Mahāsusārthavāha, the bodhisattva great being Guhagupta the merchant's son, the bodhisattva great being the youth Naladatta, the bodhisattva great being Susīma, the bodhisattva great being Indradatta, and the bodhisattva great being Varuṇa heard from the Blessed One that in the final five hundred years, in the later time, at that later age of the final five hundred years, this true Dharma would decline and be destroyed. Moved by the force of the Dharma, they wept and shed tears.

Then the bodhisattva great being, the householder Mahāsusārthavāha, Guhagupta the merchant's son, the youth Naladatta, the son of good family Susīma, Indradatta, and the bodhisattva great being Varuṇa wiped away their tears and rose from their seats. They draped their robes over one shoulder, set their right knees on the ground, bowed with palms joined toward the Blessed One, and said this to the Blessed One:

"Venerable Blessed One, in the future, after the Tathāgata has passed into nirvāṇa, in the final five hundred years, in the later time, at that later age of the final five hundred years, even when the true Dharma is being rejected, we too shall take up such sūtras as these. We shall master them. We shall write them down. We shall keep them. We shall read them. We shall also explain them extensively to others. We shall meditate on them. Venerable Blessed One, we shall make this unsurpassed, perfect awakening, which the tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly awakened buddhas fully accomplished over hundreds of thousands of millions of billions of eons, endure for a long time. We shall make it vast and widespread. Why? Because, Venerable Blessed One, we shall truly take up teachings not heard before, and we shall truly proclaim them. Fascicle five.

Again the Blessed One said to the bodhisattva, the great being Bhadrapāla: "Bhadrapāla, I directly recall that in the past, countless, vast, immeasurable, inconceivable and incalculable eons ago, at that time, on that occasion, there appeared in the world a tathāgata, an arhat, a perfectly complete buddha called King of Bliss, perfect in knowledge and conduct, the Sugata, knower of the world, unsurpassed guide of beings to be tamed, teacher of gods and humans, a buddha, a blessed one.

"Bhadrapāla, at that time there was a merchant's son called Bhadradatta. Surrounded by twenty thousand living beings and leading them, he went together with them to where the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha King of Bliss was. When he arrived, he bowed his head at the feet of the Blessed One, the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha King of Bliss, circled him three times, and sat down to one side. Having sat down to one side, the merchant's son Bhadradatta asked the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha King of Bliss about this samādhi in full.

"Bhadrapāla, then the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha King of Bliss, knowing the superior intention of the merchant's son Bhadradatta, taught this samādhi extensively and explained it correctly.

"Bhadrapāla, then the merchant's son Bhadradatta, having heard this samādhi, retained it. Having retained it, he meditated on it. Having meditated on it, he shaved off his hair and beard under the Blessed One, the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha King of Bliss, put on saffron robes, went forth from home into homelessness, and practiced the pure life. For eighty thousand years he remained meditating on this samādhi. He retained everything he had heard from the Blessed One, the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha King of Bliss, and he also retained everything he had heard from the tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly complete buddhas of the past, so that he came to possess inconceivable learning.

"Then at another time, when his life came to an end and his body broke up and he died, he was reborn in a happy destiny, in the heavenly world, sharing the same fortune as the gods of the Heaven of the Thirty-Three. Having been reborn there, in that very world-system he pleased a second tathāgata, called Lightning God. In that same eon he was born into a royal family like a great sāla tree, and having gone out from that royal family like a great sāla tree, he went forth under the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha Lightning God, and for eighty thousand years he meditated on this samādhi and lived the pure life.

"He then pleased a third tathāgata, arhat, perfectly complete buddha, called King of Light Rays. In that same eon he was born into a brahmin family like a great sāla tree, and having gone out from that brahmin family like a great sāla tree, he went forth under the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha King of Light Rays, and for eighty-four thousand years he meditated on this samādhi and lived the pure life. Later, eight eons after that, the merchant's son Bhadradatta fully awakened to unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening.

"Bhadrapāla, if you should have doubts, misgivings or uncertainty, thinking that the one who was the merchant's son called Bhadradatta at that time, on that occasion, was someone else, you should not regard it in that way, Bhadrapāla. Why is that? It was the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly complete Buddha Dīpaṃkara who at that time, on that occasion, was the merchant's son called Bhadradatta.

"Bhadrapāla, see how the merchant's son Bhadradatta sought the Dharma, desired the Dharma and had aspiration for the Dharma, and for that reason swiftly and fully awakened to unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening. Bhadrapāla, see how this samādhi of the bodhisattva, in which the buddhas of the present stand face to face, gives rise to the wisdom of a buddha in bodhisattvas, great beings, how their learning becomes like an ocean, and how greatly beneficial it proves to be.

"Therefore, Bhadrapāla, you should take up this samādhi. You should master it. You should write it down in letters. You should keep it. You should read it. Having taken it up and mastered it, you should explain it correctly and extensively to others as well. You should teach it. You should recite it. You should also strive in the practice of meditating on it. Why is that? Because, Bhadrapāla, when this samādhi is relied upon again and again, meditated upon and cultivated much, it leads to the attainment of the wisdom of a buddha, and one will attain the wisdom of a tathāgata, self-arisen wisdom, the wisdom of omniscience, inconceivable wisdom, wisdom equal to the unequaled, unsurpassed wisdom and wisdom beyond which there is nothing higher; apart from attaining these, one will not attain any other dharma.

"Again, Bhadrapāla, whatever one speaking truly would call 'that which has become the eye,' 'that which gives birth to bodhisattvas,' or 'that which gives rise to the qualities of a buddha,' Bhadrapāla, it is of this samādhi that one speaking truly would say those things.

"Again, Bhadrapāla, whatever one speaking truly would call 'the family of the Buddha,' 'the family of the Dharma,' 'the family of the Saṅgha,' 'the ground of the Buddha,' 'the family of learning,' 'learning like an ocean,' 'the accumulation of learning,' 'the basis of learning,' 'the accumulation of good qualities,' 'the generating of patience,'

"It generates loving-kindness; it attains compassion; it is the Dharma that generates awakening." Bhadrapāla, those who speak in this way speak as one who speaks correctly about this samādhi.

Again, Bhadrapāla, those who would say, as one who speaks correctly, "It is the Dharma that dispels the darkness of the world with its gods, humans and asuras; it is the Dharma that makes a great illumination," speak as one who speaks correctly about this samādhi.

Again, Bhadrapāla, those who would say, as one who speaks correctly, "It is the Dharma that generates the ten powers of the Tathāgata, the four fearlessnesses of the Tathāgata, the four correct discernments, and the eighteen unshared qualities of a buddha," speak as one who speaks correctly about this samādhi.

Bhadrapāla, see how vast is this samādhi of bodhisattvas, the direct presence of the buddhas of the present. Through it bodhisattva great beings, while remaining right here, see the buddhas of other world systems, hear the Dharma, and see the Saṅgha. Therefore, Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva great being who wishes to accomplish this precious samādhi should make firm his discipline in the four applications of mindfulness.

Bhadrapāla, how should a bodhisattva great being make firm his discipline in the four applications of mindfulness? Bhadrapāla, here a bodhisattva great being should contemplate the body as body, but should not examine the conceptual thoughts connected with the body. He should contemplate feelings as feelings, but should not examine the conceptual thoughts connected with feelings. He should contemplate the mind as mind, but should not examine the conceptual thoughts connected with the mind. He should contemplate phenomena as phenomena, but should not examine the conceptual thoughts connected with phenomena.

Bhadrapāla, apart from the tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly awakened buddhas, the irreversible bodhisattva great beings, and the śrāvakas who have realized with the body, who would have faith in this samādhi? And why is that, Bhadrapāla? Because all childish ordinary beings are confused about this samādhi of bodhisattvas, the direct presence of the buddhas of the present.

And why is that, Bhadrapāla? One should attend to those very phenomena and look at those very phenomena. One should also attend to the buddhas, the blessed ones, look at the tathāgatas, and listen to the Dharma, yet one should not cling to them. And why is that, Bhadrapāla? All these phenomena are empty by their intrinsic nature and pure by their intrinsic nature; from the very beginning they are utterly at peace.

Bhadrapāla, because all these phenomena depend on an object of reference, they cannot be referred to as objects. All these phenomena are void of that very attention. Because all these phenomena cannot be apprehended by their intrinsic nature, they cannot be grasped. Because all these phenomena are like space, they are unattached. Because all these phenomena are free of the notion of a self and of a sentient being, they are utterly pure. Because all these phenomena arise under the power of causes, they are free of afflictions. Because in all these phenomena no life force and no person can be found, they cannot be counted. Because all these phenomena are luminous by their intrinsic nature, they are equal to nirvāṇa. Because in all these phenomena no entity can be found, they are near at hand.

Bhadrapāla, if any son or daughter of noble family wishes to cultivate this samādhi, that person should by all means see the buddhas, the blessed ones, and cultivate the factor of awakening that is mindfulness, endowed with recollection of the Buddha. He should also hear the Dharma and cultivate the factor of awakening that is discernment of phenomena among phenomena. Not apprehending a self and not being conceited about phenomena, he should in this way, through engagement with the aspect of signs, enter into the engagement with signlessness.

And why is that, Bhadrapāla? Because one who perceives things as entities will not see the Buddha. One who perceives phenomena as phenomena will not see the Dharma. One who hopes for a karmic ripening will not perfect giving. One who delights in meditation will not have pure moral discipline. One who is stingy with the Dharma will not become learned. One who relies on persons will not attain complete nirvāṇa. One who delights in idle talk will not see solitude. One who delights in a dwelling place will not attain the fruit. One with attachment will not see faults. One who delights in malice will not be certain of patience. One who is hostile and resentful will not see benefits.

A son or daughter of noble family belonging to the śrāvaka vehicle will not directly realize this samādhi of bodhisattvas, the direct presence of the buddhas of the present, through which patience is attained. In a bodhisattva stinginess does not arise. One who has an object of reference will not cultivate emptiness. A lazy person will not attain clear realization. One who relies on desires will not achieve calm abiding. One who relies on supports will not accomplish meditation.

Therefore, Bhadrapāla, so that this samādhi may by no means disappear, I entrust it to the world with its gods.

The sixth fascicle.

When he had said this, the bodhisattva mahāsattva Bhadrapāla said to the Blessed One, "Venerable Blessed One, how should a bodhisattva cultivate this samādhi?"

The Blessed One said, "Bhadrapāla, if any son of good family or daughter of good family wishes to cultivate this samādhi, they should not dwell on form. They should not dwell on feeling, on mind, or on phenomena.

They should not abide in birth. They should not abide in any phenomena at all. They should not abide in emptiness, in signlessness, or in wishlessness. They should not abide in loving-kindness.

Bhadrapāla, what then is samādhi? It is attending earnestly to those phenomena, but not attending to them wrongly.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, consider the bodhisattva who dwells contemplating the body as body and does not conceptualize the conceptual thought bound up with the body, and who dwells contemplating feeling, mind, and phenomena as phenomena and does not conceptualize the conceptual thought bound up with phenomena. That bodhisattva will attain this samādhi. And why is that? Bhadrapāla, when a bodhisattva great being dwells contemplating the body as body and does not conceptualize the conceptual thought bound up with the body, and dwells contemplating feeling, mind, and phenomena as phenomena and does not conceptualize the conceptual thought bound up with phenomena, then no phenomena at all are apprehended. Because no phenomena are apprehended, he does not think and does not conceptualize. Bhadrapāla, one who does not think and does not conceptualize sees no phenomenon whatsoever. Bhadrapāla, that in which no phenomenon whatsoever is seen is to be known as unobscured wisdom. Bhadrapāla, that unobscured wisdom, Bhadrapāla, is what is called samādhi.

Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva who possesses this samādhi sees immeasurable, countless buddhas. He also hears the sublime Dharma, and having heard it, he fully comprehends it. He sees the unobscured wisdom of liberation of those tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly complete buddhas, and he also attains unimpeded wisdom.

Furthermore, Bhadrapāla, the bodhisattva dwells contemplating the body as body, and having so dwelt, he sees no phenomenon whatsoever. Having not seen, he does not think and does not conceptualize, and yet he is neither blind nor deaf. In the same way he dwells contemplating feeling, mind, and phenomena as phenomena, and having so dwelt, he sees no phenomenon whatsoever. Having not seen, he becomes free of reliance. Not relying on anything, he cultivates the path. Because he has cultivated the path, he has no doubt about phenomena. Being without doubt, he comes to see the buddhas. And having seen the buddhas, liberation comes about, because no phenomena arise.

And why is that? Bhadrapāla, if a bodhisattva engages in the perception of phenomena, that very thing becomes a view of it as his object. That very thing becomes a view of existence, a view of a self, a view of a sentient being, a view of a life principle, and a view of a person. That very thing becomes a view of aggregates, a view of elements, a view of sense bases, a view of signs, a view of entities, a view of causes, a view of conditions, and a grasping at objects.

And why is that? Bhadrapāla, the bodhisattva sees all phenomena as empty of intrinsic nature, and all phenomena as signless, ungraspable, and without grasping. Such bodhisattvas do not apprehend any phenomena. They do not form conceits about them. They do not see them. If one asks how they do not apprehend, do not form conceits, and do not see, Bhadrapāla, it is like this. For example, the non-Buddhists (tīrthikas), or the śrāvakas of the non-Buddhists, abide in the perception of a self; they abide in the perception of a sentient being, a life principle, and a person; they abide in the perception of all phenomena; they apprehend phenomena, form conceits about them, and see them. Bhadrapāla, the bodhisattva does not see in that way.

And if one asks, Bhadrapāla, how then the bodhisattva does see, Bhadrapāla, it is like this. For example, just as the tathāgatas, the irreversible bodhisattvas, the pratyekabuddhas, and the śrāvaka arhats see all phenomena, so, Bhadrapāla, should the bodhisattva great being look upon all phenomena. Having seen them, he should not delight in them. Being without delight, he does not become one who indulges in delight; free of delight, the bodhisattva who has completely destroyed delight cultivates this samādhi.

Bhadrapāla, it is like this. For example, just as space is formless, indemonstrable, without abiding, utterly pure, and free of afflictions, so the bodhisattva looks upon all phenomena, and his eye becomes unobstructed with regard to conditioned and unconditioned phenomena. For the bodhisattva whose eye is unobstructed, phenomena become directly present. When he brings them to mind, he sees the tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly complete buddhas like an adorned pillar of gold; like the king of light rays rising on high; like the full moon encircled by stars; like a wheel-turning king surrounded by his mighty host; like Brahmā seated on Brahmā's throne in the Brahmā world; like Śakra (Indra), king of the gods, surrounded by the gods of the Heaven of the Thirty-Three; like a fire blazing on a mountain peak; like a king of physicians dispensing medicine; like the maned lion, king of beasts, who fearlessly terrifies all the jackals; like the supreme goose soaring in the sky; like Sumeru, king of mountains, rising high out of the ocean; like the Himalaya, king of mountains, flourishing with every flower and medicinal herb; like the Cakravāla mountains held up and kept standing by what is born of the son of the wind; like a mass of wind supporting the masses of earth and water; like a mass of water supporting the mass of earth.

Then, at that time, the Blessed One spoke these verses:

The beautiful peak of Sumeru,
Stainless, free of stain, and pure,
Equal to the sky and like it,
Is richly adorned by the gods.

Bhadrapāla, in the same way the bodhisattva great being takes as his object those tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly complete buddhas, radiant and resplendent with majesty and glory throughout all the world systems of the great trichiliocosm; he sees them, and he also hears them teaching the Dharma.

Having heard it, they also retain it, master it, and keep it. When that bodhisattva has arisen from this samādhi, he will teach it and explain it extensively to others, just as he heard the Dharma, just as he retained it, and just as he mastered it.

Bhadrapāla, in this way this samādhi greatly benefits bodhisattvas: it produces many good qualities of both worldly and transcendent dharmas. Therefore, Bhadrapāla, a son or daughter of good family who desires awakening should take up this samādhi, master it, keep it, read it, and write it out. They should explain it extensively to others, teach it, and proclaim it. They should also strive in the practice of meditating on it.

Then the Blessed One (the Buddha) spoke these verses:

Free of taint, free of dust, the stainless Buddha,

That bodhisattva should have a sense of conscience toward that master, and should be grateful and acknowledge what has been done for him.

And what, Bhadrapāla, is the seal of the tathāgatas? It is this: all phenomena are without agent, without action, uncompounded, without attachment, beyond apprehension, emptiness, signless, wishless, without characteristics, nondual, without entry, without support, beyond counting, beyond designation, without taking up, without casting away, without abiding. All phenomena are without permanence and without annihilation; they are the exhaustion of causes, the exhaustion of suffering, the exhaustion of things; unborn, unceasing, unabiding, indestructible; without path and without fruit of the path; undisturbed by all the noble ones, abandoned by all the childish, cast aside by the ignorant, and embraced by the wise. Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva endowed with four qualities will strive even just to hear this samādhi, so what need is there to speak of receiving its transmission and reciting it? What are the four? Having previously purified the superior intention; having previously made aspirations; being devoted to the wisdom of omniscience; and taking up every approach of the Dharma with the thought, "How might I hold and teach this treasury of the precious Dharma, which the tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly awakened buddhas accomplished over many hundreds of thousands of millions of billions of eons?" They are these: bringing the Buddha to mind; being skilled in recollecting the Dharma again and again; bringing to mind the cutting off of all apprehension of phenomena; and being skilled in dismantling all signs. Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva endowed with these four qualities will strive, in seeking this samādhi, even merely to write it down.

Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva endowed with four qualities should be known as diligent in seeking this samādhi. What are the four? They are these: through the knowledge that determines all phenomena to be things that cannot be grasped, a bodhisattva should be known as diligent in this samādhi. Through being skilled in cultivating the Buddha's body as the object of meditation, a bodhisattva should be known as diligent in this samādhi. Through bringing to mind the Buddha as the object within every object, a bodhisattva should be known as diligent in this samādhi. Through being skilled in the conviction that all phenomena are devoid of entities, a bodhisattva should be known as diligent in this samādhi. Bhadrapāla, a bodhisattva endowed with these four qualities should be known as earnestly diligent in seeking this samādhi.

Bhadrapāla, in the later time, in the final five hundred years, when the true Dharma is in turmoil, suppose someone strives to seek this samādhi in order to benefit all sentient beings and out of compassion for all sentient beings, and gives rise to the thought, "How can this samādhi become widespread, vast, and long-enduring?" That son of good family generates great merit. Apart from knowing that this heap of merit inclines toward omniscience, no one can reach the end of that heap of merit.

Through the power of their aspirations, their heap of merit will swiftly be directed toward omniscience. They will be endowed with a great accumulation of roots of virtue and will emerge from abiding in selflessness. Leaving aside those who have donned the great armor for immeasurable, countless, measureless, inconceivable eons, or for even longer, all of them will have everything fulfilled just as they intended. All of them will go forth to deliverance in accordance with the particular character of their conduct and their accumulations.

Bhadrapāla, a son or daughter of good family who has entered the bodhisattva vehicle, who wishes to attain the perfection of all dharmas and the perfection of the wisdom of omniscience, and who wishes to attain pure roots of virtue, should apply themselves in the later time, in the final period when great fear arises, at the time when the true Dharma is being destroyed, to writing down this samādhi, receiving its transmission, reciting it, and proclaiming it widely to others as well.

Why is that? Bhadrapāla, the wisdom of omniscience, knowing all the aspects of all dharmas, is contained within this samādhi. By accomplishing skill in the recollection of the Buddha, one realizes all dharmas as having the nature of non-entity, and this is the recollection of the Buddha.
