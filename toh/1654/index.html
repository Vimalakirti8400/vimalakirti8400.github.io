About this text

The Abhayapaddhati, in English "Abhaya's Commentary on the Text," known in full as the Extensive Commentary on the Glorious Buddhakapāla, Great King of Tantras (Sanskrit Śrībuddhakapālamahātantrarājaṭīkā Abhayapaddhati; Tibetan Pal Sangye Töpé Gyü kyi Gyalpo Chenpö Gyacher Drelpa Jikpa Mepé Zhungdrel), is a detailed Indian commentary on the Buddhakapāla Tantra, a yoginī tantra centered on the deity Buddhakapāla and his consort Citrasenā. It was composed by the great scholar Abhayākaragupta of Vikramaśīla monastery during the reign of King Rāmapāla, and it explains the tantra chapter by chapter, covering the maṇḍala and its deities, the meditation on the deity's body, rites and offerings, the empowerments, and the completion-stage practices of the six-limbed yoga and innate great bliss, which it grounds in a philosophical analysis of emptiness free from elaboration. It is Toh 1654 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the section of tantra commentaries, and its Tibetan translation was completed by the translator Pang Lotsāwa Lodrö Tenpa, who filled in portions missing from an earlier translation.

Translation

In the language of India: śrībuddhakapālamahātantrarājaṭīkā abhayapadvati nāma. In the language of Tibet: The Extensive Commentary on the Glorious Buddhakapāla, Great King of Tantras, called Abhaya's Commentary on the Text.

Homage to glorious Vajrasattva.

He who sports with the mother of beings, radiating without measure as the supremely disciplined Great One for as long as the eons endure, whose body of supreme joy, utterly vast, is joined with Citrasenā: since the beings of existence, through his entering their hearts, come to sport in the taste of the innate bliss of perfect and excellent awakening, which is to be cultivated through sublime conduct, I, Abhaya, after his manner set forth this teaching without secrecy.

What the vajra goddesses granted me,
even as I heard it in a dream,
through their blessing I, Abhaya,
compose this commentary on the Buddhakapāla.
Surely I have been blessed
by the Vajra Holder, the primordial protector,
for the words that he has spoken
are as though spoken into my ear for my own sake.

Excellent and noble persons who rely on joy are pure and joyful;
those who see only this near shore go to bad destinies and enter long existence.
From Abhaya's commentary on the text, whatever supreme ground of the Dharma dawns,
it brings them to acceptance, and from abiding in the middle it lets them touch matchless excellence.

The teacher of this Buddhakapāla Tantra, which consists of fourteen chapters, and its subject matter, is the Blessed One Buddhakapāla. Thus too are stated the relation between what is taught and the teacher, and the relation between the words that express and what they express. The purpose is that disciples understand the meaning of the Buddhakapāla. The purpose of the purpose is the accomplishment of the Great Seal (mahāmudrā), which is like a vajra, of one taste with great bliss, and of benefit to all beings without exception.

Here, the part beginning "Thus have I" and ending with "became" is first of all the introduction, because when it is present the teaching can begin. The gathering of time, place, teacher and retinue is the introduction to the teaching, because without it the teaching would not be fitting. Concerning this, the Blessed One said in the Sūtra of the Perfect Compilation of the Dharma, "With 'Thus have I' his Dharma is to be compiled, and likewise after understanding it in its sequence." Therefore the compiler compiled it exactly so.

Who is the compiler here? If one asks what, precisely, his name is: since he declares only what the Blessed One has permitted, here it is the word of the Blessed One alone. Moreover, because the tantra was entrusted to him, it is said to be Vajrapāṇi.

Next, this particle "thus" makes clear the whole unmistaken meaning of the tantra that is to be compiled. So the syllables of "thus" should be understood as possessing the meaning of the whole meaning of the tantra. And the meaning of the tantra, being by nature wisdom and skillful means, abides as the very essence of the members of the two syllables: so it teaches.

"I" means I, the compiler. "Heard" means: the words are understood through ear consciousness and the meaning through mental consciousness, but it is not that I have realized this by direct perception, because it is the Blessed One alone who understands everything in this Dharma. In this way it shows respect for the Dharma.

Having thus taught in general, he teaches that introduction specifically and in order: "at one time" and so on. "One" is a certain special time, inconceivable, brought about by many accumulations of merit and wisdom, which cannot be measured by lunar days, constellations and the like. Here, moreover, the time is explained as being of the nature of the fourth moment. "At that one time the Blessed One": by rearranging the words of the phrase "became the dwellings," it is construed as "was dwelling."

"He has bhaga," so he is the Blessed One (Bhagavān), and that bhaga is lordship and the other qualities. As it is said:

Lordship, form and fame,
glory, wisdom and diligence:
these six excellences
are what is called fortune.

Or else, because he has vanquished the four māras, he is the Vanquishing Possessor, the Blessed One. Or else, the wisdom that discerns all phenomena with certainty and definitively removes the māra of the afflictions and the rest is the vanquishing, and since he possesses it as his very nature, he is the Vanquishing Possessor. This is stated in the Vajraśekhara Tantra:

What wisdom destroys is the afflictions;
therefore wisdom is called the vanquisher.

He is a tathāgata, and they are also all, so "all the tathāgatas" are Akṣobhya and the rest; and theirs are body, speech and mind. "Vajra" is emptiness. Its great essence, the excellences of one's own and others' aims, is alaṃ, that is, it makes them utterly unsurpassed; that of which this is the action or the object is the great ornament, namely Vajragarvā, the embodiment of the Perfection of Wisdom. Because it takes and holds her center, her essence, the awakening mind, it is the maṇḍala, namely the source of phenomena. The body, speech and mind of all the tathāgatas are this, and it is also the maṇḍala of the vajra great ornament, because here the very nature of the body, speech and mind of all the tathāgatas is of one taste with it. For that very reason, too, it is triangular; "in its center."

Although the celestial palace is not mentioned here, it should be understood by implication as "in the celestial palace in the center of the source of phenomena," just as "a rich man lives in the city" comes to mean by implication "in a mansion in the city." It is not stated so in order to indicate principally the ground of great bliss, for the celestial palace too is of one taste with it. Or else, "vajra" is emptiness, the source of phenomena; the maṇḍala of the great ornament there is the celestial palace; "in its center" means in the center of that.

By showing that the Blessed One dwells in a place of that kind, it makes one understand clearly the innate body in which emptiness and compassion are indivisible, and it shows that the teaching of the tantra by that very body is likewise of one taste with it.

Having thus stated the excellence of time, place and teacher, he speaks of the excellence of the retinue: "all" and so on. That is, he also dwelt together with the tathāgatas Akṣobhya and the rest, the yoginīs Citrasenā and the rest, and the noble Ānanda and others.

The mention of "those free from attachment" serves to indicate the followers of the Śrāvaka Vehicle, so that it may be understood that even the followers of the Perfection Vehicle accomplish the perfect awakening whose nature is great bliss through the Vajra Vehicle alone. Lokeśvara and others also had assembled there.

I have already explained this statement of the introduction in detail, as far as possible, in my extensive commentary on the glorious Saṃpuṭa, so the intelligent should ascertain it from there.

In accord with the introduction, the compiler introduces what the Blessed One taught with "then" and so on. Because he holds the vajra, the five wisdoms whose own nature is emptiness, he is the Vajradhara whose nature is great bliss, innate, in which emptiness and compassion are indivisible. The Great Seal, which is of one taste with great bliss and brings benefit to beings, that very emptiness is the vajra, because it splits apart all obscurations. "And the abode" means the basis of the excellence of one's own and others' aims. Because that alone is established by the authority of the mantras and tantras, it is well taught by being pointed out as supreme.

Therefore, "by the lord of all the tathāgatas," that is, of Vairocana and the rest: "lord" means master, and by his very lordship it makes clear that this teacher is unsurpassed. "Vajra" is his own; "lotus" is Citrasenā's. "Through their union." "Queen" is Citrasenā, and her bhaga is the source of phenomena. "Within": the vajra is the awakening mind, and the lotuses are the crown of the head and the rest; these alone are the queen's, that is, the bhagas of the avadhūtī. "Entered complete nirvāṇa" means that the very essence of the awakening mind melted.

Ultimately, the queen is wisdom, and another name for Citrasenā is Samantabhadrā. Her bhaga is emptiness. The plural is used because Citrasenā is by nature the gathering of all queens.

There, the Blessed One, the Great Seal, abiding in the vajra, through wisdom does not abide in existence and through compassion does not abide in peace; by this nonabiding nirvāṇa he passed into complete nirvāṇa. Having become one with it, by the nature of one taste untouched by any difference, from then on, for as long as saṃsāra lasts, together with immeasurable tathāgatas, bodhisattvas, yoginīs, those free from attachment, mudrās, maṇḍalas, and the eighty koṭis of mantras, although he has become free of conceptual thought, by the force of his former impetus he remains in the manner of doing whatever needs to be done for all sentient beings. That is the meaning.

Even so, the thought "I have realized this supremely profound Dharma, but beings for the most part have weak merit and poor wisdom and are inclined to the Lesser Vehicle; so who would have faith in this?" is why he passed into nirvāṇa, in order to spur on sharp disciples with respect. It shows the meaning of "unconcerned and disinclined to teach the Dharma he has understood." "That" means not abiding in existence or in peace, and being disinclined. "How could one possessed of supreme love and wisdom, who remains in the manner of doing whatever needs to be done, be disinclined?" In this way a sense of wonder is gained.

"To Citrasenā": citra means of various forms and marvelous; senā is a host, namely great bliss; she to whom it belongs, to her. "Yoginī":

The union of wisdom and means
is what is designated yoga;

she possesses this to the highest degree. For that very reason she asks, since he is able to answer what is asked. If one asks, "Is there some means by which beings may gain the ability, the capacity to bring benefit to themselves and others to completion? What is the purpose of teaching the means of mantra in the tantras and elsewhere?", it is spoken as follows.

The condensed meaning here is this. In the tantras and other works spoken by the lords of sages, no power is seen without mantra, and from mantra many purposes arise. Thus, if the great Vajra Holder has said that beings of lesser, middling and great merit accomplish it by reciting ten million, a hundred thousand and ten thousand times respectively, then how would one with doubt, wondering "Am I counted among the lesser and the others or not?", recite mantra with a clinging mind? How would those of mistaken understanding, struck down by desire and the like, recite mantra while engaged in desire? Where the reading is "beings engaged in desire," one should construe it as "of them." Although the ignorant may count their tantras and mantra recitations, how could this be done with an afflicted mind? "Utterly," also for one of correct understanding: one by whom the mind that fixes on the extremes of entity and nonentity has been thoroughly examined. The form ending in the past participle is in some copies the particle "beyond."

For such a one, how is mantra a mantra, something to be recited as mantra? From the realization of mind comes the mind that is the essence of mind and the protection that is the essence of protecting, that is, the indivisibility of emptiness and compassion, which is the ultimate mantra. Since from that alone comes the accomplishment able to achieve one's own and others' aims, what use is reciting mantra? That is the intent.

"In all, how" is for the question. "Thus": following the order of what has been said, those endowed with discernment do not recite, and even if they do recite, the accomplishment comes from elsewhere; it is not an accomplishment from that. "Ten million eons like the stars": the first case is used in the sense of the third. Just as the stars in the sky are countless, so it is by ten millions of countless eons. "Thus": just as recitation is not possible for that purpose, so, even when there is occasional recitation by those who doubt the meaning and so on, there is no accomplishment either.

"Blessed One" means "of the Blessed One." "The great goddess" is the supreme queen. For that very reason, like the Blessed One, she is the mistress of all the tantras, since she is fit to teach all the tantras. Thus it is said:

In the form of Vajravārāhī she proclaimed the Hundred-Thousand Abhidhāna
and taught the Vajraḍāka tantras.

The purpose is to show that she is none other than the Blessed One.

She is the sovereign lady because she is unobstructed everywhere and possesses inconceivable power.

"Merely by looking, the Blessed One": "Blessed One" here means "of the Blessed One." "Into the skull" means "from the skull." Kam is bliss and pāla is "protects," so the kapāla, the skull, is the indestructible seed of awakening. From it the mantra was drawn out. The intent here is this: since it is said, "One should meditate on the seed of wisdom in the form of a drop between the eyebrows," the meaning is that, having made the mind stable by meditating on it in the form of a drop between the eyebrows, one should send forth the mantra.

Or else, "from the skull" connected with the Blessed One means from the source of phenomena of Citrasenā. As will be said, "In her skull the maṇḍala, mantras and tantra are well arranged." This is the oral instruction here: coming from the indestructible place of the Blessed One's wheel of great bliss, emerging by the lower path, it enters the source of phenomena of the Blessed Lady; the emergence of the mantra from the center of the door of the avadhūtī at the point between the brows is the arising in forward order, and it enters in reverse order. This very meaning is made clear by the passage beginning "thus" and ending "entered the skull."

"Conquered" means "having conquered." "Citrasenā" here means of the avadhūtī channel of the Blessed Lady Citrasenā: it entered the center of its door at the point between the brows, melted together with the awakening mind, and entered within the lower skull, the source of phenomena. Emerging from there, it entered above the vajra path of the Blessed One, reversing the order from the anāhata.

"Of this" means "this." "Of the nāgas" means "the nāgas." "All the nāgas" is connected with "of great magical power": Vāsuki, the Mighty Mover, and the rest, and any others whatever who are sources of poison, that is, those that arise from the poisons of sight, of fangs, of saliva, of touch, of rays, and of leftover food. "Commanded" means "O goddess, command us." "Speaking" means those who speak. "Spoke" means it was spoken by Citrasenā. "Thus" means immediately upon hearing "Buddha" and so on.

The Blessed One's skull has already been explained. "Having opened" means having become fully clear. Because the drop that dwells at the point between the brows is by its very nature the vowels and consonants, the book, whose nature is the summation of all the Dharma and which consists of written leaves, emerged just as a mantra does. Or else, pu, as in pullīramālaya, means "causing to expand," and kaṃ is bliss, so pustaka, the book, is the awakening mind. The meaning is that this itself becomes a book in the form of written leaves.

"From the sky" is explained by taking the word twice. From the sky, that is, in the source of phenomena, at the time of the union of vajra and lotus, when a mere sound arose, at that moment the book emerged. At that very moment it emerged as though the gods were sounding forth from the sky.

As for "tantra," tantra means continuum, and it is of three kinds: cause, result, and the working of the means. Of these, the nature of Heruka without beginning or end is the causal tantra, because with that very nature as cause the resultant Heruka arises. The resultant Heruka, free from adventitious stains and by nature accomplishing the aims of all beings without exception, is the resultant tantra. The means of purifying that very nature is the tantra of means; the rites of the garuḍa and so on are the means that are limbs of the means. The actual means is to accomplish, at the time of the accomplishment of the Great Seal, what is explained through the yoga of the mudrā; and the actual means for that, in turn, is the completion stage, and for that, the generation stage.

"Great king": great, because by accomplishing the excellence of one's own and others' aims it holds sway over beings. Its extended explanation is "benefiting all beings." Its fully extended explanation is "raining bliss upon all beings." "All accomplishments": because all accomplishments come from this. "Vehicle of the yoginīs" means the yoginī tantra: "going to it" and "going by it," that is, its nature is result, continuum and means. The extended explanation of "unsurpassed" is "swiftly bestowing the result."

There, by way of the actual means itself, he first teaches the completion stage in brief: "raised" and so on. "Raised" means held up in praise: the vajra. "Within" is the awakening mind. "Pressed" is the lotus, since it is the support. "Born from that" is great wisdom, since it is the root of all phenomena. It is unsurpassed because it is great bliss free from obscuration. "Place" means the state, the basis of the excellence of one's own and others' aims. Or else, "place" is the abandonment of movement in the channels. "Hard to find" is for those struck down by laziness and the like. "Secret" means not to be made clear to the śrāvakas and the like. For that very reason it is supreme and exalted, since it is the domain only of those whose wisdom is sharp and who have gathered merit. "Delightful" means endowed with joy and the rest.

"It abides throughout the whole body": because it is experienced everywhere without interruption, great bliss is said to be fully expanded. The body itself and it arise as one taste, like waves and water. In this way, as pervading all things, the awareness together with the deities of the maṇḍala is taught to be the lord of the maṇḍala.

He teaches the limbs of the means in brief: "the garuḍa rite and" and so on, that is, the twelve rites up to killing. He teaches the generation stage in brief: "various mantras" are the seeds for generation, the syllable hūṃ and the rest. "As appropriate" means by the rite explained in the chapters still to be explained. "The yoginīs" means those of Citrasenā and the rest. "As they arise" means generating them as appropriate. "Heruka" means of the great hero Heruka. The causes of generation are the moon and the rest. The causes of abiding are the source of phenomena and the rest. This generation stage should be meditated on first.

Here in the secret mantra of the Great Vehicle, only the meditation on the deity's body is taught, in order to bring beginner beings in at the outset. As it is said:

For beings who are beginners,
meditation on the body of the deity itself
is definitely taught first in the tantra,
for the accomplishment of all rites.

"The completion stage arises from the method that is the generation stage proper." In order that one may understand what kind of generation stage is the method, immediately after teaching the generation stage in brief he teaches the distinction between the two stages, in the passage beginning "entity." Because there are none of the superimpositions of entity and non-entity, all of which are invalidated by every refutation, it is "free of the elaboration of entity and non-entity." Because there is no trace of the two obscurations, it is "unstained."

If one objects, "Then it would not accomplish the welfare of others, just like the nirvāṇa of the śrāvakas," he says, "acting in existence": for although there are no obscurations, out of great compassion one appears in saṃsāra for the sake of others for endless time. This is stated in the first tantra of the Paramādya:

As long as the supreme heroes
remain in the abode of saṃsāra,
so long, for the unequaled welfare of beings,
are they able not to pass into nirvāṇa.

Again he teaches the limbs of method, in the passage beginning "intentional language." From "purity of the reflection" up to "the sword" are the twelve rites, since the rites are explained earlier and later. He makes clear that all rites will be accomplished through the union of Buddhakapāla, abiding in the generation stage and abiding in the completion stage. For that very reason, by mentioning killing and corpses as examples, both are included. "Invisible" means oneself. "Not supreme" means not excellent.

Through strict and fierce austerities
the body withers, and there is suffering;
from suffering the mind is distracted,
and from distraction, accomplishment goes elsewhere.

Therefore it is said, "while indulging in the five objects of desire." By the reasoning of the verse,

Among all illusions,
the illusion of woman is especially exalted;
this division of the three wisdoms
is clearly indicated in this alone,

when one enjoys the five qualities of desire, the chief one is relying on a woman, because, through skill in means, this is the very cause of the accomplishment of the great seal (mahāmudrā).

Then, in order to teach in brief the practice of virility for that purpose, he says the passage beginning "destroyer of fear." The fears, that is, conceptual thoughts, can be destroyed, because they set at the time the coemergent dawns. "The great battle" is various kinds of love-play. "While doing" means that one man conquers a hundred thousand women through the practice of virility. If one asks, "Is this seen to be so?", he says, "by one man": one man is seen to have great desire, as with the noble Dharmodgata and others, who had sixty-eight thousand women. Because this is illustrative, "overpowers a hundred thousand women" is to be supplied. Therefore he enjoys desire. "By one" means by him alone; "all" means toward all the women. Here too the method, "making virile," means melting, holding, and increasing the awakening mind; how these three come about will be explained.

With the passage beginning "then," he teaches the garuḍa practice in brief. "According to name" means it shall be explained just as it is well known. The action of the myna bird and the action of the vidyādhara will be taught in detail. "Dice" means the dice game; by playing it, poison is destroyed. Beating a drum. "Seized" means carried off by a woman. Striking with karavīra wood from the head down to the soles of the feet. "Noose" means binding the throat with a cord in the form of a noose and pulling. Making the call of the cuckoo. "Opening the lotus" means stopping the wind according to the instruction, with the intention of drawing the poison out from its root in the inner lotuses. Others say it means working the two tips of the wheel of enjoyment as a device. "Acting as a tiger" means walking in the manner of a tiger's gait. "Water" will be taught in detail. "Conch" means the sound of a conch. "Taking with the turban" means taking away the poison by means of a written name held in a turban. "Vomiting" means inducing vomiting. Covering with a cotton cloth. "Slap" means striking with a slap. "Three syllables" means striking with ḍhakakara. Some say it means destroying poison with the three syllables ha ho hrīḥ. "Speaking with the view of meditation" means speaking with the gaze of one meditating on the white syllable a and so on. By these eighteen practices, poison is drawn out.

He teaches in detail the completion stage that was taught in brief, in the passage beginning "then." "Excellent" is the play that is in the lotus, the Blissful. "Wisdom" is the joy of coemergent joy. "As it is" means that, because it is free of the nature of one and many, it is without intrinsic nature, and so it is thus the conventional truth. "Bhaga" is the lotus; he dwells upon the ground at its center, dwelling having cleared away conceptual thoughts, through not abiding in either existence or nirvāṇa. This is stated in the Hevajra:

In the excellent bhaga of the vajra's tip,
in the Blissful, I dwell,
the jewel casket of the buddhas,
in the essence of the form of the letter e.

Likewise:

In the queen's bhaga, in great bliss,
it abides under the name of semen.
Without it there would be no bliss;
having abandoned bliss, it would not come to be.

Likewise:

I am of the nature of coemergent joy,
at the end of the supreme, at the beginning of the special joy.

Here "under the name of semen" is said because the mind that has solely the form of the awakening mind is thus made clear. "At the end of the supreme, at the beginning of the special joy" means: the end of the supreme is the special joy, of which this is the beginning; that is, immediately after the supreme comes the special joy, and immediately after that comes the coemergent. In another system it is the beginning of the special joy, meaning in between the supreme and the special joy. This view too is untenable, because it is said:

It is to be marked at the beginning of the special joy,
and that too abandons the three joys.

By this, namely "it is the special joy, and also at the beginning," one should understand that the coemergent, which dawns only at the time of the special joy, is to be marked only at its beginning. For once the coemergent has dawned, how could it be marked, since its very nature is the setting of all conceptual thoughts? For that very reason the noble master said:

This division of the three wisdoms
is clearly indicated in this alone.

By this he states that the three joys are marked and the fourth is not marked. The Blessed One too says this with the words, "Other than the three, devoid of characteristics." How then could the special joy arise immediately after the coemergent? For then the coemergent could not be marked. Since it would be marked only by the recollection immediately following the special joy, the experienced reality would not have been taught. In the place where the coemergent dawns there is no misconception whatsoever.

If one asks, "Then why is it marked at the beginning, since it has not yet been experienced at all?", it is not so: it is like the juncture of dawn. If one says, "Even so, that is merely an indication of its nature," that is true; the means of experiencing it should be relied upon in this way. If one objects, "Then such a thing is not fit even to become a means, because, from then on, it is impossible for one who clings to indicating it," that is true, if it were to happen so. As to how to act while it is occurring there, the gurus will explain. Thus the sequence of marking has been made clear for both views.

If the special joy came immediately after the coemergent, then the other tantras, and the accomplishment of the mudrā that holds the bindu of sixteen halved and halved, would be untenable, and a means of experiencing the coemergent would also be impossible; but for the bare nature of the coemergent alone there is nothing untenable. Therefore those who know the instructions teach only the former system. This much, as a digression, is enough.

That excellent wisdom, the worldly coemergent bliss, is by way of example to be regarded as what accomplishes that which is to be accomplished. So it is said, "bestowing great bliss": by cultivating, according to the instructions, the coemergent that is its means, one should generate the unchanging great bliss, unobscured and unsurpassable, which arises from the means. The reality of these blisses, the method and that which arises from the method, is only emptiness, characterized by the absence of intrinsic nature. To dispel the error of those who remain convinced that bliss alone is reality, the Blessed One said:

Because bliss is of the great elements,
therefore bliss is not reality.

The coemergent, moreover, does not arise at all times and in every way. Thus the coemergent is the own nature of entities; one realizes its ultimate, which is emptiness alone, and its conventional. Although it is realized when there is bliss, nevertheless it is the coemergent at all times and in every way, because, as with the realization at the times of experiencing suffering and equanimity, in the fourth meditative concentration, and in the formless realm, it is not experienced.

As is said in the Litany of the Names: "Holding the peak of the fourth meditative concentration." There the fourth meditative concentration is the peak of coemergent joy, the utmost culmination, free of all the stains of conceptual thought; because he abides in it as its very self, the meaning is "holding its own nature." When the boundless three realms appear as the nature of great bliss, the meditative concentrations and the rest too are of that nature; but the nature of great bliss is the Blessed One alone and no one else. This is the meaning of the explanation by repetition.

If one asks, "Then how is bliss called coemergent?": great compassion with an object, being cultivated through devoted aspiration by one who has not seen reality, is not yet real, yet it is called the conventional coemergent because, when reality is realized, it has the nature of realization. In the same way, in the state of occasionally experiencing bliss and in the state of not experiencing it, the cultivation done through devoted aspiration by one who has not seen reality, endowed with the supreme of all aspects, which is of the nature of emptiness inseparable from great compassion, is called, in the state of realizing reality, the supramundane conventional coemergent. Experience in the state of not having realized reality is the mundane conventional coemergent, because it follows the shadow of the coemergent. As it is also said:

What arises from the coemergent ones
is explained to be coemergent.
Nature is explained to be coemergent,
the single binding of all aspects.
Compassion, all of it, becomes yoga.
From the mudrā and from the absence of cause,
emptiness and compassion inseparable
is explained as the awakening mind.

Here, "binding" means that to which one is to be bound. "Of all aspects," blue, yellow and the rest, it is "single," a binding without a second, because it is the coemergent alone that appears thus. The coemergent ones from which arises what is to be called coemergent are compassion and the mudrā. Therefore the one whose means is compassion, the nature of nonreferential great compassion, becomes in this way a yogin. What is being said is this: this bliss is the reality of method, but it is not the reality of wisdom. The mudrā is emptiness and reality, the supreme wisdom characterized by the non-arising of all phenomena. "From the absence of cause" means from the causeless, because all phenomena are unborn from the very beginning. These natures too are only coemergent, because they are established of themselves. The reality arisen from these is the awakening mind, the inseparability of emptiness and compassion.

If one asks, "Is this, then, something other than the gnosis of wisdom?", it is not other: that very reality, the dispelling of the adverse side, is of this kind. Thus its own nature is made fully clear.

This awakening mind alone, born from familiarization with the coemergent, the own natures of wisdom and gnosis, is the fourth, the empowerment of the great seal, because by it all the stains of conceptual thought are actually washed away. That is stated: "The fourth too is likewise."

Teaching that the very essential nature of the coemergent is manifold, he says the passage beginning "in that very reality." In that very reality of excellent wisdom, all superimpositions as entity, non-entity and the rest abide, every one of them, as its own self, of one taste with emptiness. "Children" means the childish, that is, ordinary beings. If one asks, "How then do they not know even their own nature?": "because they are deluded," that is, they are born from these delusions that do not recognize the error of grasping at entity, non-entity and the rest. Wisdom, reality, is the essence, that is, great bliss, and it is the elixir, because it is the cause that makes qualities such as the powers and the fearlessnesses increase. "This is distinguished" means that it is far superior to external essences and the like.

For one cultivating in just this way, there is the bestower of great bliss. Just as one realizes the true entity as it is, of manifold aspect and free of entity, non-entity and the rest, so the buddhas arise: through the complete purification of stainlessness, the natures of gnosis such as the powers and the fearlessnesses definitely abide. By this the Dharma body, the own nature of the mind vajra, is taught.

"Maṇḍala" means that the wheel of the maṇḍala of Vajrasattva is solely the nature of great bliss: it has manifold aspects such as blue and yellow; it is the nature of the deities absorbed in union, emptiness and compassion inseparable; it is differentiated by the divisions of joy and the rest; and he is the agent of emanation and withdrawal, whose nature is birth, death and cessation. "Excellent" means complete in nature.

Saying that when the cultivation of the meaning of the fourth alone has been accomplished it becomes the fourth empowerment, he says, "There the excellent disciple is empowered." The empowerment through the generation stage in the center of the bhaga will be explained.

"Heruka" means Heruka, the Blessed One, whose self is the body of complete enjoyment (saṃbhogakāya). In the Perfection system, because he teaches the profound and vast Dharma to the bodhisattvas who are lords of the ten grounds, and is thereby the limb of complete enjoyment of the Dharma, the body whose self is the thirty-two major marks and the eighty excellent minor marks is the body of complete enjoyment. Here, however, this is called the emanation body, since by virtue of the Dharma body it is emanated in such and such ways, in the forms of Heruka and others, for the sake of those to be trained. What the Perfection system calls the emanated buddha, the emanation body, is here too just like that, and is also like an emanation. Even though it is an emanation of the Dharma body, in the Perfection vehicle the body of complete enjoyment is set apart from it, because it is the complete enjoyment of the Dharma. Here it is so by the nature of speech as well, and it is complete enjoyment also by enjoying what is complete in taste.

He is the hero, because he has made the adverse factors rootless. He is the lord, because he drinks the taste of the great bliss of the twenty-four goddesses, who will be explained both inwardly and in the maṇḍala, and because he protects through his Dharma speech. He is the chief, because by emanating bodies of manifold forms without limit his disposition is to come forth for the welfare of others.

The garuḍa practice is the means of destroying poison: supremely, it is emptiness alone. As will be said:

When all phenomena are empty,
how could there be snake poison from that?

It is "fearsome," because it is a cause of fear for those who have no devotion to emptiness. "Accomplishing the asuras" is said because there is none who does not come under the power of the yogin who knows thus.

"Charnel ground": by the etymology "breath moves in the generation stage" and "corpses abide," the charnel ground is where the tastes that illuminate the sphere of phenomena, characterized by selflessness, abide all around. Its limitation to the number eight will be explained. Here the body whose nature is the sphere of phenomena is itself the eight-limbed charnel ground.

In the Perfection system it is said that buddhahood is produced through three countless eons; here it is said to come about in no fixed time: "the cause of generation in an instant." "Great miraculous power in the trichiliocosm": those to whom belong great miraculous powers, emanations immeasurable and manifold, in world systems such as the trichiliocosm, are so called. By this the emanation body is called vajra.

He says that the container world too is of its nature: "world systems," that is, the world systems. "That very one" means the great seal. She is the goddess, because she is the abode of supreme love-play. "Producing all the buddhas" is her nature in the causal state. "The great glory of the bodhisattvas" is her nature as the accumulations of merit and gnosis. In the resultant state, by "the Perfection of Wisdom, the friend," wisdom gone to the far shore, that is, having supremely traveled to the utmost end and gone there completely, is the meaning of the Perfection of Wisdom. "The door to hell" is so for those who reject it. As is said in the Precious Garland:

Moreover, because they think "this is bad,"
the deluded, proud of their learning,
rejecting this, bring ruin on themselves
and go headlong to Avīci.

"That very" is reality. Freedom from all obscurations is liberation. The body for the sake of all beings without exception, arising from the nature of great bliss, which is the nature of nonreferential great compassion of one taste with emptiness, is the great seal. She is of great fame, because whatever exists anywhere, belonging to anyone, from another, is of her very nature.

For that very reason she is "the mother who wishes to benefit all beings," because she is the very nature of great compassion. She is mother, because she produces the manifold in the aspect of great bliss. She is the sister's daughter, because she is born from cultivating the distinction between the conventional and ultimate truths. She is sister, because she explains the distinction between the conventional and ultimate truths. She is daughter, because she milks the unsurpassed qualities such as the powers and the fearlessnesses from the cow of the sky, that is, because she produces them. She is woman, the cognition of a woman, because of attachment through unsurpassed bliss. Illuminated by the Perfection of Wisdom, she knows the latent tendencies and introduces and establishes beings as is fitting, and so all beings become attached to her; therefore wisdom alone is called woman.

"Moving well toward men" means moving well toward men, because she brings the aim of great bliss, of supreme compassion, undefiled and endless, into the very embrace of each being; or else because beginner beings are unable to make it stable.

Wisdom is the realization free of elaboration, up to "the Perfection of Wisdom" and "the great seal." These unsurpassed mudrās and others too will be explained in the thirteenth chapter.

She is the terrifying one, because she is a cause of fear for those of the śrāvaka and other lineages, whose own nature is ignorance, not having gathered merit. As the noble Rāhulabhadra said, "She who shows terror to the childish." "Of the views" refers to the perverse ones, annihilation and superimposition. She is fearful, because she is the cause of hell for those turned away from the higher realms and from liberation. That is stated: "By not seeing you, one is bound." Where some read "fearful to the six classes of beings," "fearful" then refers to those in saṃsāra.

Therefore wisdom alone is the nirvāṇa of these and of others too. As the noble Nāgārjunapāda said:

The full understanding of saṃsāra alone
is explained to be nirvāṇa.

It is also said in the tantra of the glorious Saṃpuṭa:

Overwhelmed by the darkness of no small conceptuality,
unsteady as lightning crazed by the shattering storm,
smeared with the stains of desire and the rest, so hard to avert:
this mind, the Vajra Holder declared, is saṃsāra.
Utterly luminous, released from turbidity,
the smear of the stains of desire and the rest fully abandoned,
without grasped and grasper: that very mind
the supreme being declared the supreme nirvāṇa.
Therefore, apart from that, there is nothing at all
that is the cause of the many heaps of suffering;
and what is the cause for the dawning of infinite bliss
is nothing other than that, O you who long for liberation.

He has great miraculous power, through the complete attainment of infinite bliss.

He teaches saṃsāra in detail, from "conceptuality" up to the end of the enumeration. Conceiving in manifold ways, as existence and non-existence, permanence and annihilation, grasped and grasper and so on, is conceptuality; its continuous operation is thorough conceptualization. Arising and setting are birth and cessation.

He teaches the means of examining that, in the passage beginning "nonconceptual." If one asks what the nonconceptual ones are, he says the passage beginning "the inferior." The inferior is the subtle entity, whose nature is mind and mental factors. The supreme is just the body, an entity of gross nature, because, for the childish, it is what is conventionally termed an entity. The middling, other than these two, are the vowels and consonants (āli and kāli). Thus body, speech and mind are spoken of, as appropriate, by the words inferior, middling and supreme. "The others" are the six senses. By "whatever ... those," the stable, such as the ground, and the moving, the other living beings, are spoken of. This is stated in the Hevajra tantra:

Inferior is the meaning of the word subtle;
supreme is to be called the entity;
middling is the abandoning of both.
"The others" means the six senses;
whatever there is, those are stable and moving.

The inferior and the rest are nonconceptual; for those cultivating these natures of the coemergent, which are without conceptualization, they become clear. It is not with the eighth line that this is said; rather, by the word "nonconceptual" the cultivation is made clear.

If one asks, "Then what does 'cultivating the nonconceptual' mean?", it means that simply not thinking of any phenomena at all, by means of great bliss, is cultivation. If one asks, "Is it not then faulty to attend to great bliss?", it is indeed faulty. That too is because it is not different from all beings; since beings are empty, and emptiness cannot be thought of either, attending to that too is faulty. It is taught because it is conducive to non-thinking. As is said:

Errors are employed as needed,
in order to dispel other errors.

Therefore, when the full understanding of all phenomena as nonconceptual flows on continuously, it should be designated "ultimate cultivation." When, being conducive to non-thinking, it has become stable, only then is it the yoga of equipoise; at other times it is only the yoga of equipoise. For that very reason, in the completion stage the yoga of equipoise is taught even without the yoga of the mudrā, because one abides, by means of great bliss, with all phenomena nonconceptual. Accomplishment, however, comes only from the yoga of the mudrā.

Thus this one alone, through the perfection of his own aim, is "of great fortune," and through the perfection of the aim of others, "raining bliss on all beings": this is the conclusion.

"Thus" refers to the two stages. "Joy" comes from hearing the generation stage. "With eyes wide open" comes from the great joy of hearing the completion stage. "Those with mind" means those endowed with mind, those whose intellect has been skillfully trained in the two stages. "Bowing" means having paid homage. "What meaning" means what subject matter. "Took up correctly" means held correctly.

The condensed meaning here of the passage beginning "Then" is as follows. The book that, as was said earlier, was drawn out of the Blessed One's skull after resting at the heart of Citrasenā is merely appearing as separate. Therefore it now enters from Vajrapāṇi's hand at the place between his eyebrows, and together with its text and meaning it melts into his heart. For that very reason he is fit to be the compiler later on.

"Recitation" means at the time of the prior approach. "Various" means by place, time and the rest. Raising, stiffening and the rest are whatever was taught. It is certain with regard to the things to be pursued diligently there. "How will it be?": the question is made clear by the passage beginning "What are ...?"

kaṃ pa, that which protects bliss, is just kapāla, the skull, that is, the lotus. "The arising of triangles" means the true arising from the triangle. It is the union of the vajra and the lotus of the Blessed One and the Blessed Lady. Inwardly, the vajra is the awakening mind, the Blessed One, which truly arises from the secret wheel, since it is said, "The Blessed One has the form of semen." It is the dust of the skull, because it is present in the skull. For that very reason it is said, "from the triangle," that is, it truly arises from the secret wheel. These are joined, according to the instruction, at the place where the jewels gather, by means of the action caṇḍālī and so on. Ultimately, it is the union of the buddha and the skull, that is, of emptiness and compassion.

"Its," that is, the secret of the threefold union, is the avadhūtī channel abiding within, which in actuality is Citrasenā. Because the seventy-two thousand channels are emanated by her, they too are Citrasenā. The third meaning of the component is "various": she makes unobscured the qualities such as the wondrous powers and the fearlessnesses; that is, she is emptiness endowed with the supreme of all aspects. That very one is various, because the three realms, whose nature is the stable and the moving, are of the nature of the gnosis of great bliss. By this he makes clear that just as the Blessed One is called Buddhakapāla because of the inseparability of emptiness and compassion, so too is the Blessed Lady Citrasenā.

Thus, that by which or to which the great ones go is the vehicle, that is, the Great Vehicle, whose nature is cause and result. This is what is meant by "the people of the wheel." "Yoginīs": they are yoginīs because they are endowed with the excellence of the three yogins; the channel that is the nature of the yoginī is the avadhūtī, and they truly arise from it. This is by the union that has been explained.

"That very" is the excellent gnosis spoken of earlier; it is marvelous. Through cultivation, by the stages of heat and the rest, it is the great and supreme, that is, the gnosis of Vajradhara which is the result. "Raining bliss on all beings" is easy to understand. Some read "raining the bliss of all the buddhas"; in that case it is raining the bliss of all the aggregates and the rest, which are of the nature of buddhas.

If one asks how it is like this, he says the passage beginning "various," that is, it is just the great excellence. "In the skull" means in the source of phenomena. "It abides with secret mantra and tantra": the mantras are oṃ buddha and the rest. The tantra has already been spoken of. It abides together with them, free of stain. Alternatively, the mantra is great bliss of one taste with emptiness; tantra, the continuum, is where that abides continuously, having cleared away conceptual thoughts; that is what is meant. "Its" means of the skull alone. "Triangle" is a designation, because it is the result of the triangle, and in order to make known that there is no other means.

"There" refers to the place where the excellent gnosis, whose nature is the gnosis of wisdom, has arisen; by relying on it and by familiarizing oneself with it, it becomes the fourth empowerment. In that place there are what are called "the seven and the five," and the fourteen empowerments. "Expressing the buddhas" refers to the purified ones, Akṣobhya and the rest, which will be explained. The various applications are pacifying and the rest, which will be explained.

"Increasing life" means that very gnosis, because it has mastery over life. Alternatively, the subtle white bindu endowed with five rays at the knot of the emanation wheel is the nature of the five empowerments. "The seven buddhas are the expressers": that bindu should be surrounded by seven lines, subtly blazing; these too come out when the breath goes out, and when it enters they enter the bindu together with the mind. The meaning is that the coemergent gnosis born of meditating in this way is the seven empowerments. "Increasing life" is so because the entering and the emerging are stopped by recitation whose nature is wisdom and method: this is the instruction.

He says that all this is hard to realize without the guru's instruction: "the path of instruction," that is, the instruction whose path is the means of realizing it is so called. The instruction too I have already set out, and will set out further.

The first chapter of the commentary on the great tantra of the glorious Buddhakapāla, the Abhayapaddhati, composed by the learned Abhayākaragupta.

To teach the generation stage in detail, the chapter on mantras is spoken, beginning "Then." This is because the generation stage has the nature of mantras and of mantra bodies, and because even the whole range of the limbs of the generation stage is to be accomplished by mantra. The essence of mantra is mantra: speech that calls, that urges reality. The chapter that makes it clear will be properly explained, as the Blessed One intended, in the twelve-thousand-verse root tantra of Buddhakapāla.

"Vajra" means the samaya substance; this is the mantra for offering it. Since all accomplishments are preceded by the torma, he says the passage beginning oṃ mahākapāla vajra. Indra and the others with their retinues are all the bhūtas; "these are its deities" gives "of all the bhūtas." The torma is a particular kind of enjoyment. The mantra that blesses it is the mantra of the torma for all the bhūtas.

Here the oral instruction is this. In the ten directions are the buddhas, the bodhisattvas and the rest, Indra and the others with their retinues, and countless living beings together with their consorts (prajñās). Bringing them under the sway of the supreme prajñā, one sees them in an instant in the form of the Blessed One and the Blessed Lady in perfect union. Then, by the meditation that will also be explained for the torma, or else in an instant, one should give to them that offering which is like mercury, of the nature of nectar, together with incense and the like.

"Sāli rice" means unbroken red sāli rice. It should be washed with the milk of a white cow. The young girl is one whose limbs are all complete and who is unspoiled. "Very fine" means very finely. With the rice ground in this way one makes a form, that is, an image of Heruka; one makes it oneself. "Possessed of prajñā" means possessed of a prajñā who is unspoiled. As for "vajra crest": the vajra crest is the awakening mind of both partners, arising from the union of vajra and lotus at the time of an unspoiled young woman's first flower. Mix that with the self-arisen flower, knead the two together, and with charnel-ground charcoal powder in an equal amount, smear that image.

"The yogin of Buddhakapāla" means that in all the rites one is oneself of the very nature of Heruka. By "through the application of the form of Heruka," one should meditate on the image as Heruka actually present, the samaya being inseparable from the wisdom being. In this way the image is applied as the form of Heruka. Touching that image with the hand, one recites the mantra beginning oṃ mahā vajra one hundred and eight times. "Water" means vajra water. After that, one makes offerings to the washed image, and when, with one's wish in mind, one washes one's own face with that bathing water, the practitioner becomes invisible.

To explain the rite of the ashes, he speaks the passage beginning "its." "Having pierced it with babbola wood" is to be supplied. "With fire" means with charnel-ground fire. "Its" means the image's. As for "Brahmā's vajra," Brahmā's vajra is Vairocana. "Its" means the image's. "Ashes" means ashes. "By this" means by oṃ buddha and the rest. oṃ buddha and the rest, ending in adhiṣṭhaya hrīḥ hūṃ, is a single mantra of five lines. "Of the mantra" means the mantra.

Here is the condensed meaning together with the oral instruction. In a charnel ground, kindle dry bark of babbola wood and dry Vairocana together with charnel-ground fire. In that fire place the image with babbola wood set at its heart, and at the heart of that image make the fire offering one hundred and eight times with Brahmā's vajra, the five-line mantra oṃ buddha and the rest; burn the image as well, reducing it to ashes. After that, in a fresh bowl never yet used, a lotus vessel, draw a four-petalled lotus in the center. At its hub goes the citrasenā line of the five-line mantra, and on the petals the other lines. As for all the mantras, it is said in the Tantra of the Accomplished Hero:

The deity itself, in the form of mantra,
Accomplishes every siddhi.

Therefore mantras and deities are not different, and one meditates on them as the nature of one's own deity. Then place the ashes in the unused bowl and, for twenty-one days, three times each day, empower them by reciting the mantra oṃ buddha and the rest one hundred and eight times. After that, by "in the skull cup it likewise becomes so," the ashes come to be accomplished. "By it" means by kneading in the vajra crest accomplished in that way. "That very one" means the enemy.

He teaches the second rite. "That very" means by the accomplished ashes. Good clarified butter is clarified fat rendered from a man; with that. "Them" means the treasures, and "threatens" is to be supplied. "All" means the treasures.

He teaches the third rite, the passage beginning "with those ashes." Smearing it with the accomplished ashes, one threatens it with the threatening forefinger, circles it with the thumb, and makes it fall face down.

He teaches the fourth rite. "With it" means with the accomplished ashes. Those ashes, ground and refined lead powder, and the vajra crest are taken in equal parts. "A little" is a measure less than the lead powder. Having mixed all of this, "vajra beak" means a black boar, and its juice is blood. For the reading "vajra staff," the juice of the vajra staff is human urine. Moisten it with that, that is, with the blood or the urine. Afterwards place it in a copper vessel weighing one sang, thin enough to be pierced by a thorn, fill it with the juice of the vajra beak or with human urine, and steep it for seven days. After that, with great bile, that is, human bile, knead it in that same vessel for seven days. "Then" means from the vessel. "In an iron vessel": the meaning is that, having steeped it and so forth with the vajra beak and the rest, one should place it in a vessel of magnetic iron, one sang in measure, thin enough to be pierced by a needle. "It" means that copper vessel or iron vessel. "Placing it on the fire" means that it should be placed there. Some say that it should be placed on ground that burns without interruption and, once it is there, left for seven days. Others say that it should be placed there and boiled. Then the copper vessel turns into gold, and the iron vessel turns into silver. Still others say that, in any vessel whatever, it is first moistened with the juice of the vajra beak; then it is taken out, and the moistening, kneading and placing are done in the copper vessel or iron vessel, and it is placed, together with that copper or iron vessel, "in the middle of the fire," as before. In the method of boiling, however, one should use up borax powder and magnetite powder. "A thousand" and the rest is easy to understand.

He teaches the fifth rite. "By that very" means by the accomplished ashes. On an auspicious day at dawn, place those ashes on the head of a young girl and wash it with the vajra crest. The five scents: one fumigates with the incense of the five lamps while reciting the five-line mantra.

He teaches the sixth rite. White mustard seed is planted in the skull of a young girl, and the fruit that grows from it is empowered seven times with this mantra, the approach having first been done by reciting it one hundred thousand times. By this rite, with its reckoning of the stars, one should strike the face of the young girl blessed by all the buddhas, or of someone other than the steadfast practitioner himself, while reciting the mantra in reverse. Then that young girl laughs. "One" means that, swiftly seizing one sword among the swords that come out of the young girl's mouth, one gains the accomplishment of the sword. "Good" means the good woman, that is, one who abides in her natural state.

He teaches the seventh rite, the passage beginning "its." Pills of the ashes are wrapped in turn in gold, silver and copper, and empowered seven times through one hundred thousand recitations of the mantra just stated; then, after the signs of warmth, smoke and blazing, one should place one in the mouth. Here the measure of the gold is three māṣas with seven and a half raktis over. That of the silver is four with two and a half raktis over. That of the copper is five with five raktis over. This is stated as follows:

Seven and a half, and three māṣas;
Two and a half, and likewise four;
Five raktis and three māṣas:
With these the pills are bound, it is certain.

"The mantra that accomplishes the three worlds" means the mantra that throws a city into turmoil. Following on the rites, he will explain the eightfold division of the body colors of the Blessed One and the Blessed Lady.

This is the second chapter in the Abhayapaddhati composed by the learned Abhayākaragupta.

Having explained the mantras, he speaks the passage beginning "Then" in order to explain also the body whose nature is mantra and the nature of completion. "The chapter of the deities" means the chapter of those born from mantras and of the deities whose nature is completion. The meditation on the wheel of the maṇḍala, beginning with the syllable raṃ, will be explained clearly in one place. As for "within, the vajra ground" and so on: the vajra ground is the navel, and this means in its center.

In the completion stage, the great self, the syllable e, is the nature of the dharmadhātu. The triangle into which the syllable e transforms is the source of phenomena, the wheel of emanation; one should imagine it as the very nature of the three realms. In the center of that triangle is the syllable e or the seed syllable laṃ, and what it transforms into is the earth maṇḍala. At the heart, the syllable baṃ or the syllable oṃ is dharma, that is, mind, and its nature is what arises from it: the wheel is the wheel of Dharma.

"Having the nature of all the channels" means having the nature of the eight channels. The channels of the four wheels too are of its nature, because they are not different from the mind of great bliss. "On the head the yoginīs abide" refers to the thirty-two yoginīs, in particular where the drops of awakening mind trickling down from the seat of great bliss abide. "Excellent" means the nature of that wheel of great bliss. The general and particular functions of these thirty-two channels, the nature of all the channels, through which the awakening mind flows, and their names as well, I shall explain following another tantra.

Among them, the avadhūtī carries only the awakening mind. The lalanā, hanging as it were at the root of the navel, carries the fluid from what is eaten and drunk once it has been ripened by the fire of the belly. This fluid, moving into one channel after another, pervades the body. The rasanā carries blood from the fluid; therefore it too pervades the body in just that way. The summary of them all is this:

Abhedyā carries teeth and nails.
Sūkṣmarūpā brings forth hair of head and body.
Divyā carries skin and grime.
Vāmā carries flesh,
And Vāmanī comes to carry fat.
Kūrmajā is held to carry bone.
Bhāvakī comes to carry the kidneys.
Sekā comes to carry the heart.
Doṣavatī is held to carry the eyes.
The Tail One is said to carry bile.
Mātarā carries the lungs.
Śarvarī carries the intestines.
Śītadā carries the sides of the belly.
Ūṣmā comes to carry the stomach.
Lalanā is the one that carries Akṣobhya.
Avadhūtī carries the moon.
Rasanā is said to carry blood.
Pravaṇā is held to be for excrement.
Joyful Face is at the hairline.
Sāmānyā carries pus.
Phlegm is assigned to Hetudāyikā.
Viyogā is the one that carries menstrual blood.
Premaṇī is said to be for fat.
Siddhā carries the tears of grief.
The Cleaving One is held to be for saliva.
Sumanā is the one that carries mucus.
Trivṛttā is held to be for form.
Kāminī is said to be for sound.
Gehā comes to carry smell.
Caṇḍikā is the one that brings forth taste.
For touch, know her as the Yoginī.
For phenomena she becomes Māradārikā.

Here the lalanā, the rasanā and the avadhūtī are Citrasenā. Of the rest, the pairs in order are Sumālinī, Kapālinī, Bhīmā and Durjayā. The others are to be known as having the nature of the twenty yoginīs beginning with Śubhamekhalā. Māradārikā is simply merged with the avadhūtī: it is the channel of time, rising from the navel as far as the tip of the tongue, through which flow the yoginīs who are the phenomena characterized by cessation. Now it is included by gathering up the yoginīs, because it is the very nature of ignorance.

If one asks, "How then is it that the Two Examinations speaks of thirty-two that carry the awakening mind?", there is no fault, because the channel of time makes it flow into cessation itself. Where the channels are shown as having the nature of yoginīs, the meaning is that this one is left out entirely. Through its power, although this world is born as the nature of great bliss, one does not see its maṇḍala, nor its bliss either. For that very reason it is said, "With effort, abandon what remains of all."

The Blessed One, the vajra of the awakening mind, is the means of all. Furthermore, he teaches the completion maṇḍala, which ultimately is unborn from any seed, the very nature of the awakening mind in which emptiness and compassion are inseparable: "Adorned with sixteen parts" refers to the throat. Here the parts are the channels; possessing sixteen channels, it is the wheel of enjoyment. "Emanation" is that emanation lotus in which are the sixty-four petals, which are the channels.

"Thus" means that in the four wheels there are one hundred and twenty channels: the channels come to one hundred and twenty. "One should imagine it thus" means that one should imagine the completion maṇḍala in this way, that is, meditate on it and recite.

Here the life-wind in the lalanā and the rasanā shifts sixteen times through day and night, half a watch at a time, because it governs them; these are at the throat. That same wind, by the division into the four maṇḍalas, governing four daṇḍas each, comes to sixty-four daṇḍas in the wheel of emanation. As the Blessed One taught in the tantras such as the Saṃpuṭa, and as the noble Nāgārjunapāda also said:

In the maṇḍalas of fire and of wind,
Of Mahendra, and likewise of water,
It moves, and it goes upward, to the side,
Straight ahead, and downward.

Because that same wind, enjoying the two maṇḍalas whose natures are wisdom and means respectively, moves fully into the root wheel, there are the thirty-two channels in the wheel of great bliss. By the division into day and night, which comes from the division into wisdom and means, there are the eight watches; these are at the heart.

What other tantras teach, that a day and night consist of sixty daṇḍas, comes from assigning three hundred and sixty breaths to each daṇḍa. Here, by setting it out in four maṇḍalas, three hundred and thirty-seven and a half breaths are assigned to each daṇḍa. In both cases there is no difference: in a day and night they come to twenty-one thousand six hundred breaths. Thus, in relation to the completion stage, the recitation too is made fully clear. It is said:

The lord of yogins, through day and night,
By his recitation counts
Twenty thousand and a thousand,
And likewise six hundred.

When the mind becomes stable through reciting the vidyā, one comes face to face with the completion maṇḍala, whose nature is great bliss. "Possessing wisdom" means possessing great wisdom, skilled in meditating on the maṇḍala of the completion stage. "In the vajra body" means in the body whose nature is great bliss, of one taste with emptiness. For that very reason, because self and others become fit for the accomplishment of mahāmudrā, it is the great self. "There" means in the wheel of Dharma. In the generation stage, it is the water maṇḍala arisen from baṃ.

The maṇḍala of all the channels
Dissolves into the water maṇḍala.

This means that the other channel maṇḍalas shift only into the eight channels of the heart; therefore all the channel maṇḍalas are gathered into the water maṇḍala.

As for "again" and so on: beneath the wheel of emanation is the fire maṇḍala, arisen from a red syllable ma or from raṃ, triangular and blazing, as though clinging to the channel wheel of emanation. Beneath the fire is the wind maṇḍala, arisen from the syllable yā or the syllable yaṃ. "The form that stirs all the channels": that wind maṇḍala makes the fire blaze and sets moving the awakening mind that abides in all the channels. "What is dissolving is awareness of the wheel": what is dissolving into the wheel is awareness, that which makes known; when the awakening mind set in motion by it dissolves into the wind wheel, the coemergent bliss comes to be known.

Through all of them dissolving,
The yogin abides in the center.

"There" means in the knowing of coemergent bliss. "All" means the aggregates and the rest, which are like drops; "the yogin" is mind; and it too abides there as its very nature. "Moon" means the awakening mind, in the wheel of great bliss up to the crown of the head. "Sun" means the rajas, below the wheel of emanation. For hūṃ āḥ some read hūṃ aṃ. The blue syllable hūṃ is in the wheel of Dharma; the red syllable āḥ or aṃ is in the wheel of emanation; in the wheel of enjoyment is a red syllable oṃ, because this is taught in another tantra.

"Adorned with the syllable ka and the syllable ha" means that it is both the syllable ka and the syllable ha, and is also adorned. kaṃ is great bliss, and its nature is this: in the wheel of great bliss, at the forehead, one should imagine the syllable ha, empty and indestructible, white, adorned with a half moon and a drop. "The syllable ka dissolves into the syllable ha" means that great bliss becomes merged with the indestructible, up to the word "inseparable." "ha and hūṃ inseparable" means that just as the nature of the indestructible is, so is the syllable hūṃ: the meaning is that each is of the nature of both. How so? From the statement "Its bliss is said to be what is desired": kaṃ is wisdom and ha is means, and they are inseparable. Thus the syllable ha, whose nature is bliss, joined with the syllable ūṃ, which is wisdom, gives the two letters ha and hūṃ merged; the syllable hūṃ is mind, the means. This is the inseparability of wisdom and means.

"The rays of the syllable hūṃ": the rays of the caṇḍālī fire, urged on by the wind, reach the wheel of great bliss. There they stir the hare-marked moon, and as the moon's torment of conceptual thought is pacified, the snow-like syllable ka, which is great bliss, the sun, which is rajas, and ha and oṃ melt together and come to rest in the syllable hūṃ. Then the syllable hūṃ too melts and comes to rest in the seed of the wheel of emanation. That and the two maṇḍalas of sun and moon also become merged, and "sun" means that one should view the rajas maṇḍala alone as predominant, in the form of a drop; the semen is not what predominates. "Again the rays of the syllable hūṃ" means that the lines of light from the drop of the melted hūṃ touch all the channel wheels. "Seed" means the form of the drop that gives rise to great bliss. Thus the yoga of the drop is taught.

In meditating on the completion maṇḍala, without generating seed syllables and the like, the caṇḍālī whose nature is rajas, emerging with certainty, blazes intensely. The hare-marked moon, supremely kindled by it, drips all the more. The caṇḍālī, increased by that, blazes yet more intensely. The hare-marked moon, supremely heated by it, drips still more.

Thus the inner moon and sun come together evenly and without interruption, and there fully arises the nature of great bliss, which overpowers the imputed aggregates, elements and the rest.

Those who are not inclined to this, having gained mastery in restraining the life-force through the recitation already explained, draw in the downward-clearing wind, focus on the mahāmudrā in the navel maṇḍala, and by holding the wind cause the caṇḍālī to blaze. Then, in just that way, great bliss becomes clear. Those who cannot do that remain, in just that way, imagining that the wind is held in the navel maṇḍala. When the breath goes out, they imagine the Blessed Lady blazing as a straight line in the nature of caṇḍālī. When, having reached the seat of great bliss, the breath comes in, the syllable haṃ, in the form of a drop dripping from the moon, visualized in just that way, comes to the navel maṇḍala whose nature is great bliss and becomes free of any object. One should remain thus until the life-force is stopped, and in just that way one should practice the outgoing and incoming breaths again and again. Whoever cannot examine the going out and coming in of the life breath practices by strong aspiration alone.

The meditator of the generation stage, with that alone as the preliminary, should focus on all of this according to his strength. One who delights in elaboration should view the Blessed Lady Citrasenā, the nature of the entire wheel of yoginīs, the very self of mahāmudrā, blazing in the nature of caṇḍālī and radiating to the ten directions; and should view the tathāgatas, roused by the light of that fire of great passion, in every way enamored with great passion, entering the wheel of great bliss. Then one should accomplish everything just so. Thus the blessing of the buddhas too arises in this. This is a division of the caṇḍālī instruction. All of this too is related as the holy guru instructed it.

For "by that very," the words "with fresh human sinew" are to be supplied. "Thus the Perfection Lady" means the nature of the perfection of wisdom. The Blessed One is the nature of great compassion. "The light rays born from her body" means the caṇḍālī rays born from the body of that Blessed One. "By that dripping" means that, at the time the seed of the wheel of Dharma is washed, the yoginīs who are the nature of the eight channels of the wheel of Dharma are emanated outward. "The five fragrances" are the five nectars. The flower is woman's rajas. The incense is that of the lamp of caṇḍālī, blazing with the passion of Rāgavajrā. "The hero complete" means that, seeing the hero accomplished, it drips as before. "Mango leaves" are sahakāra leaves. "One should supplicate" means one should earnestly seek it.

As for "through empowerment": empowerment is what makes one stainless. The learned are those who possess knowledge, those who know the means of liberation. As for "without a son" and so on: just as a house whose son has died is empty of a son, so a person without empowerment is without omniscient wisdom. Just as, even when everything has been assembled, a vīṇā without strings cannot be played, and no sound comes from it. "For as long as the buddhas do not abandon him" runs up to "he does not escape hell." By this he teaches that all the empowerments must without fail be received. "When one has become Heruka": even if one has completed the meditation in the form of Heruka, what is the use? For without empowerment there is no buddhahood. The meaning is that it becomes merely merit. "That" means "to that." From the indication "outer and secret," the wisdom empowerment and the fourth empowerment should also be understood.

This is the third chapter in the Abhayapaddhati, the commentary on the great tantra of glorious Buddhakapāla.

To explain the garuḍa rites, he speaks the passage beginning "Then." "Of us" means "we." "By the rays" means by the rays of the mantra emanated in the first chapter. "The rays of the heart" means those rays of Citrasenā's heart that, having brought the nāgas, came back and entered Citrasenā's heart. "Commanded" means "we." "Goddess" means "O Goddess." "Spoke" means spoken by Citrasenā. Having blessed the nāgas, in order to teach the reality of poison extensively through their mouths, there are the five questions: the passage beginning "The garuḍa rites of the tīrthikas and others." These are the garuḍa rites of Viṣṇu, Īśvara and the others, and of the Blessed One Vajradhara, distinguished by mantra, medicine and samādhi. "How is it held?": and that is wisdom too; by "it truly arises," "arising" is also meant, that is, how wisdom arises. The neuter gender is used either to stress the particular or as the usage of the scripture itself. "Of poison" means poison. "How" asks about the divisions. "Asked" means asked by the nāgas.

The passage beginning "Viṣṇu" answers the fifth question, concerning the side of the tīrthikas. Viṣṇu, Īśvara and the others have inverted minds and do not directly know the reality of things. In their system, the poison held in the fangs and the fangs themselves exist ultimately. Therefore, because they do not know reality, they are harmed by the ills of poison: one dies, and cannot be saved even by garuḍa rites. Thus "it is said to be seized at the time":

Arrow, direction, time, fire and horse,
Pair, moon and the rest, by day;
Six, Veda, hand, seven, arrow,
Quality, fullness, mountain, by night.

In the answers beginning with these, the half-watches in their order are the times, and one seized at such a time is said to be incurable. This is the answer to the third question.

He answers the first question with the passage beginning "of the snake." "If one is not seized at such a time, it takes it away": "takes away" means removes. There is a method, devised by Viṣṇu, Īśvara and the others, for overpowering snake venom. When that is applied, the one who was seized has no snake venom. When, for one seized at such a time, there is no garuḍa rite, the passage beginning "by the snake" sums it up. "Great miraculous power" is because one performs the garuḍa rite.

He answers the second question, concerning the side of the Blessed One, with the passage beginning "the Blessed One." He is the Teacher because he teaches what is to be rejected and what is to be adopted. Viṣṇu, Īśvara and the others are also called teachers by those devoted to them; therefore it says, "who taught emptiness definitively." From the reasoning stated in

Because it arises from means, and the cause is hidden,
To explain it is difficult,

he, having understood the reality of all things, and being under the sway of love, taught those to be trained the reality whose nature is emptiness. "Definitively" means having grasped it; it qualifies the action. The intention is that Śaṃkara and the others have not themselves ascertained reality, and do not speak of it definitively to others either.

That the samādhi of emptiness he taught is alone the unsurpassed remedy for poison: with this he answers the first question. "In emptiness" means in things without intrinsic nature, being free of the nature of one or many.

Whatever is dependent origination,
That itself you hold to be emptiness.

This means in all phenomena, that is, in the aggregates, elements and sense sources. Snake, seizing and poison too: how could they be? They are simply nonexistent. The meaning is this: because the reality of poison is ascertained through emptiness, the poison will without doubt be destroyed. For that very reason, the answer to the third question is the passage beginning "how": the "what" in "in all" has the sense of denial.

The answer to the fifth question is the passage beginning "seized": one dies from not ascertaining emptiness. Therefore, since they are taught by the proponent of emptiness, the Blessed One spoke definitively.

Now, in order to explain that those who have no inner poison have no poison from snakes and the like either, he speaks the passage beginning "Attachment." Attachment is clinging to defiled things. Delusion is ignorance, clinging to things, non-things and the like. "Greatly Renowned One" addresses Citrasenā. As for "the ordinary object": ordinary here means ignorance, and what is imputed by it is ordinary. As for "Objects by nature are stainless": nature here is emptiness. The five objects, form and the rest, the five constructed by the mind, from which, through it, the stains of attachment together with their habitual tendencies have been thoroughly uprooted, and which have the nature of emptiness, are so called. The singular used for the plural is the usage of the scripture. Their stainlessness, too, comes from the stainlessness of mind. And mind is by nature stainless, as it is said in the Pramāṇavārttika:

The nature of mind is luminosity;
The stains are adventitious.

The connection with the root text is the passage beginning "stains." The meaning is this: attachment and the rest alone are the inner poison, because they destroy the life of the accumulations of merit and wisdom. Through the power of that mind, the very nature of objects is poison too. When, from the stainlessness of mind, whose nature is emptiness and compassion inseparable, the absence of poison is ascertained, then the very nature of objects is also free of stain: how could it be poison? It is simply not poison. In this way it is made clear that, when one eats poison and the like relied upon in this manner, it does no harm, because of one's aspiration toward reality. This is taught by way of an example in the Hevajra Tantra:

The witless worldly man
Who has eaten poison falls into a faint;
One free of delusion knows reality,
And at that very moment casts off grief.

Here "free of delusion" is because one fully knows the reality of poison. "Knows reality" means aspiring toward the reality of poison. "Grief" means grief. The destruction of inner poison is also taught there by that same example:

So too, one who knows the means to nirvāṇa
And has labored at Hevajra
Is not bound by ignorance and the rest,
Nor by the bonds of delusion and the rest.

Here "at Hevajra" means at the wisdom in which emptiness, great bliss and objectless great compassion are inseparable. "By ignorance and the rest" means by the twelve links. "By delusion and the rest" means by the afflictions.

Thus it is taught that one who is free of inner poison is not harmed by outer poison either, and with the passage beginning "even by the snake" he answers the fourth question. As for "Ordinary poison and the snake's poison are only faults": ordinary is ignorance, and what arises from its imputations is ordinary poison. "The snake's" means belonging to the snake, because the suffix aṇ takes no strengthening. Both that and the poison of objects are construed with "what do they do?"

He answers the second question concerning the side of the tīrthikas: "the gods are obscured by objects." Śaṃkara and the others have their knowledge of reality obscured by the afflictive and cognitive obscurations regarding objects; therefore they have both inner and outer poison. Nevertheless, through their great miraculous power they perform means of pacifying poison, yet they say that one seized at such a time is incurable. For that very reason it will be said, "Great in miraculous power, but without understanding."

He shows with the passage beginning "objects" that the Blessed One Buddhakapāla, who has uprooted all obscurations together with their habitual tendencies, is not bound even when enjoying sense pleasures. That the yogins of Buddhakapāla who act in the same way have, even when they enjoy, nothing to fear, by virtue of the yoga of Buddhakapāla, he teaches with the passage beginning "buddha." Concluding by indicating everything: "therefore, no one seized at the time" means by the practice of Buddhakapāla.

Having thus stated the ultimate means of pacifying poison, he teaches the conventional one: the passage beginning "śāgili." "Bound" means that here the action of the śālika becomes that of perfectly binding. The intention is that the tīrthikas say one seized at such a time is incurable, but the Blessed One does not.

Having spoken thus, the nāgas
Bowed to Citrasenā
And went each to their own abodes.

This is spoken by the compiler: the passage beginning "such." "Fully realized" means contemplating one's own nature with certainty. oṃ is body, speech and mind. śūnyatā is the absence of intrinsic nature. jñāna is great compassion.

Because those three are indivisible, that whose self is the nature of vajra, which is its own essence, is "I." To teach that just as I am such, so is everything, he speaks the passage beginning "empty and not empty." It is empty because it is devoid of its own essence. It is not empty because it cannot even be expressed in that way, and because it abides as the nature free of duality.

"Five spans" means within root lines of sixty finger-widths: this is the square maṇḍala. The circle is thirty finger-widths, and within it is an eight-petaled lotus of ten finger-widths, encircled by a garland of blue vajras. In other tantras, purification by digging the earth and so on, removing foreign objects and the like, is taught. Here, however, because this is a supreme tantra, he states that by touching it with the hand and reciting the mantra one hundred and eight times, one should purify the ground of the maṇḍala, and also the ground of the fire-offering hearth that will be explained: the passage beginning "ground." He states that here, in the maṇḍala made in connection with the poison hearth, the deity platform and the bands are not to be made: the passage beginning "platform." "With red colored powder" means that in all four directions every area of the maṇḍala should be filled with red powder alone. He states that it is not as in the Guhyasamāja and other tantras, where the colors of the ground differ according to the directions of Vairocana and the others: the passage beginning "other." The rest, such as the five lines (rekhā), should be done with pigments of the five colors as appropriate. Offering the torma, purifying the ground, consecrating the cords with mantra, laying out the lines, applying the colored powders, and so on, are to be done by the guru who is endowed with the yoga of Heruka.

"The vases" are vases whose bases are not black, with full bellies and hanging lips, and long necks; they stand in the intermediate directions, that of Īśāna, that of Agni, and so on. On the four vases are the four emblems, respectively. The victory vase is the fifth, that of the principal deity. Although it is not mentioned here, following the method of other tantras it should be placed slightly to the south of the eastern gate. That very vase is here the vase for all activities. Its emblem here is a ḍamaru drum in the center of a source of phenomena, and upon the ḍamaru a vajra and a lotus. The master should become endowed with the yoga of Buddhakapāla. If one asks how, he speaks the passage beginning "lotus": on the center of a multicolored lotus.

As for the passage beginning "afterward": the master himself, as Heruka surrounded by the eight goddesses, should empower himself immediately after absorbing the wisdom being. "Rays born from the body of Heruka" means that the master should cause the eight goddesses, together with the rays of light emanating from the eight channels at the heart, to touch the Tathāgatas. Goddesses emanated by the rays of the Tathāgatas who have come with those goddesses are made to bathe Heruka and the eight goddesses. "Crowned" means that Heruka is crowned, together with the yoginīs; the meaning is that they too are crowned. "The maṇḍala complete" means: when one has thus been completed as Heruka oneself, one instantly visualizes, in the maṇḍala of colored powder, the commitment being, the wisdom being, the empowerment and the sealing, in order to accomplish the maṇḍala.

"Torma" is for the nāgas. "Lamps": four lamps, of honey, of butter, of fish fat, and of fragrant sesame oil, should be offered close to the maṇḍala. The four fruits are mango, coconut, plantain and wood apple. The three pairs of cloth are the lower garment, the upper garment and the headcloth. The yoginī is the guru's wisdom consort, offered by oneself, and the hero is the guru. "Circle" means the gathering of the assembly.

Regarding "With mind one-pointed on the heart of the commitment," the heart of the commitment is the Blessed One. "Averting one's own fear" means that the intention is that this is not for one who has no fear of poisoning. Stationary poison is that of aconite and the like. Moving poison is that of snakes and the like. Body-born poison is that of abscesses and the like. Artificial poison is such as mixing butter and honey in equal parts. The four kinds of poison are rendered poisonless. "Stick" is a tooth stick.

The disciple should be visualized in the form of Heruka. "At the throat" means at that of the master and at that of the disciple. "Also in the hand" means in the left. "On a moon" refers to the one at the throat and the one in the hand. "By the power of meditation" means that one visualizes the body of Heruka, the moon and so on, and, placing the hand so visualized on the disciple's head, recites the mantra. "Vase" means the measure of a vase. "Hard to bear" means painful. The passage beginning "the garuḍa practice" means: when this being comes to master the practice of the garuḍa, then nowhere at all will there be any support for us by standing firm in order to protect ourselves.

"Immediately" means immediately after the preparatory rite. "Bestowed" has the suffix kta applied in the sense of the agent, in accordance with the grammatical treatise; the meaning is that it should be placed on the head in sequence. As for the passage beginning "conch vessel": on one stand is the conch, and on the second is the copper vessel. "The vases" are the victory vase and the four other vases. Regarding "the eight yoginīs having melted," these are those abiding as its very nature together with the Blessed One and the goddesses dwelling in the maṇḍala. The very becoming of one taste of the commitment being and the wisdom being is the consecration of the maṇḍala. "In the vase" means that in the victory vase the Blessed One and the eight goddesses reside, melted, and in the other vases one imagines the goddesses as appropriate. "Water" means that a little of the water of the victory vase and the others should be put into the copper vessel and the conch vessel. "With water" means with the water of the copper vessel, and after that with a little water from the conch bowl. "Have recite" means have read aloud: the master has the disciple do so. "Have worship" means the guru has the disciple do so. "The disciple" refers to the removal of the blindfold. "Again" is in relation to taking the water for bathing. With both hands holding the vajra, one has the disciple drink, while reciting the mantra of Heruka, three times, the water of the conch mixed with the water of the copper vessel.

The benefits of drinking the water will be explained.

Here the sequence is as follows. A master who has been duly empowered and has thereafter abided by it as a disciple, who is skilled in the three samādhis, and who has become one in nature with Heruka through reciting the mantra, wishes to empower a disciple who has been well examined over a long time without interruption and who earnestly seeks the goal. First, in a maṇḍala house or similar place free from every harm, such as from kings and thieves, in a maṇḍala of colored powder erected according to the rite, or in a maṇḍala painted on cloth or the like, he worships with the five offerings described, the lamps, the fruits and so on. When milk has been offered for the nāgas, he should perform the preparatory rite for the vases.

The victory vase is marked with a ḍamaru standing in the center of a source of phenomena, and with a vajra and a lotus. It is filled with scented water and contains the five precious substances, gold, silver, sapphire, pearl and coral; the five grains, barley, wheat, sesame, beans and unhusked rice; and the five medicinal plants, bṛhatī, kaṇṭakārī, white aparājitā, daṇḍotpala and sahadevā. Its mouth is adorned with mango leaves, its neck is wrapped in red cloth, and it bears a garland of flowers. On top of it is a vajra tied with a flower garland, and it stands upon a vessel filled with milk. Having furnished it within with the commitment being, generated in an instant, he invites from Akaniṣṭha, by the rays of the seed syllable hūṃ resting on a sun in the center of a vajra, the Blessed One surrounded by the eight yoginīs. He recites the heart mantra seven times over the welcoming water and offers it with this: oṃ heruka vajra arghaṃ pratīccha hūṃ. Then he causes the Blessed One to enter the center of the vase. He imagines that water as the essence of the awakening mind, of the nature of great bliss, become one with the commitment being. Holding in his left fist the flower garland tied to the vajra, he consecrates it with the heart mantra one hundred and eight times.

The other vases of the maṇḍala are completely filled with scented water, furnished with mango branches, their necks wrapped in red cloth, bearing flower garlands, and marked respectively with the emblems of the skull cup, the khaṭvāṅga, the curved knife and the ḍamaru. They contain within them the water of the melted commitment goddesses, indivisible from the wisdom goddesses. Each should be consecrated one hundred and eight times with the mantra of the seed syllable of its goddess. In the vase marked with the skull cup are Śauṇḍinī and Citrasenā. In the one marked with the khaṭvāṅga are the Spirit Woman and Subhadrā. In the one marked with the curved knife are Caturbhujā and Pātālavāsinī. In the one marked with the ḍamaru are Ākāśavāsinī and the Desirous One. hūṃ oṃ hūṃ svāḥ hūṃ khaṃ hūṃ hūṃ are, in order, the seed syllables of these goddesses.

After that he should properly worship them all together with flowers, incense and so on, offer torma, and, preceded by praise with the verses beginning "vajra lotus," ring the bell and pay homage.

Immediately after this rite of preparing the vases, the master, adorned with the three cloths and other things offered by the disciple, having sported with his wisdom consort, sits on a seat outside the area of the maṇḍala. The disciple has offered three maṇḍalas, holds a flower garland, kneels with his knees on the ground, and joins his palms; he is a fit vessel for the methods of pacifying poison and the like. The master draws him mentally into his own body, emits him from the vajra so that he rests in the lotus of the wisdom consort, and there the melted Tathāgatas, invited by the rays of the seed syllable at the heart and entering through the Avadhūtī, empower him. Then the master places him, in the form of Heruka, at the eastern gate of the maṇḍala, and has him request entry with these words:

O Blessed One, Guru and Teacher,
Solely devoted to the welfare of beings,
I seek the excellent method of pacifying poison.
The practice that performs the śālika has arisen,
Which brings excellent benefit to beings
And averts the seizure when it comes:
Great Teacher, teach this to me,
Through union with Buddhakapāla.

He has him say three times:

I go for refuge to the Three Jewels.
I confess each wrong and nonvirtue.
I rejoice in the virtue of beings.
I hold a buddha's awakening in mind.

He protects him with the heart mantra, and should imagine at the disciple's heart, in the center of a black vajra, the letter hūṃ; at his throat, on a red lotus, āḥ; and at his head, on a white wheel, oṃ; the letters being black, red and white respectively. Then, rising, with his right hand holding scented water and the vajra, saying hūṃ āḥ oṃ, he touches the heart, the throat and the head, and gives a flower at the head, incense and a lamp in front, and perfume at the heart.

After that, facing east, he should give a tooth stick of aśvattha or a similar tree, its tip not split, not crooked or otherwise faulty, about twelve finger-widths long, with a flower garland wound around its tip, washed in fragrant water, and consecrated seven times with the tooth-stick mantra. The disciple too, facing east or north, chews the tip and, without looking up, throws it onto a patch of ground the size of a cowhide that has been smeared. Then, to rinse the mouth, the master has him drink three handfuls of scented water with this mantra: oṃ hrīḥ biśuddha sarba dharmā sarba pāpa ni ca asye biśodhaya sarba bikalpan apanaya hūṃ.

After that the master visualizes a moon disc on his own left hand and on his throat, which are smeared with perfume and wreathed with flowers. He should imagine that the buddhas of the ten directions, invited by its rays, melt through great passion and dissolve into the two moons. Then, placing that hand on the disciple's head, he recites the mantra of the preparatory rite seven times. Then, imagining that those buddhas at the throat and in the hand, together with the moons, enter the disciple's body and bless him, he should protect the disciple. Then he spreads out kuśa grass over which the heart mantra has been recited, and gives it to him as a pillow and a mat.

"The master, entering the maṇḍala house, should perform meditation and recitation" means the following. Having performed the preparatory rite for the disciple, at dawn the master gives torma, comes out of the maṇḍala house, asks the disciple about his dreams, and should protect him with the heart mantra. Then, having worshipped in the maṇḍala house according to his means, he conceives that the circle of deities is actually present there. Mentally taking the principal deity as his guru, he himself first enters the maṇḍala by the rite for bringing in disciples that will be explained, and has the lord of the family carry his own empowerment through to its conclusion. From that very deity the master obtains authorization for all activities, and he should then make the blessing of truth with these words:

By the truth whereby supreme wisdom,
The self of wisdom and means, is the maṇḍala,
By that truth, O Protector,
Fulfill my wishes completely.
Phenomena are like reflections,
Clear, pure and free of turbidity,
Ungraspable and inexpressible,
Truly arisen from cause and action;
They are born from suchness itself.
By such a truth, may all the disciples
See the maṇḍala clearly as a reflection,
Every one of them, free of evil.

After that he has the disciple bathe. The disciple is made clean with fine perfumes and the like, wears a lower and an upper garment of red cloth, and has a red blindfold tied over his face; he is visualized in the form of glorious Heruka. He is made to hold a flower with the mantra ā khaṃ bīra hūṃ, offers a gift outside the curtain, is sprinkled with water from the vase for all activities, and is placed with the syllable hūṃ at the eastern gate of the maṇḍala. The master should then question him in order to bring him into the maṇḍala, saying, "O, who are you?" The disciple should answer, "I am a son of the Victor."

Then the master has him generate the awakening mind with this: oṃ sarba yoga cittaṃ utpādayāmi. He should place the vajra, together with the flower, at the disciple's heart, saying hūṃ surate samaya stvaṃ hoḥ siddhi bajra yathā sukhaṃ. Then, placing the vajra on the crown of his head, he should say: "This is your commitment vajra. If you speak of this to anyone, it will shatter your crown." Then, placing the vajra at his heart, he says:

oṃ Now Vajrasattva himself
Has truly entered your heart.
If you speak of this way,
He will at once tear free and depart.

With this he has him take the oath. Then there is the milk in a lotus vessel, blessed by the three syllables, set upon the vase in which the five nectars are mixed, and recited over seven times with this: oṃ pañca amṛta udaka ṭhaḥ hūṃ. He has the disciple drink it while saying:

This is your water of hell:
If you transgress the commitment, it will burn you;
By guarding the commitment comes accomplishment.
Drink the water of vajra nectar!

Having said, "You must not despise me, lest you break your commitment and go to hell," he joins his palms toward the Blessed One and says:

These disciples whom I
Have properly brought into the maṇḍala,
O deities, may the order of their families
Accord with their merit.
Whatever accomplishment this one will attain,
Whatever family he is a vessel for,
And whatever the power of his merit,
Let it be so shown in the maṇḍala.

Having made this blessing of truth, he has the disciple cast into the center of the maṇḍala, with the syllable hūṃ, the flower that he earlier took up with the five syllables and that has been blessed with the syllable traṃ, and has him recite oṃ bajra heruka bajra puṣpaṃ pratīccha hūṃ. The master ties the cast flower onto the disciple's head and says, "O mighty one, take this back!"

After that he visualizes at the disciple's heart the lord upon a moon, at his forehead the syllable ra, and on his two eyes two syllables oṃ. Reciting

oṃ Today Vajrasattva himself
Strives to open your eyes.
He opens the eyes of all;
The vajra eye is unsurpassed,

he removes the blindfold. "In the maṇḍala whose nature is ninefold": he worships with the mantras beginning oṃ heruka bajra puṣpaṃ pratīccha hūṃ, and also with the mantras for incense and the rest. Saying, "O vajra one, look!", he should show him the maṇḍala, first stating the names of its deities.

Immediately after entering the maṇḍala, the disciple prostrates with the five limbs, sets his right knee on the ground, joins his palms, and makes supplication, saying:

I am sunk in the mire of saṃsāra:
Be my ally and protector!
I am of meager fortune and little zeal,
Parted from the buddhas and the Dharma.
O Protector, Teacher, great Guru,
Protector, have compassion!

Then the master, at the time when the nectar melted by the blazing of Caṇḍikā at his navel washes the seed syllable at his heart, supplicates the goddesses emanated as the nature of the eight channels at the heart, and the Tathāgatas of the ten directions, invited by the rays of the seed syllable at the heart, together with the knowledge goddesses, saying:

O compassionate ones, I pray you, grant this one empowerment;
Again and again I bow, and with joined palms I supplicate.

They bear parasols, victory banners, flags, garments, music and so on, hold in their hands white vases filled with the water of wisdom, and sing songs of auspiciousness. At the time when they confer empowerment on the disciple in the form of Heruka, the master fills the copper vessel and the conch vessel resting on the two stands with water from the victory vase and the others. First with a little water of the copper vessel, held in both hands together with the vajra, and after that with the conch, he says:

The great vajra empowerment,
To which the three realms bow,
Arisen from the abode of the three secrets
Of all the buddhas, I shall bestow.

Saying oṃ heruka pīra stvaṃ abhiṣiñcāmi, he bathes the disciple, and should bless him with the crown. To the disciple thus empowered with the water and crown empowerments, he gives to drink three times the remaining water of the conch, held as before, mixed with the remaining water in the copper vessel, while reciting the mantra given in the first chapter, beginning oṃ buddha.

"Prepared ones" means those that are made. "Others" are the poisons born in the body, such as abscesses.

"Secondary poisons" are those of the road and the like. The meaning is "the water of arrow poison." "Smearing" means having smeared poisons on food and the like so that they will be eaten. "Which" means the poisons. "Those" means all of them. "In that very" means in just those by which one is seized or which are given. "By the action of the śālikā" means: if you wish to know merely the action of the śālikā, know it from that alone. Alternatively, the meaning is the methods of pacifying poison, all of them, both worldly and ultimate.

In the passage beginning "thousands," to one among a hundred thousand whose intention is pure, one gives the instructions and the mantra that will be explained. The passage beginning "snake" concerns one seized by a snake. "Likewise" means not making the effort. The meaning is this: as long as other methods of curing poison, such as mantras and medicinal preparations, are being applied to the one seized, or will be applied, the yogin of Buddhakapāla should not set to work. Rather, when the other healers of poison have abandoned him, saying, "This one has been seized at the time; he is dead," then he should undertake the rite for the one who has been seized.

The passage beginning "rays" is spoken to the nāgas. When, in the abodes of the nāgas such as Vāsuki, they are urged on by the rays of the seed syllable at the heart of oneself in the form of Heruka, their hearts wither, and they say, "What shall we do?" "Formerly" means in the first chapter. Here too it reminds them of what Buddhakapāla spoke; the intention is, "You, overpowered by the rays of the mantra, petitioned the Blessed Lady and accepted her command." "Then it becomes" is the nāgas' reply: "It is not thus that the knowledge mantra is accomplished." "O rays, depart!" Because there is no one seized at the time, there is no seized and no seizer: the seizer is nothing other than the seizure at the time. "Fear": because one who is afraid becomes free of the fear of dying from poison. "Ray" means rays.

The cow dung that has not fallen to the ground is that of a tawny cow. With it one should make the birds, a male myna and a female myna. The white vessel is one made of ground white rice, and the two birds should be placed in it. There, in a white vessel such as a conch, one places ink made by kneading charcoal from a burned corpse, and with it, using a pen of white karavīra, one should write the myna mantra. "As male and female": the female is the female myna, and her male is the myna. This is a specification; therefore it teaches that the two leaves of birch bark or the like, on which the mantra is written with that ink, are placed at the heart of the male myna and at the heart of the female myna.

In the passage beginning "Ink should be applied," one smears and draws on the two mynas with that ink, places them facing one's own eyes, and looks at them as though they were two real mynas. Reciting the mantra seven times for each with a leaf of white karavīra, one visualizes them as inseparable from the two divine mynas who have been summoned. From oṃ through to svāhā is a single mantra. "Entered" means that those two mynas went and entered the abode of the nāgas. The passage beginning "by which" refers to the nāga who is the seizer. Having seen the mynas of great power, even if it does not die, it does not become blind. "To the śālikās" means to the two śālikās. "Petition" means petitions. "Word" is the word promised to Citrasenā: "We shall not act otherwise." Some versions read "do not do." In that case the meaning is: ride upon the clouds, but by the word through which the king of the garuḍas was made to act so as not to harm the nāgas, he gives command to all the mighty birds; therefore, "Do not make that word otherwise."

The passage beginning "thus": the male myna and the female śālikā take hold of the blind snake by its head and tail and carry it before the seized person. With earth from a snake's hole, consecrated with the summoning mantra, the snake is sprinkled, preceded by recitation of the summoning mantra. Its eyes appear, and, seeing the seized person, it goes three times from his feet to the top of his head and from his head to his feet. Then, when the seized person rises, the two śālikās carry the snake away to its own abode; that is, they make it remain always in its own abode. This is the general meaning. The meaning of the parts: "of the seized" means the seized. "By the snake" means the snake. "Of the dead and fallen" means one who has died and fallen. "Going" is up to the top of the head. "Coming" is down to the feet.

Having taught the conventional method of curing poison, the action of the mynas, he teaches the ultimate: "the wisdom of emptiness." He explains this very point in detail in the passage beginning "empty." "Empty" means without intrinsic nature, free of all the stains of conceptual thought. "All" means things whose nature is outer and inner. Having removed the superimposition of existence, he rejects the superimposition of nonexistence: "Dharma," the nature of reality of things, emptiness, does not exist, because it is free of elaboration.

The passage beginning "for that long" means for as long as twenty-one days. What need is there to say more? As long as the face and so on of the seized person do not open, that is, do not stay open, for that long one should perform the work of the yantra. The conclusion is the passage beginning "thus": having thus taken him into one's care by the rite culminating in giving him the poison water to drink, one should explain the method of pacifying poison, the action of the mynas, and the ultimate. With the yoga of Buddhakapāla one should eat poison consecrated twenty-one times with this: oṃ buddha kapālinī āḥ hūṃ.

"In an outer place" means at the site of the maṇḍala, after requesting the deities of the maṇḍala to depart. "By this" means by what has already been stated: one should worship the ninefold maṇḍala of colored powder, as before. Or else the construction is: the steadfast practitioner, in the nature of Heruka, should worship in due order with these offering mantras already stated. "Maṇḍala" means one made with sandalwood and the like. "White-bodied" means endowed with a host of white qualities. "Fire destroyed" means without a head; that this is within the place of bliss is the oral instruction. "The mouth of the vessel" is the mouth of the vase. "Moon" means the moon that has melted. "Risen" means "causes to rise."

This is the fourth chapter in the Fearless Commentary, the commentary on the great Buddhakapāla Tantra composed by the learned Abhayākaragupta.

To teach the elixir practice in detail, he speaks the passage beginning "then." For "for six months," the words "it should be administered through the nose" are to be supplied. The "crow" consort is one whose form and color are like a crow's: her body is black. The "swan" is a consort whose form is white like a particular kind of swan. Ūṣmā is a consort whose "form is warmth," that is, blue mixed with white. Some say that she is blue in color, and that "warm form" means that her body is always warm. These are the three kinds of consort. Of these, the menstrual blood of the crow woman should be taken when it occurs in the dark fortnight; that of the swan woman when it occurs in the bright fortnight; and that of Ūṣmā when it occurs in either fortnight.

He gives the characteristics of Kumārī and the others. "Blue-green" is the crow woman, distinguished by blue eyes. From such a woman of eighteen years come three things: the substance that is the discharge of her eyes; the awakening mind of both partners, born from the union in which she is properly joined with the method partner, as the words "joined in union" say; and her menstrual blood. These three are called Kumārī. Because they arise in those three consorts, it says "arisen there." If one asks, "Of what in them is it?", he says, "of the channels," that is, of the Avadhūtī and the others. "Lotus-born" is the substance born in the source of phenomena, the awakening mind. If one asks, "Where in it does it abide?", he says, "in the anthers," that is, where the channels gather. If one asks what is special about it: "in the hidden domain." The hidden domain is the place where the substance abides; the meaning is that it arises from the navel and abides in the anthers.

The meaning of this is as follows. The awakening mind of both partners born from union with any one of the three consorts who has reached twelve years, and her first menstrual blood: these two are Śālikā. In the passage beginning "possessing flowers," the awakening mind of both arising from union with a crow woman of sixteen years who is menstruating is Siddhā. "Undivided enjoyment" means equal conduct. "Woman" means any one of the three consorts. "Undamaged" means not approached by another man; the meaning is that her womb is intact. "First arisen from her": the menstrual blood arising on the first day of the menses of that woman of twenty years, and the awakening mind born from union with her at that very time: these two are She of the Child. In the passage beginning "innate," the fluid of both partners from union with any one of the three consorts aged twenty-five, and the oil arising from the base of the skull of a dead woman: these two are Kapālinī.

These five medicines, together with purified mercury and ratire, should be applied in the morning for periods of six months, three, one, a half, two and one respectively, as will be explained. Of these, the crow-woman medicine should be put into the nose at the time when the breath flows through the left nostril; the swan-woman medicine through the right; and Ūṣmā through both. This he states in the passage beginning "moon." Here "moon" is the wind of Lalanā and "sun" is the wind of Rasanā. Or, "moon" is semen and "sun" is menstrual blood: if semen predominates in the medicine, then it goes into the left nostril; if menstrual blood predominates, then through the right.

The passage beginning "then from this" is easy to understand. "Supreme of yoginīs": the five nectars are the yoginīs, and the supreme yogins are the five lamps, that is, the method. For "eating," "able" is to be supplied. "Concept" means concepts. The hyperbolic statement beginning "the Tathāgatas do not understand it" is made in order to generate respect. If one asks, "Then who does understand it?", he speaks the passage beginning "devoted to the guru": those who, by meditating on emptiness that transcends the extremes of permanence and the rest, are free of attachment and the other afflictions. For that very reason, "having abandoned all afflictions" means that they have overpowered these, and are not attached to food and the like. "By wisdom, method and so on" means by the five sense pleasures, touch and the rest. "By one side" means one-sidedly; the meaning is that accomplishment comes from meditating on reality, with unsurpassed wisdom and method in a single essence, both in meditative equipoise and outside it.

So that one may devote oneself solely to meditating on reality, he teaches the abandonment of attachment to objects in the passage beginning "O sir." "Nonsense" means what is contradictory. What is that? Being harmed by the senses and utterly attached to objects. "These beings" are people who are attached. "Hā hā" expresses sorrow. "Nonsense" is the mind's distraction toward eating, drinking and so on, other than meditation on reality. "Shadow" means reflection. "Lords of the innate": the five Tathāgatas, Vairocana and the others, whose nature is the five wisdoms, the mirror-like and the rest, are accomplished; the meaning is that the yogin comes to have their nature.

This is the fifth chapter in the Fearless Commentary.

So that it may be understood that after empowerment one should clarify reality; that empowerment is preceded by a maṇḍala; and that a disciple of the highest faculties should then be empowered in the maṇḍala whose nature is mind, he speaks the passage beginning "then." It is "terrifying" because it terrifies those attached to the outer maṇḍala. It is "excellent" because it is a source of joy for those of sharp faculties; it is of the nature of mind. It is "supreme" because it yields the fruit of what terrifies. "By which" means by the maṇḍala. "The practice of wisdom and method" means the practice of wisdom and of method: one practices in accordance with it. "The end" is the tip of the bird face, being joined and connected with it; that is, it is joined at the end. The outer maṇḍala is the maṇḍala of colored powder. "By merely destroying the deity" should be understood as: by carelessly destroying the colored powder without having asked the deities to depart. Otherwise it would contradict what comes before and after, and the other tantras.

For that very reason, since destroying the deity is possible, it applies to the deities of colored powder, because they are grasped in terms of apprehended and apprehender. The intended meaning is that it does not exist for what has the nature of nondual wisdom, but does exist for the maṇḍala that is mental in nature. To show that, just as with destroying the deity, destroying its emblem is also a sin, the text says "vajra" and so on: these are the emblems of the lords of the families, Akṣobhya and the rest. Again, from "mind" one should understand the seed syllables, and those of the other deities as well. "Having gone" means going. Ultimately, however, the text says "the buddhas" and so on: "by what" means by nothing whatsoever. Inviting and the rest are taught on account of the inclinations of inferior beings. "Anywhere" means, ultimately, not anywhere at all. To show this, the noble Perfection of Wisdom says: "Son of good family, the tathāgatas neither come from anywhere nor go anywhere. Suchness is unmoving, and that is the Tathāgata."

"Stainless" means those without stains, because they are free from attachment and the rest. Because they are free from the obscuration to the knowable, they are pure: the dharmakāyas. "Others" means this: one should not hold that, apart from the unobscured tathāgatas who are freed from the elaborations of the colored-powder maṇḍala, of departing and so on, there exist other tathāgatas for whom inviting and the rest would be ultimately possible. If one asks what the authority for this is, the text says "here" and so on.

If one asks what kind of wisdom woman it is in whose lotus the maṇḍala comes to be, the text says "twelve years old" and so on. "Showing the moon" is the gathered awakening mind. "Unwavering" means not wavering, up to "does not go to another man." As for the disciples who offer such a wisdom woman, "these alone" means those endowed with the qualities that will be explained. "Reality" is the fourth. "Five" means the water, the crown, the vajra, the lord, and the name. The mantra, the vajra observance, the conduct observance, the prophecy, the irreversible, and the secret, together with the observance of the vidyā, are seven. The two are the wisdom gnosis and the fourth. "Gathered into one" means that when these are combined they become the fourteen empowerments. Among them, those from the water up to the irreversible are to be called the master empowerment. Reality together with the observance of the vidyā is the outer. "Secret" is the secret empowerment. "The reality of the secret" is the wisdom empowerment. "Reality" is the fourth empowerment. Thus too, including the observance of the vidyā within the master empowerment or the wisdom empowerment as a branch of the one or the other, it is said:

Master, secret, wisdom,
And the fourth likewise.

"What is to be offered" is the gold and so on that the disciple offers to the guru. "To the guru" means the guru. "Not speaking" means being intent on meditative absorption. That all this is merely indicative and not explicitly complete, the compiler states with "this" and so on.

Here the oral instruction is this. Here the master meditates on himself as glorious Heruka, surrounded by the twenty-four goddesses, and likewise on the disciple too as glorious Heruka. Into the center of the lotus ground of the embracing action seal, in the form of Citrasenā, purified according to the rite, he invites the Blessed One who embodies the twenty-five, by means of the mass of light rays from the seed syllable at his own heart, and makes him enter through the upper opening of the Avadhūtī; and the particles of awakening mind, now melted, are brought down by way of the vajra path. By this, in an instant, the maṇḍala embodying the twenty-five deities together with the palace, mental in nature and threefold in its tiers, is accomplished. The disciple makes five maṇḍala offerings and supplicates three times:

Into the supreme city of great liberation,
Protector, I pray, let me enter.

Having mentally drawn him into his own body and brought him out by way of the vajra path, as he sits at the eastern door of the maṇḍala, the master says:

Buddha self, pure by nature,
Body by nature free of stain,
Sovereign city of heroes and heroines:
Son, behold the maṇḍala!

and so shows him the maṇḍala. Then, when he has supplicated with "I, sunk in the mire of saṃsāra" and so on, Caṇḍikā washes him with the nectar of melted awakening mind; and the tathāgatas and the vidyā goddesses, summoned by the light rays of the seed syllable at the heart, enter through the upper opening, melt, and are brought out by way of the vajra path. With that water of awakening mind, which has the nature of Akṣobhya, the master says:

The empowerment of all the buddhas,
Arisen from the abode of the three secrets,
Honored by all the three realms:
I shall bestow the great vajra.

and one should empower with the mantra oṃ śrī cakra vīra stvāṃ abhiṣiñcāmi. This is the water empowerment. Then one meditates on that water as a crown embodying Ratnasambhava and should say abhiṣiñca tumaṃ sarva yoginyo mukuṭe atra adhitiṣṭhantu āgacchantu hūṃ svāhā. This is the crown. With the right hand, at the heart, he has him hold the vajra that embodies Amitābha, saying:

Glorious Heruka and the other heroes
Today confer empowerment on you.
This is all the buddhas themselves:
Hold the vajra for perfect accomplishment.

This is the vajra. He has him hold at the heart, with the left hand, the bell that has the nature of Amoghasiddhi, saying:

This, by all the buddhas,
Is declared the bell that follows sound.
You too must always hold it;

Having conferred empowerment by the rite as stated,

This explains "the fourth empowerment."

For a disciple of middling faculties, one should visualize, upon the nature of wisdom gnosis visualized externally, within the lotus of the source of phenomena, the maṇḍala that is mental in nature, threefold in its tiers, and measuring a koṭi of yojanas, and should confer empowerment by the rite described, as is fitting. One who has gathered merit and clearly sees these two maṇḍalas as the emanation of a guru able to generate them is of the very highest faculties, and he too should be empowered in this way. For one of dull faculties, it is done in a maṇḍala of colored powder or a maṇḍala painted on cloth. One should act having distinguished the instructions in this way, for fear that the instructions may degenerate.

Having taught the maṇḍala on the side of the generation stage, the text, in order to speak of the maṇḍala of completion, says "why" and so on. "Why" refutes, ultimately, a support. "What" refutes the supported, the essence of the deity. Although erecting a maṇḍala and so on is the path to great awakening, because it accomplishes merit, even so it is distant and empty of qualities such as the superknowledges; to say this, the text says "fall" and so on. "In the false" means in what is false in nature, superimposed by conceptual thought. "Idle chatter": with loving concern he says that beings, through distractions of various kinds, are cut off from the path that swiftly accomplishes perfect awakening, and fall.

If one asks, "What then is not superimposed?" the text says "empty" and so on. "All" means the aggregates, the elements, and so on. Such a maṇḍala is the completion maṇḍala. If one asks how, the text says "lotus" and so on: it abides in the center of the lotuses of great bliss and the rest. The host of yoginīs is the nature of the channels. Heruka is the awakening mind. Reality, whose very being is great bliss, of one taste with emptiness, with a nature like a dream, a reflection, an illusion and so on, arises in the form of the aggregates and the rest; to say this, the text says "producer of all." Therefore, "meditate on and behold this completion maṇḍala that is to be taken up." As Ghaṇṭāpāda says:

The lines laid down where the mind abides,
And the stages of spreading colored powder, arise;
Yet this world is itself the maṇḍala,
Established by nature, nondual.
Whatever has the nature of the two contrived ones
Is held to be for the sake of those to be trained.
It is not what seekers of liberation must accomplish:
Liberation comes from seeing the true meaning.

If one objects, "If it is complete in this way, then it is present at all times, so what is the use of meditation?" the text says "by harm." Harm is error, that is, delusion about the aggregates and the rest, whose nature is that of a dream. Therefore, "look": investigate decisively your delusion about what is to be adopted and what is to be rejected. "Swiftly" means quickly, that is, for as long as this perfect leisure and endowment, which cannot be relied upon, remains; this is to be connected with meditation.

The sixth chapter of the Abhayapaddhati.

Now, in order to speak of the meditation on the Blessed One, the tantra of the result, the text first teaches the outer maṇḍala for the sake of the people to be trained: "Then" and so on. "Mountain" and so on mean just what the words say. On "round" and so on, the procedure is this. The ground of the maṇḍala is thoroughly purified according to the rite, or is purified by touching it with the hand and reciting the earth-purifying mantra one hundred and eight times. By the method I have explained in the Introduction to the Kālacakra, the directions and intermediate directions are determined by the shadow of the gnomon and by the fish figures, and the torma is given. Then the master, in the form of the deity, first faces east. He takes the marking cord: twenty-five threads, visualized as the five tathāgatas each embodying the five wisdoms, twisted together by the master, as thick as a barley grain, new, and longer than twice the maṇḍala by a span. Holding it at the center with the vajra fist of his left hand, together with the disciple in the form of the deity, he strikes in the sky the two Brahmā lines called the lines of wisdom, and then below, on the ground, strikes the Brahmā line straight in the same way.

Then, in order to make the maṇḍala square, he strikes the second Brahmā line over the mouth and tail of the fish, facing north. Then he strikes the two diagonal lines according to the rite. Then come the four root lines of a maṇḍala measuring one cubit or more. Then, going outward from the two Brahmā lines toward the east and the other directions, leaving one line at the measure of the colored-powder ground, there are four lines. Then again one line. After that, leaving two lines, there is one line for the deity band. Thus, counting from the Brahmā line and including the root line, there are ten lines on one side, and on the other sides too it is in the same order.

Then, outward from the root line, there is one line for the colored-powder ground, at the measure of a quarter of the door, which is an eighth of the wheel. Then one line for the deity platform, at the measure of half the door. Then one line for the jewel band, at the measure of the colored ground. Then one line for the band of nets and half nets, at the measure of a band. Then one line, at the measure of the colored ground, for the bakulī band, or, in some traditions, once again the jewel band. Then one short line for the band of darkness, and on that very line are the parapets. Then, for fear of the trouble of erasing, whether with short lines or with long ones, there are the four lines of the archways, three times the door. Then, leaving two lines, one line connected with the archways. Then, leaving three lines, one line for the place of the banners stirred by the wind and hung with bells, the victory banners, the parasols, the wish-fulfilling trees, and so on. Again, leaving one line, one line. Then one line for the garland of lotus petals. For that very reason, the palace stands on the hub of the crossed vajra that lies within the center of the lotus. Outside that hub, on the center of the lotus, one draws the crossed-vajra fence, the archways, and so on. Then one line for the vajra garland. Then, leaving two lines, one line for the garland of light rays.

Some say that there is no garland of lotus petals. Koṅkaṇa says that there are four lines: those of the vajra garland, the lotus garland, the wheel garland, and the garland of light rays. Thus, beginning from the line of the colored ground, there are sixteen lines on one side. "Likewise on the other sides too": this is the rite for laying down the twenty-six lines in a maṇḍala of two tiers. In just this way one should lay down the lines in maṇḍalas of three tiers and so on. By laying out the lines thus on a single occasion, one should understand the layout of maṇḍalas that gather nine, thirteen, fifteen, nineteen, thirty-two, or thirty-seven deities, and so on up to a hundred thousand and more. Here the particulars of the inner lines in the archways and elsewhere, the purifying of the ground and so on, the erasing, the spreading of the colors and the rest, we have explained at length in our Maṇḍala Rite.

"Encircled by three vajras" means encircled by three vajra garlands, blue, red, and white. "In an instant": because the yoginīs and the Blessed One, twenty-five in all, are generated from the single syllable hūṃ alone. "Emitted there" means from the syllable hūṃ at the time the Blessed One is generated. "Adorned with five colors" is the supporting maṇḍala. "Having the self of hūṃ" is said of whatever has a self born from the syllable hūṃ. "Black, white, and green" are those dwelling in the central, the second, and the third tiers, in that order. The gatekeeper goddesses are black, like those dwelling in the central tier. "Crowned with Akṣobhya" refers to the principal himself, Akṣobhya, dwelling in the center; and it is also said that he is adorned with a crown of the five buddhas. "All the yoginīs" means the twenty-five. "Their form": just as the Blessed One wears an ornament on his head, so they too are sealed. Yet the tathāgata dwelling on the crown of each of them is their own respective lord; to say this, the text says "lord" and so on. "Should be desired" means one causes them to desire. The yoginīs are the four, Sumālinī and the rest. Some gurus say they are the gatekeeper goddesses.

"Become dispirited" means appearing dispirited to others, because he has reached the very culmination of emptiness alone. "Eye" means one who has vowed to bring beings out. "Reminder" means one who brings to mind. "O Lord" means lord of all. "The deed" means having done only what serves oneself. "Lord, formerly" and so on means: "Lord, remember how you vowed before." "Dispirited" means despondent, because of being afflicted by the sufferings of saṃsāra. "Wrong" means in wrong conceptions, that is, in the extreme of existence and the extreme of nonexistence. "Pride" means overweening pride. "Delight" means grasping. To say that in this way one comes to grasp at things, the text says "see" and so on. The meaning is this: through desire for the host of yoginīs there arises great bliss of one taste with emptiness, and from that the supreme grasping at things, non-things and so on is exhausted.

"Faces, hand emblems and so on as before" means like the causal Vajradhara. As for the body color, in other tantras the resultant Vajradhara is black and the causal Vajradhara white. Here, however, it is blue alone. Some read "body color, hand emblems and so on as before," and then it is clear that the causal Vajradhara too is blue. "Arises" means together with Citrasenā.

If one asks, "Then, just as the causal Vajradhara was instructed to be the transformation of the moon, the sun and the rest, which have the nature of mirror-like wisdom and the others, why is it not so here too?" it is not so, because he is born from the syllable hūṃ together with the vajra born from the hūṃ that arose from the melting. If one asks, "Does that resultant Vajradhara then have the nature of the five wisdoms?" not in that way, but because the utterly purified ones arise as his very essence. Thus: that in which all beings appear distinctly, having become reflections as in a mirror, is mirror-like wisdom. The wisdom of equality is that by which one realizes as equal the beings who have thus become reflections. Discriminating wisdom is that by which, from within that very equipoise, one discerns individually the mental activities of all sentient beings. The wisdom that accomplishes activities is that by which one accomplishes, in accordance with them, the activities of the beings thus discerned. The wisdom of the utterly pure dharmadhātu is the complete realization of the dharmadhātu, freed from the two obscurations together with their habitual tendencies, whose nature is great bliss, compassion and wisdom nondual. Hence the resultant Vajradhara too has the nature of the five wisdoms.

The beginner meditates on these wisdoms through firm conviction, with these thoughts: "The mind of all beings, utterly clear, is just like the moon. When mirror, moon and sun come together, the mind whose nature is great bliss sees all phenomena as like reflections and yet does not see them as separate; it is devoted to ascertaining the welfare of beings; and it accomplishes their welfare through countless emanations. Thus has it become clear." In the completion, one becomes familiar with this by reflecting on its meaning with certainty. Enough said.

This is the stage of meditation here, according to the oral instruction. Here the mantra practitioner who has taken up the meditation, awakened by the song of the four goddesses, rises early and, with hūṃ phaṭ or oṃ āḥ hūṃ, protects the place, himself, and the yoga. Then, in a charnel ground or some other place agreeable to the mind, he sits on a comfortable seat in the vajra posture, with a wisdom woman or without one, naked and with hair unbound. For all rites he faces south; for the distinctions of the four rites and so on, he faces the direction appropriate to each. Having first relied on the pledge substances, he prepares the nectar by the procedure that will be explained, and gives the torma.

Then, by the mass of light rays of the syllable hūṃ abiding on a sun disk at his own heart, he invites into the sky before him the Blessed One Heruka dwelling in Akaniṣṭha, embraced by his innate Citrasenā, surrounded by the twenty-four vajra dancing goddesses, Sumālinī and the rest, facing himself, with the body color and other features that will be explained. Immediately after those light rays have entered the syllable hūṃ, he should worship him with the vajra goddesses who arise all at once from the syllable hūṃ.

Of these, to the east of the Blessed One, Vajra Steadfast One offers dust to the principal with a skull cup held in both hands in the manner of a lotus. In the northeast, Vajradūtī sounds a ḍamaru with her two hands. In the southeast, Vajravārāhī, with a skull cup held in the manner of a lotus, has him partake of Vairocana. In the southwest, Vajrā, holding it in the same way, offers a vessel of offering water filled with vajra water. In the south, Vajra Fanged One beats a great drum with her hands. In the northwest, Vajranābhī, holding it in the same way, offers with a skull cup the one marked by the deer. In the west, Vajrabhīmā, holding it in the same way, has him partake, from a skull cup, of jackal flesh, widely known as "the cutter." In the north, Rāgasenā, having become one with him, embraces him perfectly and delights him with great passion.

Having worshiped perfectly with dances, songs and so on as well, one prostrates and performs the confession of wrongdoing and the rest as follows:

I confess each and every wrongdoing of my own, and shall not commit them again. I rejoice in all the merit of all the buddhas, bodhisattvas, noble ones, and ordinary beings. I dedicate all my own happiness to unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. Until awakening I go for refuge to the Three Jewels, namely the Buddha Jewel, the Dharma Jewel which is the Perfection of Wisdom, and the Saṅgha Jewel which is the noble assembly of irreversible bodhisattvas. Ah! For the sake of the aim, the benefit and the happiness of all beings, and in order to establish them in the unsurpassed nirvāṇa that is utterly final, the wisdom of the Tathāgata, I myself, by accomplishing its path, shall fully awaken to unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening.

After that, by the light rays of the syllable hūṃ at the heart, embraced by Caṇḍikā blazing as she is stirred by the lower wind, he consumes all the elements without exception; and with oṃ śūnyatā jñāna vajra svabhāva ātmako'haṃ, making manifest the meaning of this, which has first been inwardly recited, he places all phenomena in emptiness.

After that, by the force of his former aspiration, a sun arises from the seed raṃ, and upon it the syllable hūṃ transforms into a crossed vajra. With branches of light rays stirred by the hūṃ syllables abiding at its base, he makes the vajra fence, the tent, the net of arrows, the canopy, and the sealing of the boundaries. Below is the vajra ground. Above that emanated crossed vajra, within a white triangular source of phenomena arisen from the syllable ā, on the hub of a crossed vajra abiding at the center of a lotus, there is a blue wind maṇḍala arisen from yaṃ, shaped like a bow and bearing two banners. Upon it, from the transformation of the syllable raṃ, there is a red triangular fire maṇḍala, blazing and radiating seven flames. Upon that there is a water maṇḍala arisen from vaṃ, white and round, marked with a vase. Upon that, from the transformation of the syllable laṃ, there is a square maṇḍala marked with vajras, golden in nature and lovely.

Thus, in the palace formed from the blending of these four elements, each blessed by its own seed, there is a round structure of three tiers, square on the outside, with four doors, adorned with four archways, with necklaces and so on, having four platforms filled with the radiance of the goddesses, made of every kind of jewel, and adorned with pillars and little bells. At its center, upon a black wheel with eight spokes, at the center of a multicolored lotus with eight petals, is a corpse whose nature is the dharmadhātu. Upon it, at the heart, on the sun, the vowels, doubled and transformed, become the form of the moon, the cause of the thirty-two marks, having the nature of mirror-like wisdom. The consonants, doubled together with ya ra la va ḍa ḍha and transformed, become the sun, the cause of the eighty minor marks, whose essence is the wisdom of equality. Between them is the syllable hūṃ abiding at the heart of a five-pronged vajra born from hūṃ, embodying wisdom and method, whose nature is discriminating wisdom. From it alone five kinds of light rays issue, and the deities thus emanated work everywhere for the welfare of beings; this has the nature of the wisdom that accomplishes activities.

From the transformation of this moon and the rest, one should meditate on the causal Vajradhara of the Blessed One Buddhakapāla, whose nature is the utterly pure dharmadhātu: with a body like a freshly opened plantain flower, or, by the method of other tantras, radiant and white as jasmine; with arms and other features like those of the resultant Vajradhara he will come to bear; embraced by red Citrasenā; surrounded by the twenty-four goddesses, Sumālinī and the rest, who are accomplished from the syllable hūṃ at the very moment the Blessed One is accomplished.

Some of my gurus say that at this point, too, the Blessed One partakes of the five nectars in the skull cup offered to him by the twenty-four goddesses.

After that, one should meditate on these syllables, oṃ āḥ hūṃ svāhā, in order, at Citrasenā's head, throat, heart, secret place and thighs. As they radiate out between one another, they pervade her body. Then one should meditate that at the center of a red lotus arisen from the syllable hrīḥ there is a red syllable āḥ, and that the Blessed One's vajra is born from a black syllable hūṃ, its tip blessed by a white syllable oṃ. The twenty-four goddesses, indivisible from Citrasenā, make love to the Blessed One through the union of vajra and lotus in this way, and so too does the Blessed One. As he arouses joy with the mantra oṃ sarvatathāgata anurāgaṇa vajra svabhāva ātmako'haṃ, through great passion he melts, together with the Blessed Lady, upon the sun. This is the first samādhi, called the First Union.

Then, in keeping with his former aspiration, the four goddesses emanated from the melted drop, tormented at not seeing their lord, implore the Protector with songs. Sumālinī, whose nature is great loving-kindness, does so with this song:

Why have you become disheartened?
What has become of the Protector of the World?
If you remember your word,
Lord of all, arise!

Kapālinī, the embodiment of great compassion, does so with this:

Having done what you yourself must do,
Do not set your mind on emptiness.
All beings have fallen into the terrors of existence;
Friend of the Yoginī, arise!

Bhīmā, whose nature is great joy, does so with this:

Lord, remember what you spoke before;
Do not grow weary of your task.
If you vanish, all people
Will fall, and beings will lose heart.

Immediately after being urged in this way, from the syllable hūṃ that arose from the melted drop, the hūṃ together with a vajra born from hūṃ wholly transforms into the Blessed One Heruka. He is blue in color, with one face and four arms. With his two right hands he holds a ḍamaru drum and a curved knife, and with his left a khaṭvāṅga and a skull cup. He stands on a sun disc resting on the breast of a corpse lying on a multicolored lotus. Bound in the half cross-legged posture, he dances with the nine moods of the dance, grace and the rest. From his neck hangs a garland of fifty fresh human heads, and he wears a sacred thread of fresh human entrails. Behind he wears a tiger skin, while in front he is naked, and he bears the wheel. He is adorned with earrings, necklace, armlets, bracelets, a hanging girdle, anklets, and the sound of bells. He is crowned with the five buddhas, and his topknot of matted locks is marked with a crossed vajra and a half moon. A garland of five skulls is bound at the edge of his brow. He is adorned with the ashes of burnt corpses. His body is greatly fierce with feigned wrath. He has three eyes like the disc of the rising sun. By proclaiming the syllable hūṃ he threatens the onslaught of the arrogant hosts of Māra and grinds them to dust. With supreme loving-kindness and great compassion he draws out embodied beings whose bodies are sunk in the depths of the ocean of saṃsāra. The yogin should meditate on him while holding the breath within.

This lord's beloved, Citrasenā, is born from the syllable hūṃ at the very same moment as the Blessed One. She is unclothed, with bone ornaments, but without the garland of human heads. Her mass of hair is loose, reddish yellow and blazing upward, and she is crowned with bandhūka flowers. Her body is playful with grace and movement. She has one face, slightly baring the tips of her fangs, and three eyes. With her left arm she amorously embraces the lord's neck, and she offers the lord the stream of flowers, poured into a skull cup, that flows from the opening of her innate lotus. As if striking the hosts who dwell in the three realms of existence and have strayed from the good path, she holds her right hand, with the curved knife, raised straight upward. With the sound śīt she gives him the nectar of her lower lip and the rest, and, being highly skilled in the arts and in amorous play, her fluid flows, and she makes the lord's mind utterly tender and impassioned.

At the moment this father and mother are accomplished, the vajra dancing goddesses, Sumālinī and the rest, are accomplished. Of these, on the eastern petal of the Blessed One's seat is Sumālinī, on the northern Kapālinī, on the western Bhīmā, and on the southern Durjayā. On the petals of the intermediate directions, beginning with Īśāna's in the northeast and going clockwise, are skull cups filled with semen, with blood, with the five nectars, and with the five lamps. This is the first circle: an eight-petaled lotus encircled by a garland of blue vajras.

Next is the second circle: eight lotuses resting on eight red spokes, encircled by a garland of red vajras that stands out clearly on the outside like a palisade. On its eastern spoke is Śubhamekhalā, on the northern Rūpiṇī, on the western Vijayā, and on the southern Kāriṇī. In the Īśāna quarter is Kālinī, in the Agni quarter the Lady of the Great Ocean, in the Nairṛti quarter Kāriṇī, and in the Vāyu quarter the Slayer.

Next is the third circle: eight lotuses resting on eight white spokes, encircled by a garland of white vajras. On its eastern spoke is Vajratāriṇī, on the northern Bhīmadarśanā, on the western Sudarśanā, and on the southern Ajitā. In the Īśāna quarter is Śubhā, in the Agni quarter the Suppressor of Shadows, in the Nairṛti quarter Kālarātrī, and in the Vāyu quarter the Greatly Renowned One.

Then, on a lotus at the eastern gate is Sundarī, on a lotus at the northern Vasundharā, on a lotus at the western Subhagā, and on a lotus at the southern Priyadarśanā.

All of these hold a curved knife and a skull cup in the branches of their right and left arms. Thinking of their lord, their lips quiver and they show their teeth a little. Each has one face, is clothed only in the directions, wears bone ornaments, and is without the garland of heads. Their masses of hair are loose, reddish yellow and blazing upward. From intense passion their fluid flows. They dance in the half cross-legged posture upon sun discs like freshly opened mandārava flowers. The gatekeepers stand on moons in the posture with the right leg extended.

All of them also wear skirts of tiger skin, all blaze with rays of light, streams of blood drip from their mouths, they have three eyes, they possess the whole set of moods, grace and the rest, and their bodies are the color of freshly broken sapphire.

Alternatively, the four, Sumālinī and the rest, are black; the goddesses of the second circle are whitish yellow; those who dwell in the third circle are green; and the gatekeeper goddesses are black.

Other gurus of mine say: "Sumālinī is white, Kapālinī is yellow, Bhīmā is white, and Durjayā is black. Those of the second circle are red. Those who dwell in the third circle are white. Sundarī is white, Vasundharā yellow, Subhagā red, and Priyadarśanā black. For so it is taught in the extensive tantra." One should meditate according to one's inclination, since the second position too is taught in the shorter tantra itself.

Then comes the armor: at the Blessed One's heart oṃ, at the forehead hūṃ, at the throat heṃ, at the crown of the head haiṃ, on the two shoulders hoṃ, on the two eyes hauṃ, at the secret place haṃ, and over the whole body haḥ.

After that, at the heart there is hūṃ at the center of a vajra born from a black hūṃ; at the throat there is āḥ on a lotus transformed from a red āḥ; and at the wheel of great bliss there is oṃ on a wheel born from a white syllable oṃ. Having thus blessed mind, speech and body, one draws in the wisdom circle with the rays of light of the syllable hūṃ resting on the sun and vajra at the heart. One drives away the obstructors with the syllable hūṃ, and offers the welcome water. Then, by means of the four gatekeepers, with jaḥ hūṃ baṃ hoḥ in order, one summons them, draws them in, binds them and brings them under control. Reciting oṃ buddhakapālinī āḥ khaṃ kroṃ druṃ sruṃ aṃ aṃ aṃ hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ phaṭ with a swaying gesture, one sees them become one with the pledge maṇḍala.

Then, as the fierce fire (caṇḍālī) blazes at the navel, the nectar resting in the wheel of great bliss gradually melts. At the time it washes the seed of the wheel of Dharma, the eight ḍākinīs, whose nature is the eight channels of the wheel of Dharma and whose essence is great bliss, are emanated. They are adorned with garments and other ornaments of the various kinds described for the time of offering; they are resting places of unsurpassed beauty and grace; they are twelve years old; in the branches of their hands they hold vases filled with bodhicitta, together with sprays of mango; and they shine with peaceful, smiling rays of light. They go forth outside. At that very moment, to the tathāgatas of the ten directions, together with their goddesses, invited by the rays of light of the seed at the heart, one prays:

Again and again I bow down,
And, with joined palms, I pray:
You who possess compassion,
Please confer empowerment upon me.

This prayer is made with oṃ abhiṣiñcatu māṃ sarvayoginyo makuṭe atra adhiṣṭhantu āgacchantu hūṃ svāhā. Then they, furnished with every requisite, parasols, victory banners, pennants, garments, music and the rest, their hands holding white vases filled with the water of wisdom, confer empowerment with the words "May you be victorious! Be victorious!" and so on, through the outer, inner, secret, wisdom and fourth empowerments. At the time of empowerment, too, there are shouts of acclamation, auspicious verses and the sound of great drums, and a rain of sandalwood, saffron, flowers and the like falls from the depths of the sky. After that, one should dissolve them into those channels.

Then the Blessed One and the goddesses should be sealed by the lord of their own respective families. Here the Blessed One, Vajrasattva, is sealed by the five buddhas, Akṣobhya and the rest, because it is said that his crown is adorned with the five buddhas. Citrasenā is sealed by the great Vairocana, whose other name is Vajrasattva. The four, Sumālinī and the rest, are sealed by Ratnasambhava. Those who dwell in the second circle are sealed by Amitābha. Those who dwell in the third circle are sealed by Vairocana. The gatekeepers are sealed by Amoghasiddhi.

Vajra and lotus joined, the bliss of nectar swelling,
Your form transformed by stages, you dance for beings' sake;
You sport in the playful delight of the vajra goddesses, and to deluded beings,
To every being without slight, you grant all joy: to you I bow.

The nectar should be tasted in this way. Roused by the sound of the joy of the Blessed One and the Blessed Lady, the five tathāgatas arrive, enter by the door of Vairocana, melt, and descend into the lotus. At that time, one takes for oneself, with the play of the tip of the tongue, the nectar of the five wisdoms that are one's inner nature, and one should satisfy the deities of the maṇḍala.

Outwardly, there is a blue wind maṇḍala, born from the syllable yaṃ, with yaṃ as its heart, and marked by two fluttering banners. On it is a red fire maṇḍala, arisen from raṃ, bearing raṃ, with seven blazing flames. Above that, resting on three heads born from three syllables, is a lotus vessel born from the syllable āḥ.

In its center are a half moon and a drop, together with bi ra śu ma mu and go ku da ha na, transformed into the five nectars and the five meats, along with oṃ hūṃ khaṃ sva hūṃ. Then, heated by the fire stirred up by the wind, the ten substances in the skull cup boil, and their vapor rises into the space above, a span's distance away, where it touches a blue vajra born from hūṃ and marked with hūṃ. The vajra melts and falls in, and from this everything ripens into a single taste. A span above that is a moon maṇḍala, born from oṃ and blessed by the syllable oṃ. The rays of light of its syllable oṃ draw in the bodhicitta nectars of the tathāgatas and others who dwell in the ten directions, and these enter together with the special potencies of jewels, mantras and medicines. Then all of it becomes the color of quicksilver, a nectar cool like a heap of snow. One blesses that very nectar three times with the three realities, and then, through the tube of the tongue, a single-pointed vajra born from a white hūṃ and shining with rays of light, one should satisfy oneself and the deities of the maṇḍala. This is the second samādhi, called the Supreme King of the Maṇḍala.

Then all the ḍākinīs, urged on by the Blessed One, and the clouds of bodies emanated by the Blessed One act for the welfare of sentient beings. Having brought them to attain the state of the great Vajradhara, one gathers the clouds of one's own bodies back into the root body. This is the third samādhi, called the Supreme King of Activity.

At the tip of the nose, Kosala, is Kāriṇī. These are the secondary fields, the ground called Radiant. At the mouth, Kaliṅga, is Kālinī. At the throat, Lampāka, is the Lady of the Great Ocean. These are the gathering places, the ground called Hard to Master. At the heart, Kāñcī, is Kāriṇī. At the testicles, Himālaya, is the Slayer. These are the secondary gathering places, the ground called Manifest. These are the earth goers in the wheel of speech.

At the sexual organ, Pretapurī, is Tāriṇī. At the anus, Gṛhadevatā, is Bhīmadarśanā. These are the meeting places, the ground called Gone Far. At the two thighs, Saurāṣṭra, is Sudarśanā. At the shanks, Suvarṇadvīpa, is Ajitā. These are the secondary meeting places, the ground called Immovable. At the fingers and toes, Nagara, is Śubhā. At the insteps, Sindhu, is the Suppressor of Shadows. These are the charnel grounds, the ground called Good Intelligence. At the thumbs and big toes, Maru, is Kālarātrī. At the knees, Kulutā, is the Greatly Renowned One. These are the secondary charnel grounds, the ground called Cloud of Dharma. These are the dwellers below the earth in the wheel of body.

The four, bodhicitta and the rest, are in the four wheels, great bliss and the rest. The place of the Blessed One and Citrasenā is the two centers of the lotus. The Blessed One is the embodiment of conventional bodhicitta; Citrasenā is of the nature of menstrual blood. The four, bodhicitta and the rest, and all the others as well, spread through all of these. The twenty-four goddesses, whose nature is the channels that dwell in their respective places, are perfectly embraced by the Blessed One alone, spreading through them.

By meditating on the varieties of caṇḍālī according to the instructions, according to reality and according to one's inclination, one should meditate on all of this, and on beings too, as being solely of the nature of Citrasenā and Buddhakapāla, whose essence is the inseparability of ultimate emptiness and compassion, the nature of great bliss.

If the yogin wishes to make the mind stable in order to accomplish all the accomplishments swiftly, then, having meditated on the three samādhis in condensed form, he should proceed as follows. In the opening of the sixty-four-petaled lotus of the wheel of emanation, facing upward, is a blue oṃ. In the eight-spoked wheel of Dharma, facing downward, is a blue hūṃ. In the sixteen-spoked wheel of enjoyment, facing upward, is a red oṃ. In the thirty-two-spoked wheel of great bliss, facing downward, is a white haṃ. The fire of the wind dwelling in the secret wheel is stirred up, and the blazing rays of light of the syllable aṃ, the nature of caṇḍālī, melt the three syllables, hūṃ and the rest. Inseparable from them, the syllable aṃ becomes a drop whose nature is innate joy. One should meditate on this as the hero, and, when convinced that the meditation has fully matured, one should see a single fine blazing line of light standing up from it. By its power, nectar drips down and fills it, washing the hero, and that line of light becomes cool. Then, with the mind fixed on the hero, one should keep watching for as long as it takes until the signs show that stability of mind has been attained. When stability is indicated, one imagines that the light of that line of light illuminates all the various things, and one sees everything like an emblic myrobalan fruit in the palm of the hand. All the samādhis, too, will swiftly be perfected. This is the yoga of the drop. One should also meditate on the divisions of caṇḍālī according to the instructions and according to one's inclination.

Since accomplishment comes from reciting the vidyā mantra, I shall explain recitation. When one grows weary of meditating, one should recite this root mantra: oṃ hrīḥ buddha hūṃ kapālinī. The sequence of its recitation is as follows. When the breath of the nostrils enters the drop just described, located at the navel, it is oṃ; for its coming out from the nostrils, hrīḥ; for its abiding in the drop, buddha; for the bliss, hūṃ; for its cessation, kapālini; and for its rising from the drop, aḥ. Among the recitations of the limbs, this is the vajra recitation. The subtle yoga too should be understood in this way.

oṃ buddhakapālinī āḥ hi hai hūṃ phaṭ is the heart mantra.

oṃ buddhakapālinī vajreṇāṃ hau durjaya durlakṣe hūṃ phaṭ is the near-heart mantra.

Having recited whichever of these mantras one likes, at the time of rising one gives a torma to all the bhūtas with this mantra: oṃ buddhakapāla vajre citre sucatre alaṃ bale prīṇaya sarvabhūtāna māraya sarvamārāna kīlaya sarvaduṣṭāna ucacāya sarvavighnān vitrāpaya bho bhagavati yakṣarakṣasapiśācāna śoṣaya tāḍaya saptapātālagatāna sara sara prasara prasara hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ phaṭ phaṭ. Then, having fully dedicated all this virtue for the sake of unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening and for the sake of the accomplishments one desires, and having made offerings as before, one should gather the twenty-four deities of the maṇḍala inward, according to the instructions, into the channels located in the twenty-four places, those within and the rest.

The skull cup of bodhicitta goes to the wheel of great bliss; the skull cup of blood to the wheel of emanation; the skull cup of the five nectars to the heart; and the skull cup of the five lamps to the throat.

Having thus withdrawn them inward, one should abide for as long as one wishes, through conviction, with oneself and the limitless three realms as the body of the Blessed One embraced by the Blessed Lady, great bliss of one taste with emptiness, whose nature has the character of an illusion.

At the junctures of the sessions at midday, in the evening and at midnight, one should instantly emanate the entire circle of goddesses and accomplish everything, the armor and the rest, without exception. At the time of sleeping, one should melt as before, and then sleep convinced that one is oneself of that nature, the innate nature whose essence is emptiness. When one wishes to rise, then, urged by the four goddesses as before, one rises instantly and completes everything exactly as before, leaving nothing out. In this way, until one obtains the signs of accomplishment, one should bathe every day by the rite of empowerment, and all one's eating should be done by the rite of tasting nectar.

The passage beginning "Then" teaches the limbs of method in detail. "With wood" is said because this is explained in another tantra. "With flowers" states briefly what is then taught in detail: "the five flowers" are the five flowers, the lotus and the rest, that will be explained. "With milk," being merely illustrative, means with butter and the like as well. For the inner fire offering, the five flowers are the menstrual blood of the five consorts, and the milk is the bodhicitta of the male, which, when they are in union, should be understood to be mixed with her blood. This is taught elsewhere, but it is not the subject here.

For "pacifying" and the rest, the hearth here is square, twelve finger-widths in width and depth. Above its mouth there is a ledge of four finger-widths. On it, in the four directions, are white five-pronged vajras with open prongs. Outside that, a rim four finger-widths in width and height, shaped like the mouth of a hearth and slightly reddish at the lip, goes all the way around. At the corners it is marked with three-pronged vajras, and its outer face is encircled by a garland of blue three-pronged vajras. In the center of the hearth, in a multistoried house standing in the center of a source of phenomena, is a lotus of eight finger-widths, encircled by three vajra garlands, blue, red and white. On the lotus is a five-pronged vajra with its head pointing east, about four finger-widths long and one finger-width high. On it is a lotus, in its center a sun, and on that the syllable hrīḥ. At the corners of the multistoried house are yellow nine-pronged vajras. All of these, the open-pronged vajras and the rest, may also be drawn with colored powders. This is the summary meaning.

Now I shall state the meaning of the words. "Generating oneself from the syllable hūṃ" means being convinced that one's nature is born from hūṃ, or having hūṃ as one's essence.

"The wheel" is the celestial palace. "The triangle" is the source of phenomena. "e vaṃ mā ya" means that one should visualize the wheel as the transformation of the four elements, earth and the rest, born from the four syllables. "With a hub" means distinguished by a lotus, which the hub indicates. "Twofold" is two hundred plus sixteen. "With khalūka" means with a particular kind of saffron. "Below, a lotus" means an eight-petaled lotus below the vajra. "Twelve finger-widths" is measured by the master's fingers. "There" means in the hearth. "Of each" refers to the mango and the rest. "Holding a red vajra and a skull cup" means that a red vajra and a skull cup are the hand emblems, the skull cup filled with blood. "Emptiness" means that nothing at all is drawn in the center of the hearth. The five meats are the meats of go ku da ha na. "Agni" is the Agni explained earlier. The other particulars of these rites, pacifying and the rest, are in the fire-offering rite I composed, the Jyotirmañjarī; they should be adopted after discarding whatever conflicts with the particulars explained here. All this is in conventional terms; hence the passage beginning, "Ultimately, with what does one burn?"

This is the eighth chapter in the Abhayapaddhati.

Even when both stages have become manifest, if one lacks the conduct there is no accomplishment of the great seal (mahāmudrā); therefore the conduct is taught, in the passage beginning "Then." Conduct is a special activity of body, speech and mind. Because it transcends the extremes of permanence, annihilation and the rest, it is superior to other kinds of conduct, and so it is "most supreme." Because it transcends the world, it is "sprung forth." In some versions one reads "a woman who is most supreme"; in that case "having taken her" is to be supplied.

That this is not to be done carelessly, out of excitement and distraction, is stated in the passage beginning "the eight accomplishments." These are the accomplishments of the underworld, eye ointment, foot ointment, the pill, invisibility, the sword, the corpse and the elixir; or else the eight qualities of mastery. This shows that when one has gained the capacity and so wishes, the conduct is permitted; by means of it this one who delights in yoga does not become heedless, those who seek an opening cannot get the better of him, he becomes able both to subdue them and to take them into his care, and he is not overwhelmed by cold, heat and the like. Because he is not harmed by the eight worldly concerns and so on, he is "free from all obscurations." For he "has become equal to space," that is, all phenomena are of one taste in sameness. "Delighting in emptiness" means that when one is begging alms and the givers refuse, saying "There is none," one should meditate on emptiness, the ultimate nature of all phenomena. "Meditating" means meditating on method. "Unsurpassed" is the inseparability of wisdom and method. "Supremely devoted to the Dharma" means applying oneself without interruption to that nondual dharma. In the passage beginning "substances," the sense is this: being satisfied with whatever food is gathered, through particular familiarization in places, times and circumstances as one wishes, one's yoga becomes uninterrupted; and so, just as a lamp's wick dispels the darkness of a cave gathered over a long time, one dispels the great mass of the faults of conceptuality, though it too has gathered over a long time, and actualizes the great seal. This is the general meaning.

In "of one endowed with the yoga of conduct," the genitive stands in the sense of the nominative. "In the tantra" means in the Buddhakapāla Tantra of twelve thousand lines. Regarding "pure by nature," someone may ask: "If beings are pure by nature, then everyone would at all times attain the great seal without effort, so what is the use of familiarizing oneself with yoga?" Has it not been said that this purity is impaired by conceptuality? For that very reason Jñānapāda said:

Apart from the workings of ordinary conceptual thought,
there is no suffering of existence at all.
What is opposed to that
is brought about here, first, by this very mind.

This is the ninth chapter in the Abhayapaddhati.

For one intent on caring for others, the passage beginning "Then" is spoken in order to explain the means for doing so. "Wholly settled on the sole benefit of beings" means that in which the sole benefit of beings is to be well established. "Makes gold and makes it brilliant" means it makes gold. "Of coral" means coral. "By which" means by gold. With wealth of pearls and the like, one "goes," that is, becomes, wealthy. What was taught in brief, that here there are none of the external substances for that gold which are explained in other tantras, is now taught in detail in the passage beginning "husk and bark." "Of other metals" means other metals. "Yet" refers to other accomplished medicines. "Of silver" means silver. "What are they?" means what is that supreme great medicine. "Accomplished by recitation" describes that which has a mantra accomplished by recitation, that is, by inner utterance. "That too" means that too is accomplished. "In the vessel" means in the lotus of the wisdom partner. "Food" is the medicine. "Essence" is the awakening mind. "In the center" means of the lotus. Since it leads the taste down from its upper place, "she who holds the essence of taste" is the avadhūtī, and it abides there. "The young woman" is one in her prime, sixteen years old. "She too" means that woman too. "Tathāgatas" are the yoginīs of Buddhakapāla. "Without what causes nausea" means without such things; nausea may arise from her being in her menses. "Is not accomplished" carries the intent that the fluids of wisdom and method emitted at the same moment are the cause of accomplishment. This connects with "the place of guidance of the buddhas." By holding it for a short time, the simultaneous emission accomplishes the great medicine, that is, gold; by holding it without interruption, it is buddhahood itself, whose nature is great bliss. This is the essential meaning.

"The steadfast one should urge it on" means one should produce that great medicine. "The moon" is semen. "Smeared" means with blood as well. "There" means on the moon. "Meru" is the syllable suṃ. "The act" is union. "To be inserted" means to be propelled. "The substance" is the awakening mind. Left and right are the two channels. "Contracting" is a particular action; contracting is taught as a method for holding back a swift emission. Even so, it should be learned clearly from the oral instructions.

In this way, having drawn the life wind and the downward-clearing wind together at the navel, and having steadied the mind according to the instructions, there come about in sequence the drawing down from its own place of the awakening mind, whose movement as mind has been brought under control, the guarding against its going out, and its increase. This alone is what produces virility. One who wants gold and the like releases it. For that very reason it is said "co-emergent": it is cast simultaneously by wisdom and by method. Some read "with the substance of one measure of copper." "At the side" means at the left flank of the wisdom partner; some say it means inside the lotus. Then comes the detailed teaching itself: "the act" means union. In "Meru" and the rest, the genitive stands in the sense of the nominative. "The king of seeds" is the syllable hriḥ. "The bell" is a lump of copper. "The wise one," being endowed with the yoga of the deity, should carry out the protection with the heart mantra and the other mantras.

The meaning is this. One embraces the young woman, who has the form of the Blessed Lady, while oneself in the form of the Blessed One. In one's heart is the awakening mind, formed as a moon disk, all its essence mixed with blood; on it one imagines the seed syllable suṃ, and on that the syllable hrīḥ. These three melt through great passion and, having melted and mingled with the awakening mind that arises from the joy shared equally, they fall into the lotus and are mixed with the wisdom partner's awakening mind and menstrual blood. While this is still warm, one should quickly smear it on copper or iron and cast it into the fire. So it is said.

Nor is it that the buddhas are not authoritative. Then comes the passage beginning "Therefore this." "Whatever things" means those already explained and those yet to be explained. "Not authoritative" means imputing them to be not valid. "He does not get free of hell" means hell does not let him go. In the passage beginning "the Dharma," the unsurpassed great secret of the eighty-four thousand heaps of Dharma is this yoginī tantra. "Into gold and silver as well" means that copper becomes gold and magnetic iron becomes silver. This very substance, while warm, together with one rati of purified mercury, should be poured into the nose every day for six months; its benefit is given in the passage beginning "lives." "Perfect union" is of vajra and lotus. "Four" is said because the victory vase, being the principal one, is understood even though it is not mentioned. Here the consecration of the vases is as explained in the fourth chapter.

The particular procedure is stated in the passage beginning "one's own": that is, with blood from one's own ring finger mixed with the barren woman's chief bodily element. "Black, red, greatly terrifying" means greatly terrifying through those colors, black and red. The black Blessed One is embraced by the red Citrasenā; here the sense is "surrounded by eight goddesses." Some lamas say that "both black and slightly red" refers only to the one without a wisdom partner.

The meaning is this. Here the master, as Heruka with his retinue, makes offerings with the five offerings to a maṇḍala constructed according to the rite, or to a maṇḍala painted on cloth, and gives torma to the ten directions at its eastern gate. Then he consecrates five vases whose mouths have been smeared inside with blood and the chief element. When the barren woman, facing the maṇḍala, bathes right after her menses, he should rise and first bathe her with the victory vase, reciting the heart mantra together with a long-drawn syllable hūṃ. At that very moment countless heroes and heroines, invited by the light rays of the seed syllable at his heart, holding in their outstretched hands vases filled with the awakening mind, bathe her. At that very time four fierce men holding swords, in the form of Heruka alone without a wisdom partner, should all at once strike the sky with their swords, intent on slaying the māras, grahas and the rest. In this sequence she is bathed with the other vases in turn. After that one should engage in lovemaking. Then she becomes pregnant with a son; and likewise, "a woman whose children die will have a child who lives."

This is the tenth chapter in the Abhayapaddhati.

To teach in detail the practice of the vidyādhara, the passage beginning "Then" is spoken. All the preliminary rites of the mountain operation in the fourth chapter are to be understood here. The particular points are stated: "by this very one" means by what is about to be explained. The passage beginning "one who is seized" applies from the moment when someone has been seized by Kāla, by death, and all the healers of poison, unable to help, have given him up, saying, "He has been seized by Kāla." "White-bodied" means that the Blessed One and the goddesses of the cardinal directions are white, while the goddesses of the intermediate directions are red. "According to the rite" means by completing the three samādhis. The sword is made of ground rice. "At that time" refers to the two vidyādharas. One draws it and puts it in place. "Leaves" means birch bark and the like. "The switch" is of white karavīra. "On one" means on one vidyādhara. "It falls" means onto the one who is seized. At that very moment the one who was seized rises up. Thus the mountain operation, the water operation and the vidyādhara operation are explained in detail; the other fifteen operations should be known as they are taught at length in the Buddhakapāla Tantra of twelve thousand lines.

This is the eleventh chapter in the Abhayapaddhati.

The brief teaching on the accomplishment of eye ointment is given in the passage beginning "Then." "Become chief" means of the twenty-four yoginīs. "Seen" means the things seen. "Which" means in which. "Not taught" means in the other tantras. "Greatly fortunate" means worthy of praise, because it reveals great qualities such as the unobscured powers and fearlessnesses. By "striving for the benefit of beings," the perfect fulfillment of others' benefit is taught. Because she is the very nature of the causal state, she is "the mother who gives birth to all buddhas." Because she is the essence of the result, she is "the great seal." "Yogin" means yogins. "All" means the attainment of every wonder. "Having attained other powers with certainty" is to be supplied. "Banner" means a man who is like a banner. "The hearth" is triangular. "In it" means in the hearth. "With the head" means in the head, that is, in the skull. "Its" means of the flesh. "Again" means in another skull.

"Of the obstructor" means the obstructors. "The smoke" means all of it. "Should be drunk" means by oneself. "Should be made to drink" refers to the wisdom partner.

This teaches the following. On a triangular hearth made from the hip bones of a mother who has been purified in the charnel ground, the yogin of Buddhakapāla places her skull, filled with the fat of the flesh of her buttocks, with a wick of charnel-ground cloth lit with charnel-ground fire. Above it, in the skull of another purified mother, he should collect the smoke while reciting the mantra. First he takes that soot with both hands before it touches the ground, and afterward places it in his left hand. Then he makes love slowly with a girl who has drunk kelimāli, whose womb is unimpaired and who is menstruating, visualized in the form of Citrasenā. The two of them taste a little of the awakening mind of both, mixed with the menstrual blood, and with much of the remaining portion he rubs the soot with the ring finger and thumb of his right hand. Then from that very powder he makes one hundred and eight pills, and having consecrated each pill one hundred and eight times by reciting the mantra, he applies it to the eyes as eye ointment. This is the combined meaning.

"With this very one" connects with "anoint with this" and what follows. "The great-armed one" is Vajrapāṇi, because his hands are devoted to satisfying all beings without exception. "The supreme yoginī" is easily found in the yoginī tantras. "With the ring finger and the thumb" means of the right hand; likewise, for the eye ointment described below, the action is done with the right hand. "Normal" means that by rubbing the eyes they become as they were before the ointment was applied. "It comes to the measure of seven claps" serves to turn back excessive rain. With "of the great being," the words "through the power" are to be supplied. In "of transforming one's own form," the genitive stands in the sense of the nominative. "Practices" means that the practices for accomplishing the state of a vidyādhara and the like, which are not taught in the shorter tantra but are taught in the extensive one, should be learned from the guru's own mouth.

To state the different kinds of rosary for the different kinds of mantra recitation, the passage beginning "Then" is spoken. "With elephant bone" means that one recites with a rosary of elephant bone at the time of fierce rites. "Great bone" is human bone. "Bird bone" is vulture bone. "With iron" means with an iron rosary. All this is elaboration even within the generation stage, taught in these various ways for the sake of the people to be trained. For the completion stage, "What use is this?" is said, in the passage beginning "The one mind." Mind alone is the stainless mirror of the three realms, for whatever it thinks of appears like a reflection. All this is imputed by mind, and that mind too is ultimately of the nature of a dream, free of all elaboration. Those deluded ones who do not know this possess great conceptuality, lacking nothing; and "they, through learning and the rest" means that even so they are held fast by conceptuality. Therefore, intending to set them firmly in meditation on suchness free of elaboration, he was moved by compassion.

This is the twelfth chapter in the Abhayapaddhati.

That in both stages it is this very suchness alone that is the purification of all obscurations is stated in the passage beginning "Then." Regarding "the chapter on purity": purified suchness is "purity," but suchness as such is not, because suchness exists at all times, and it would then follow that everyone is purified at all times. Moreover, that purity is not easy to actualize without the suchness of method; so, to show how it is actualized by relying on the Vajrayāna, which possesses that method, the passage beginning "the sister" is spoken. For the yogin it is to be taken literally. Alternatively, "the sister" is one well established in the Vajrayāna. "Her daughter" is the niece. "She who confers empowerment" is the mother. "The daughter" is a gentle woman, or one who is to be placed in that role. "Possessing wisdom" is said because of the word "purified." "The great seal" means for the sake of actualizing it. By this, the suchness that arises from method is taught.

The ḍombī (a woman of low caste) is one who delights in meditative concentration. The dancer is one skilled in conduct. The washerwoman is one who delights in white dharmas. The caṇḍālī is one whose disposition is patient, because she has decisively destroyed pride. The leatherworker is one who delights in a person whose body is the three vajras, made of virtuous formations.

The sister of the inner wisdom partner is lalanā. The sister's daughter is rasanā. The mother is the avadhūtī. Joining the former two with the latter through the guru's instruction is the daughter. The great bliss that arises from the perfect union of those three channels, whose nature is the nonduality of wisdom and method, is the wife. As it is said, "Its bliss is declared to be Kāminī." Ear consciousness is the ḍombī. Tongue consciousness is the dancer. Body consciousness is the washerwoman. Nose consciousness is the caṇḍālī. Eye consciousness is the leatherworker.

As for the definitive meaning, the unsurpassed wisdom partners, the sister and the rest, have already been explained in the first chapter. "One should gather the accumulations of merit and wisdom": this is the wife. Because she is of one taste with emptiness, she cannot be touched by any adventitious elaborations, and so she is the ḍombī. Because she abides neither in existence nor in nirvāṇa, she dances for those to be trained through various emanations of an illusory nature, and so she is the dancer. Because, through the sequence of realizations, the lesser of the lesser and the rest, she washes away the stains of the obscurations, the greater of the greater and the rest, together with their latent tendencies, she is the washerwoman. caṇḍalī is the definite realization of one's own nature as perfect wisdom; āli is great compassion; these lead to the state that cannot be surpassed, and so she is the caṇḍālī. Because she splits open the hide of ignorance that runs through the twelve links of existence, she is the leatherworker. These are the unsurpassed wisdom partners.

These alone are the wisdom partners, of outer and inner nature. Because they conform to the qualities of unsurpassed wisdom, they are to be understood as wisdom itself.

For that very reason it will be said, "From purifying the impure mind." By this, for the completion stage, the outer wisdom partner is the action seal, the inner wisdom partner is the dharma seal, and the unsurpassed wisdom partner is the great seal. Because it is by this alone that one abides in final purity, the dharma seal is the dharma body, since it is the very nature of the mind of great bliss. The body made beautiful with the major and minor marks through union with the action seal is the enjoyment body. The emanations, signless, manifold and endless, such as the wheels of maṇḍalas, are the pledge seal and the emanation body, because they never let pass the time for taking fortunate trainees into their care. The great seal, the pervader, is the essential body, the fourth.

Alternatively, the great seal, whose form blazes with the major and minor marks and possesses arms, faces, hand emblems and so on, is the enjoyment body. The nature of mind, to be known by each through his own awareness by means of the pledge seal, is the dharma body, taking "pledge" in the sense of perfect realization. The dharma seal is of the nature of speech, which, through the blessing of the dharma body and the enjoyment body, makes clear the suchness of dharmas in their various aspects. The action seal, being of the nature of accomplishing activity, is the emanation body, because it brings activities to perfection through various emanations.

In the generation stage, the outer wisdom partner herself, visualized in the form of the desired goddess, is the action seal. "A woman like oneself" is another name for the wisdom seal. The syllable hūṃ and the rest visualized in the body are also the dharma seal. The pledge seals are the deities assembled in the wheel of the maṇḍala, which is completed from seed syllables and the like, and which perform such functions as emanating and withdrawing; the hand emblems are also pledge seals. Oneself in the form of the principal deity is the great seal. Thus it has been made clear.

To state that the inner wisdom partners themselves are of the nature of the sense faculties, the afflictions and the objects, the passage beginning "Then" is spoken. "Taste" means the tongue. What is indicated here by "mind" is the body faculty. Because of the words to be explained below, beginning "How does the sister become form?", the objects, form and the rest, indicated by the eye and the rest, should also be known to be of the nature of the sister and the others.

"By which" means that which is form: form is delusion, because it is the cause of delusion; and correspondingly with aversion and the rest. "Desire" means desire too; "pride," pride too; and envy too. Because the objects, form and the rest, give rise to delusion and the rest, they are conventionally designated delusion and the rest, but not in reality. That is the meaning.

"Of mind" means that the eye, delusion, form and all these are particular states of mind alone. And mind is simply impure, through the stains of delusion and the rest; therefore the eye and the rest are impure as well. That is the intent.

If one asks, "Then how are the sister and the others explained to be wisdom itself?", it is said: "Therefore." Because these are wisdom only when purified, and they are purified when mind is purified, therefore one should purify the mind. By whom? It is said: "one endowed with great wisdom." Great wisdom is the great seal, and the one endowed with it is the one who meditates on it, because by it alone all stains without exception are washed away. For that very reason it is said, "endowed with a mind in perfect union," that is, wholly beyond the extremes of existence, nonexistence and the rest, of one taste in the nonduality of wisdom and method. If one does not do so, there will be no accomplishment, that is, no accomplishment of the great seal. When, by meditating with devotion and without interruption, one becomes that very purity whose nature is the great bliss of self-awareness, then the accomplishment comes "in this very one," that is, in this human birth. As the Blessed One said:

One who desires accomplishment
should not abide even for an instant
with a mind set on anything else.

And the complete perfection of the accumulations of merit and wisdom that arises from meditating with devotion and without interruption comes in a single life. For one who lacks meditation of this kind, it comes in another existence, that is, after an indefinite number of births, when one obtains a birth free of the eight unfree states and favorable to familiarization. Therefore purification comes from familiarization. That in the Perfection approach purification is taught to take three countless eons is because its accumulation of merit is of the nature of activity and it lacks the special method; so there is no contradiction.

Someone may object: "But then nothing at all to be meditated upon is taught here, because only the seals that are of the nature of completion are taught at length, and because yogins too make it clear in that way by means of them." That is true, because in reality neither an object of meditation nor meditation arises, since all dharmas are at peace from the very beginning. This is shown by the passage beginning "In what is not an entity": in what is not an entity, that is, in the unborn ultimate truth, where any imputation of entities has been negated, how could there be meditation? There is none at all, because it is pure from the very beginning.

"Then unborn suchness exists as something to be meditated upon." To this it is said, "What is a nonentity like?" There is no such thing as the unborn; it is called ultimate suchness only because it accords with the realization of the ultimate. And whatever entity is of the nature of conventional truth is not a nonentity to be meditated upon either, because it is a truth. The intent is that, since one is liberated merely by seeing the two truths as indivisible, those who have realized the stage of union have no clinging to an object of meditation, a meditator and meditation. As it is said in the Glorious Guhyasamāja:

In what is not an entity there is no meditation;
meditation is not meditation.
Thus, entities being no entities,
meditation is not apprehended.

For that very reason it concludes: "How could there be meditation?" The intent is that therefore it is not apprehended.

"Is meditation in the completion stage, then, simply cut off?" It is not cut off. "Then what is it like?" What it is like will be explained.

Having thus removed clinging to an object of meditation and the rest in ultimate terms, and wishing to make things clear with regard to form and the rest, he speaks the passage beginning "the sister." "How," that is, why: the distinct divisions, the white one and the rest, endowed with form and connected with the sense faculties through the relation of what is benefited and what benefits, are seen by the impure mind, but not by the pure mind, because ultimately the faculties, the objects and the cognitions of them are without intrinsic nature. Likewise with what follows. "Threefold" is by the distinction of mind, mental consciousness and that which does not think. "Of one endowed with such union" means endowed with the yoga of the great seal. "Purified" refers to what is smelled, that is, odor. "Not to be seen" means neither an object of sight nor an object of knowledge. For that very reason, attachment, aversion and the rest do not arise from odors and the like. "Of the form of tasting" means that there is no form of tasting, because tongue and taste do not exist. "By mind," that is, by the body faculty indicated by "mind," one experiences pleasure arising toward tangible objects, and, as indicated by that, pain and equanimity; and what is that like? The mental faculty is mind itself. From pleasure, as indicated, comes pain; that is, one experiences joy, dejection and equanimity, and this connects with "what is that like?"

Someone may ask: "Why are the five objects, form and the rest, the five faculties that apprehend them, and their consciousnesses presented as having the nature of the five wisdom partners, while the sixth object, faculty and consciousness are not mentioned?" The mental faculty is not something separate, like the faculties of the eye and the rest. The consciousness that has just ceased is called the mental faculty, because it is the governing condition that produces a later, particular consciousness; its particulars are mind and the mental factors, its object, and mental consciousness. Thus the nonconceptual ones, the mental faculty and its object, are of the nature of the five sense consciousnesses. The mental factors too are nothing other than minds; as it is said, "Mental factors are not other than mind." The conceptual ones are of the nature of mental consciousness. As for mental consciousness, by the reasoning of the verse:

Cutting off and reconnecting, freedom from desire,
decline, death and transference, and birth
are held to belong to mental consciousness alone,

it is the principal one, and so it is the enjoyer; therefore, as what benefits, it is the enjoyer, and it is not presented as having the nature of a wisdom partner. The unsurpassed wisdom presented earlier exists only in mental consciousness. "Mind" is mentioned here so that one may enter into analysis of the entire city of entities.

Thus by this close connection the eighteen elements are taught; gathering just these gives the twelve sense sources, and condensing them further gives the aggregates. The three realms too are just these. And this is mind only. As it is said in the Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra: "O children of the Victors, it is thus: the three realms are mind only." The noble Nāgārjunapāda too said in the Commentary on the Awakening Mind:

To turn back the clinging to self,
the aggregates, elements and the rest were taught;
and these too, by establishing mind only,
the greatly fortunate dismantle.

That mind too is conventional, as the same text says:

That all these are mind only,
as the sages have taught,
was to dispel the fear of the childish;
it is not reality.

If one asks how mind is not reality, he says "from analyzing the two," that is, from examining the own nature of entities and of nonentities. "Then the mind that analyzes exists ultimately?" To this he says "analysis." From "one engages in analysis," the analysis, that is, the consciousness of the analyzer, is "there" not: it does not stand once the two have been analyzed. As the noble Śāntidevapāda said:

When what is to be analyzed has been analyzed,
analysis has no basis left;
having no basis, it does not arise,
and this is called nirvāṇa.

Likewise he said:

When neither entity nor nonentity
remains before the mind,
then, there being no other alternative,
with no object, it is completely at peace.

And in the Precious Garland for the King it is said:

All the dharmas of beings are held
to be fuel for the fire of consciousness;
burned by the fire of analysis as things truly are,
they are brought to peace.

If one asks how the two are analyzed, he says, in the passage beginning "in entities": entity and nonentity are not each other's nature, because they are contradictory. "Then they exist separately." To this he says, "Both entity and nonentity are empty": the natures of entity and of nonentity are empty, because mind is without intrinsic nature, being free of the nature of one and of not-one.

Thus it is not one. For if it were a single thing, there would be no experience of it appearing variegated, with the nature of blue and of yellow; and it would follow that yellow and all the rest would be realized as included within blue. Again, if blue were distinct in its earlier, later and middle parts, it would not be realized as appearing coarse; rather, by the reasoning "Whatever has distinct directional parts cannot be one," it would appear as mere atoms. Nor is it not-one. If mind were not one, then, since it would be divided into atoms, and there is no genuine experience of anything other than that, the atoms too would each be of the nature of individual self-awareness, and it would follow that only separate atoms appear. Nor is it reasonable that it has the nature of both, because no aspect of that kind is experienced; for in every case our view rests on valid cognition. Nor is any direct experience observed of a distinguished aspect, whether together or apart, that could be inferred; nor is it of the nature of inference either, since inference is preceded by that.

How, then, could one conceive of an essential nature of exclusion, which is not observed and for which there is no valid cognition, as something to be inferred? Suppose it is said: "Through its variegatedness, the experience of the gross aspect as the nature of self-awareness cannot be denied and exists just to that extent; and it would not come about if there were no essential nature of exclusion to be inferred. Since it is not tenable otherwise than if that exists, it must be postulated." That is not so, because it is most clearly tenable as freedom from elaboration solely through a nature that is neither of the two, and so the argument amounts to no more than understanding by implication.

Therefore arising, too, is not reasonable, because there is no valid cognition of the relation of cause and effect. If the relation of cause and effect is established by valid cognition operating on what is prior and what is subsequent, then the nondual experience free of the two aspects has no operation on prior and subsequent, and there is no correct knowing other than it, since there is no valid cognition of it; this has already been proven. When, in this way, because there is no valid cognition of the relation of cause and effect, there is simply no arising, then from what would anything arise: from itself, from another, from both, or without a cause? Since there is thus no arising, mind does not exist with the nature of an entity either; for an entity is so called because it comes into being, and it is posited only for a coming into being preceded by nonexistence. For that very reason, it does not have the nature of a nonentity either, which is the nature of that entity's destruction. And because there is no intrinsic nature of entity or nonentity, there are no elaborations at all; for since elaborations depend on entity and nonentity, entity and nonentity pervade them, and when the pervader is absent, the pervaded is absent as well.

Therefore, through this analysis, one should realize emptiness as freedom from elaboration. This alone is the seeing of suchness, of which it is said: "When, from definitive examination with the eye of wisdom, the light of correct primordial wisdom has arisen, one does not see any phenomena at all." As is said in the Blessed Lady, the Perfection of Wisdom: "What is the wisdom eye of the buddhas, the blessed ones? That by which not even the slightest thing is seen. Likewise, what is seeing the ultimate? That in which no phenomena at all are seen."

Therefore yogins of suchness, whatever kind of conduct they abide in, see all phenomena and yet have no seeing. It is just this kind of non-seeing that is intended, and not the non-attending of those who are asleep or whose eyes are closed. For in that case, since habitual tendencies would not have been abandoned, attachment and the rest, which are rooted in clinging to form and so on, would arise again, just as on rising from the absorption without perception and the like, and the yogin would remain simply unliberated. Nor is abandonment achieved merely by giving up the attention that clings to form and so on, which has existed since beginningless time; for then it would follow that one had abandoned them through the absorption without perception and the like as well. Nor is one able to give up that attention without abandoning doubt about objects such as form, just as heat is not removed so long as fire is not removed. It is therefore certain that clinging to form and so on is to be dispelled only through freedom from the seed of doubt, and not in the way one pulls out a thorn.

Having thus dispelled attention to entity and nonentity, to one's own entity and another's entity, and so on, the mind is withdrawn from everything and thinks of nothing at all. From that not-thinking there arises, for the yogin who served the guru during the earlier period of conduct, the Blessed Lady, the Perfection of Wisdom, realized solely through the guru's kindness. Being utterly free of the factors of the means of valid cognition, the object of valid cognition, and the result, its self-luminous nature is free of elaboration; being thereby free of the mass of suffering that is the nature of saṃsāra, which consists of elaboration, its own essential nature is supreme joy, and this is directly realized in the manner of non-seeing. Through its power the yogin reflects: "Alas, the power of delusion! Although these beings are in nirvāṇa, they do not realize their own nature. How can they be brought to realize it?" And in that very nature, focusing on all beings without exception, great compassion arises, whose nature is free of any object of focus.

The nature of the complete maṇḍala, which has just that as its self, the self and the nature of all things, whose identity is great compassion, is to be viewed as the Blessed Lady Prajñā, otherwise named Mahāmudrā, and, because it has her nature, as inseparable from her. At that time emptiness and compassion are of that nature, and this is the nonconceptual yoga. It is precisely the yoga of six limbs taught in the Later Tantra of the Guhyasamāja:

Withdrawal, and likewise
meditative concentration, breath control, retention,
recollection, and samādhi:
these are held to be the six limbs of yoga.

Thus, just as in seeing the images of a prognostic mirror rite performed through a young girl, the senses' apprehending of their own objects, being of one taste with emptiness, is withdrawal. After that comes meditative concentration, which has five aspects: conception, which is the definite conceiving of an entity; analysis, in which, the senses' apprehending of their own objects being likewise of one taste with emptiness, entities are analyzed as having the very nature of emptiness; joy, which is the bliss that comes from turning, through such analysis, toward entry into suchness; great bliss, such as the perfect pliancy of body that arises from that; and, through familiarization in this way, one-pointedness of mind, whose nature is the wisdom that ultimately makes all phenomena one in emptiness.

Then the entry of the life-wind, whose nature is entering, abiding and emerging solely along the path of the avadhūtī, the lalanā and the rasanā having been stopped, is breath control. Then the entry of the life-wind, the mount of the mind, into the drop of awakening without coming out of the avadhūtī, being of one taste with emptiness, is retention. At that time, too, what issues from caṇḍālī (the inner fire) blazes fiercely, and the hare-marked one (the moon), intensely heated by it, flows down profusely. At that time the undiminished awakening mind, held within the vajra jewel, is gradually perfected with the blisses beginning with the concordant one, and becomes stainless for as long as it remains.

Then, when the mind has fully entered from retention, many rays of light radiate from that nonconceptual seeing of the deities of the complete maṇḍala, which has the form of a reflection and is preceded by the separate appearance of signs, and from them the three realms are emanated: this is recollection. Then that very nonconceptual yoga, whose nature is the great bliss of the flow, whose self is union, the wisdom body having the nature of the great Vajradhara, is samādhi.

Thus, when the yogin has the nature of wisdom and means free of elaboration, at that time the protectors establish him as the nature of all merit and the nature of all wisdom, as having the nature of all the deities of the maṇḍala, and as the nature of all the perfections. This is stated:

This is the supreme yoga,
which has abandoned all conceptual thought;
the yogin who abides in it
becomes in actuality the omniscient Blessed One.
This is the supreme reality,
the peace that is the nature of wisdom and means;
this itself is nondual wisdom,
this is the supreme awakening mind.

The noble Nāgārjunapāda too said:

Whatever person, even for just a single instant,
thoroughly meditates on the awakening mind,
the heap of that one's merit
cannot be counted as being just so much.
This precious mind free of afflictions
is declared to be the one supreme treasure;
it cannot be destroyed or stolen
by the thieves, the afflictions, the māras and the rest.

With all this in mind it is said, "These are the very purity of mind": "these" means these things just described. For that very reason, because it is this alone that accomplishes the ground of Vajradhara, which is of the nature of great bliss, this approach is distinguished from the approach of the perfections.

But, one may ask, this is simply of the nature of nondual wisdom, so what is its distinction? There is a distinction: it is precisely the accomplishing of that as its very nature. It is just as, in fact, a great distinction is proclaimed because the powers, the fearlessnesses and the rest, which are of one taste with emptiness, are not accomplished as emptiness even by those who realize them in the śrāvaka approach, whereas in the approach of the perfections they are so accomplished.

But, one may object, those who have attained the first meditative concentration also present bliss, as do wheel-turning monarchs, so what is the use of realizing the twofold selflessness? And now the first ground is called Very Joyful solely because of surpassing joy. That is true. But this is not innate bliss, and for that very reason it is not great bliss; nor does it serve as a means in the stage of the beginner. For to experience the eye of a needle, which is called a hole, is not to experience what is equal to space.

But, one may ask, is it not said in the Perfection of Wisdom, "It is the bliss of unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening"? This is indeed said, but its means is not taught there, because innate great bliss, which serves as the means, is not taught. For that very reason, that too is to be understood only through this sequence. What, then, is there no awakening in the approach of the perfections? That is not so. Rather, one should say: "From what, in the Perfection of Wisdom, does even the scent of unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening come? The four culminations too definitively depend on this means." But that is a very extensive matter, so let this suffice; only those who know the oral instructions will come to understand this distinction clearly.

Because it accomplishes great bliss, the passage beginning "Then" is spoken in order to establish chiefly the object, the tangible, together with its sense faculty and its consciousness, as having the nature of compassion and the rest, and also as having the nature of the five wisdoms. For the body of the outer Prajñā is the object, and from union with her the inner body and wisdom are united. What takes place will be explained. "Endowed with gazing": seeing the supreme woman is compassion, which is freedom from suffering. Embracing is loving-kindness, which is endowment with happiness. From the rubbing of the two organs comes joy, which is not being parted from happiness. From the flowing of the awakening mind comes equanimity, which is freedom from all conceptual thought. In the same way, the inner body and cognition have the nature of compassion and the rest.

"The yogin who has become thus": one endowed with the Mahāmudrā that has the nature of compassion and the rest is not bound by attachment and the rest. That beings are bound when there is no yoga of Mahāmudrā is stated in the passage beginning "from seeing." "Somewhat more" is its result. "The end of all" means from the flowing. Thus joy and weariness are taught as the results. "Born of the three realms" intends that compassion and the rest, which are of emptiness, are also causes of liberation. The summary is the passage beginning "Perfectly pure." "The three things" means by object, sense faculty and consciousness.

Thus, by devoting oneself to familiarization,
just as the foods of the feast are gathered:

explaining this, it says "whatever" and so on: excrement, urine and the rest, the nectars and the five lamps, are to be regarded as nectars by nature. And not these alone, but also their playful delights and so on. The Mahāmudrā too should be viewed as having the nature of Mahāmudrā; one endowed with such meditation attains accomplishment.

"How" is then stated. "Five" refers to the yoginīs, the sister and the rest. "Grants glory and the great seal": glory is characterized by the perfection of one's own and others' aims, and Mahāmudrā alone grants it, namely to those who make offerings. That is why it says, "One should make offerings." If one asks how, it says, "One should engage in desire."

But, one may object, the supreme offering to these is then simply engaging in desire, and that is a fault. It is not a fault, because "one is not stained." But, one may object, engaging in desire is itself being stained. It is not, because one is said to be stained when one is possessed of jealousy and the like, and those faults of jealousy and the like do not harm this yogin.

But, one may ask, how is such an offering the very highest and connected with its result, since it is merely a particular kind of superimposition? Superimposition is of two kinds: of the nature of reality and of a nature that is not reality.

Of these, superimposition having the nature of reality is without fault, because, like superimposing the notion of mother and so on, it brings about awakening indirectly.

But, one may object, since the experience of some is also forbidden, there is a contradiction. That is not so. Action is of two kinds, divided into what is motivated and what motivates. Of these, all actions of body and speech are what is motivated, and the mind is what motivates. That too is of two kinds: what has the nature of non-attachment and the rest, and the other. Of these, all actions of body and speech motivated by attachment and the rest are nonvirtuous, and it is these that are forbidden. All that is motivated by non-attachment and the rest is solely virtuous. The yogin endowed with the nondual yoga of Mahāmudrā appears to beings as wholly having the nature of non-attachment and the rest. Therefore, though he engages in every kind of conduct, he is solely of the nature of virtue, because his nature is such that no nonvirtue is found in it: this is stated in the passage beginning "That yogin." Reality is the nature of Mahāmudrā; therefore he is not stained by the faults of saṃsāra. Therefore meditation on the awakening mind, whose nature is the nonduality of wisdom and means, is alone what purifies all wrongdoing and brings about all good. As the noble Nāgārjunapāda said:

This is the supreme Great Vehicle;
this is the supreme awakening mind.
Those in meditative equipoise, with effort,
should generate the awakening mind.
For accomplishing one's own and others' aims in saṃsāra,
no other means apart from the awakening mind
has ever before
been seen by the buddhas.

And:

Since this is that supreme yoga,
it is worthy of homage from the tathāgatas.
The yogas that arise from it
are proclaimed to be infinite.
From reality itself, samādhis
arise, infinite in number.
Mudrās, maṇḍalas and mantras
are utterly pure through that yoga.
Actions of pacifying and enriching,
and whatever else is born from mantra,
minor accomplishments as many
as the grains of sand of Jahnu's daughter (the Ganges),
and the other accomplishments of mudrā,
as many as are enumerated on the earth,
are declared by the Vajra of Mind
to be born from this yoga.
Whatever is conducive to penetration,
in its four kinds, and the ten grounds,
and abiding in the fruit: all these
truly arise from this yoga.
The victors' children who have arisen, who will arise,
and who are now arising,
the great beings such as Lokeśvara:
they too abide in this alone.
The buddhas of the past and the future,
and those held to have arisen in the present,
the rhinoceros-like ones (pratyekabuddhas) and the śrāvaka Saṅgha:
they too arise from this.
As many emanations as arise
through the divisions of Yoga, Caryā and Kriyā,
all of them, as yoga,
arise definitively from this alone.
The nature of the imagined,
the things that arise through the force of habitual tendencies,
and as many things as are stationary and moving:
all of these depend on that.

That emptiness would be simply tasteless if there were no five sense pleasures is stated in the passage beginning "As long as." As long as the essences of the sense objects do not nurture the sprout of awakening, so long the wish-fulfilling tree of buddhahood will not come into being, just as a perfect tree does not come from a sprout deprived of moisture. As Sarahapāda said:

If the very tiny sprout of mind
is not moistened by the pure essence of the body,
how will one obtain the wish-fulfilling tree itself,
which bestows fruit that pervades the sky?

This is the thirteenth chapter of the Abhayapaddhati.

Because exceptional faith arose from the teaching of the unsurpassed meaning that was desired, the passage beginning "Then" is spoken in order to show how she offered praise. Because she sports with great bliss, she is "Goddess." "Generatrix" is the nature of the path. "Glorious One" is the nature of the result, the perfection of realization and abandonment. Because she is a source of great terror for those without the lineage, she is "Fearsome One." "Greatly Renowned One" is she whose renown is widely heard. Because her nature is loving-kindness, she is "Gentle One." Because she magically displays the circle of the maṇḍala and the rest through infinite miraculous power, she is "Goddess." Because she is not different from great compassion and great wisdom, she is "Mahāmudrā"; through these epithets she is praised for the perfection of her own aim. "She who shows the result of the great aim": the great aim is Vajradharahood itself. That alone is the supreme result of familiarization with the path, and she shows it to those to be trained; in this way she is praised for the perfection of others' aim.

She is praised by way of those to whom she teaches, in the passage beginning "Buddhas." "By the buddhas" means by Vairocana and the rest. "Which" refers to the accomplishment that is not the domain even of bodhisattvas. "Goddess" means by the goddess. "With a single utterance" means not first saying something hard to understand and then clarifying it with other words. "Great vessel" means the vessel of the great mundane and supramundane accomplishments. "All" means having the nature of the twenty-four yoginīs. "Which" means that accomplishment. "Spoken" means by Citrasenā. "This" means "of this." "The qualities of the buddhas" are the powers, the fearlessnesses and the rest: "may they too forbear with me," because their nature as cause and result is hidden from those to be trained by the supreme victors, and I have made it clear in this tantra, whose domain is a meaning extremely hidden and very hard to find. Hence the intention is: "It is these alone who have taught this, through me." And that one should not be impatient with me through superimposing on me the harm of having taught wrongly is expressed by "Supreme victors, be forbearing!", with the grammatical case changed.

Having taught the whole tantra, in order to show that only those who have received empowerment are authorized for it, the empowerment is taught in the passage beginning "Then." "Having supplicated" means for the sake of the secret empowerment and the wisdom-gnosis empowerment. "To the great goddess" means to the great goddess.

"Cut" means by you. For the reading "covered," it means pervading, namely me. "Her" means of that woman. Why does one strive in this way to please Prajñā? It says: "She is worshipped by all the buddhas." The oral instruction is that in both the causal and the resultant states the accomplishment of Mahāmudrā comes solely through the yoga of the action seal. How is this so? It says: "In this alone," that is, the buddhas and the rest dwell in Prajñā; they are of the nature of great bliss, and their support too is Prajñā. Therefore one should receive from her the wisdom-gnosis empowerment in accordance with the rite.

For that very reason it makes clear the very nature of wisdom-gnosis, in the passage beginning "The senses": the objects and the rest are also implied by the mention of the senses. "Self" means one's own mind, in accordance with the statement, "The mind is imagined as a self." Or else, the five appropriated aggregates, the basis on which a self is imputed: all this "self" is without intrinsic nature, and where it is not observed, that is the realization of supreme joy. Supreme joy here is solely innate joy, otherwise called wisdom-gnosis, because it is free of superimposition onto the senses and the rest. This is stated in the Two-Part Tantra:

When supreme joy is attained,
in the instant free of variety.

Its true attainment is to be understood only through the sequence of arising, and that has already been examined in the first chapter.

Homage is paid to supreme joy alone as the cause, in the passage beginning "To that." "By whose dawning" means by the dawning of the innate. Homage is paid to it as the result: whatever, through familiarization, by the sequence of realizing what is conducive to penetration and the rest, is manifest joy; the full possession of outstanding precious qualities such as the all-round higher knowledges, the powers and the fearlessnesses, which is beautiful because the victors are free of every trace of the turbidity of the afflictions; the body abiding in the supreme delight of Samantabhadra, the fourth body, Vajradhara, the skilled one: this is hidden in the minds of the perfectly complete buddhas, because they have abandoned all distraction of conceiving it as having that nature, and it is not in any way the domain of words or intellect. For that very reason it will be explained that Citrasenā and the others, having realized this, remained in silence. "The One" is nondual supreme joy; it is "awakened" because it is free of the sleep of the residual habitual tendencies of all obscurations: "homage to it."

Then by this verse it is said that wisdom-gnosis itself, arising in a distinguished nature like the form of a wave of water, is the fourth empowerment. The meaning is that this Vajradhara, whose nature is great bliss, is the setting of all the tantras.

The fourteenth chapter of the Abhayapaddhati is complete.

Through the great virtue that has arisen from explaining the path of the glorious hero Heruka,
may these beings cut through the many delusions of saṃsāra and gather into his maṇḍala.
May I long uphold the infinite body of this Sage,
and, spreading bliss with great devotion to each living being, hold their fears in check.

The scholar of Vikramaśīla,
Abhaya, with words that touch the vital points,
in the twenty-fifth year
of the reign of Rāmapāla, composed this.

Through the power of the glorious Yakṣapāla, son of the lord of Magadha,
whose body, the perfect ground of victory over the three worlds, is unimpaired,
amassed for the sake of benefiting beings in no small measure,
this treatise was composed at his request, that it might become fully stable.

The Vajra Lady, the ground magically displayed by the play of the sublime nondual essence that bestows the bliss of joy, sporting with this glorious wisdom, is the abode of the garland of stairways arranged and set in order by the host of sages. May she be victorious, possessed of the power of right discernment of what has and has not been done for those who seek the meaning; and through my explanation of her, may there arise the satisfaction that seekers seek, filled with the nectar of incomparable virtue.

Sixteen hundred verses and a quarter:
this is the measure of the whole.
For the adornment of the learned,
may its substance, in accord with this measure, dwell in their hearts.

Thus ends the Abhayapaddhati, a commentary on the great Buddhakapāla Tantra, composed by the elder and scholar Abhayākaragupta.

This had earlier been translated by a learned translator, but in the meantime part of the manuscript was lost and it was incomplete. The translator, the monk Pang Lotsāwa Lodrö Tenpa, urged by the words of the preceptor Jampal Serpo of Chiwo Lhé, filled in the missing portions: in the first chapter, from "the Mahāmudrā vajra of the Blessed One" up to "arises from the lower path," and, where only the meditation on the deity's body is taught, from "what is taught" up to "the stationary, the earth and the rest, and the moving"; from the end of the third chapter, "having completed the meditation in the form of Heruka," up to the end of the fourth chapter, "here, ignorance; by that"; from the beginning of the thirteenth chapter, "reality in all," up to the end of the fourteenth chapter, "by whose dawning"; and the two verses beginning "Victorious over the three worlds." For the remainder he acted as chief editor, and so translated and finalized the text.

Translator's notes

The opening dedicatory verses are in long, compressed meter and their syntax is obscure; the rendering gives the most likely construal. In "because when it is present the teaching can begin," the text as written reads stong pa, "emptiness," which makes little sense here; it is read as ston pa, "teaching." The "two syllables" are e and vaṃ of evaṃ, "thus," understood as wisdom and skillful means. The "fourth moment" is the moment of signlessness, associated with innate joy. The commentator's name is Abhaya, that is, Abhayākaragupta. "Entered complete nirvāṇa" is read here both literally, as the melting of the awakening mind in union, and as the nonabiding nirvāṇa. The remark on the "form ending in the past participle" and the particle "beyond" is a note on the Sanskrit wording of the root text (a niṣṭhā participle, read in some copies as para); its exact reference is uncertain. In "there is no accomplishment either," the negation is supplied from the parallel "just as recitation is not possible," which the Tibetan carries over by "also." The etymology of mantra plays on man, "mind," and tra, "protecting." The etymologies of kapāla (kam "bliss" plus pāla "protecting") and pustaka (pu "expanding" plus kaṃ "bliss") play on the Sanskrit; the word pullīramālaya cited for pu is obscure and is given as written. "Reversing the order from the anāhata": the text writes anāhala, read as anāhata, the unstruck center at the heart; this is uncertain. The nāga king stobs rgyu is not securely identified and is rendered by meaning as "the Mighty Mover." "The first tantra of the Paramādya" takes མཆོག་གི་དང་པོ as the title Paramādya. "The supreme" and "the special joy" are the joys paramānanda and viramānanda. In "opening the lotus," རྩ་བའི་འདོན་པ is read as drawing the poison out from its root; it could instead refer to the channels. ཐོད་ལོ་ཀོར, rendered "turban," is uncertain. "Myna bird" renders ཤཱ་ལི་ཀཱ (śālikā). "The noble master" renders འཕགས་པའི་ཞབས, an honorific (Āryapāda) whose referent is not specified here. "The special joy" is viramānanda. "The Litany of the Names" is the Mañjuśrīnāmasaṃgīti. "Bliss is of the great elements" takes འབྱུང་བ་ཆེ as mahābhūta, following the Hevajra verse cited. In the quoted verse, སྦྲུལ་དྲུག ("six snakes") is read as སྦྲུལ་དུག, "snake poison," since the passage concerns destroying poison. The etymology of "charnel ground" plays on ro, "corpse" and "taste"; this cannot be carried over into English. The last sentence, on "great fame," is obscure. Some etymological wordplay is translated literally, as with "daughter" as "she who milks" (Skt. duh). In the Saṃpuṭa verse, "shattering storm" renders a Tibetan calque of Skt. prabhañjana, "storm wind." In the verse on errors, the Tibetan ཉེར་མཁོའི་ཆད is unclear; it is rendered "employed as needed." The sentence ending "at other times it is only the yoga of equipoise" is translated as it stands. It probably intends a contrast, such as "not the yoga of equipoise." The sentence on the eighth line is obscure. "Raising, stiffening" renders སྤར་དང་རེངས་པ, rite names whose exact sense is uncertain. The pairing of "the seven and the five" with "the fourteen empowerments" is translated as it stands. "The twelve-thousand-verse root tantra" takes སྟོང་ཕྲག་བཅུ་གཉིས་པ as the size of the root tantra. ཤེས་རབ here alternates between "consort" (prajñā) and "wisdom"; "bringing them under the sway of the supreme prajñā" and "possessed of a prajñā who is unspoiled" follow the consort sense suggested by the context. "Vajra crest," "vajra beak," "vajra staff," "Vairocana" and "the five lamps" are coded ritual terms that the commentary partly decodes (Vairocana here names a substance burned with the wood, and the five lamps conventionally denote the five meats). "Babbola" is given as spelled; it is likely the babbūla tree (acacia). "Clarified fat rendered from a man" is a tentative reading of སྐྱེས་བུ་སྦྱངས་པའི་མར་ཁུ. The Tantra of the Accomplished Hero is translated from the Tibetan title, as its Sanskrit name could not be identified with confidence. The prose weights for the pills read literally "six or three," "drung or four" and "six or five" before the raktis; the unclear syllables were taken as standing for the māṣa named in the verse, and the prose gives five for the copper where the verse gives three. "Reciting the mantra in reverse" renders བཟློག, which could be a slip for བཟླས, "reciting." In the list of channels: "Bhāvakī" reads སྦོམ་པ་མོ for the usual སྒོམ་པ་མོ; Kūrmajā's "bone" reads རུས་སྦལ ("turtle"), evidently for རུས་པ; "Phreng bzangs ma" (Sumālinī) is written ཕྲིང. The Tail One (མཇུག་མ), Joyful Face (དགའ་གདོང་མ) and the Cleaving One (འཆེད་མ) could not be identified with confidence and are translated literally; "the sides of the belly" for ལྟ་ཟུར is tentative. "Akṣobhya" and "the moon" in the list are code terms for subtle-body substances. The Two Examinations is the usual Tibetan name for the Hevajra Tantra. "Drips" renders འཛེག ("climbs"), read as འཛག, which the context of the melting moon requires. Here གཏུམ་མོ is the caṇḍālī fire of the completion stage, not the channel named Caṇḍikā in the earlier list. The "moon's torment of conceptual thought is pacified" takes གདུང་བཞི་བ as གདུང་ཞི་བ. The daṇḍa is an Indian time unit of twenty-four minutes; both reckonings give 21,600 breaths a day. "The five questions" renders དྲི་མ་ལྔ ("five stains"), read as དྲི་བ་ལྔ, since the commentary goes on to answer the first, third and fifth questions. The verse lists Indian number-words (arrow for five, Veda for four, hand for two, mountain for seven, and so on) naming the half-watches of day and night at which a snakebite is incurable; they are translated as words. The remark on the neuter gender refers to the Sanskrit of the root text and is obscure in the Tibetan. "Brought the nāgas" for ཀླུ་རྣམས་གཏད་པར་བྱས་ནས is tentative. "The unsurpassed remedy for poison" renders དུག་བྱེད་པ་བླ་ན་མེད་པ, literally "unsurpassed poison-doer," understood as what acts upon poison. "Nothing to fear" for མི་དགོས is a tentative rendering of a terse phrase. The two-line quotation on the hidden cause is from Dharmakīrti's reasoning on the Buddha as an authority; "Whatever is dependent origination" is a verse attributed to Nāgārjuna. Śaṃkara is a name of Śiva. The grammatical remark on aṇ concerns the Sanskrit of the root text.
