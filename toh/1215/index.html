About this text

The Determination of the Sites and So Forth, known in Sanskrit as the Pīṭhādinirṇaya and in Tibetan as gnas la sogs pa gtan la dbab pa, is a short tantric treatise that opens with homage to glorious Hevajra and to the lotus feet of Vajrayoginī. Its author promises to write concisely and clearly, and the work explains a passage from the Hevajra Tantra, the scripture known as the Two Examinations, which lists twelve sacred grounds. These grounds are the site and near-site, the field and near-field, the chandoha and near-chandoha, the gathering and near-gathering, the pīlava and near-pīlava, and the charnel ground and near-charnel ground. The text sets out to identify and clarify these sacred places, which are central to the practice and symbolism of the Hevajra and yoginī tantra traditions. It is Toh 1215 in the Tengyur, where it belongs among the commentarial works on the tantras.

Translation

In the language of India: pīṭhādinirṇṇaya. In the language of Tibet: The Determination of the Sites and So Forth.

Homage to glorious Hevajra.

Having first bowed deeply
at the lotus feet of glorious Vajrayoginī,
I shall write, concisely and clearly,
this determination of the sites and so forth.

On this subject, the Blessed One said in the Two Examinations:

"The site and the near-site,
the field and the near-field,
chandoha and the near-chandoha,
the gathering and likewise the near-gathering,
pīlava and the near-pīlava,
the charnel ground and the near-charnel ground:
these are the twelve grounds.
The lord of the ten grounds is the protector.
These are not to be named by other terms."

Here the words beginning "the site" give the teaching in brief. By "these" are meant the site and the rest, which are the ground Very Joyful and the rest; together with the ground of conduct through aspiration, they are twelve. The grounds are distinct states of the wisdom of buddhas and bodhisattvas, because buddhas and bodhisattvas realize them ever more fully by thorough meditation. For that very reason it is said, "The lord of the ten grounds," which is the bodhisattva, and "the protector," which is the buddha. These are to be named with the terms site and so forth, and not with other terms distinct from site and so forth. Why? Because they are established as the sites and so forth with the inner meaning as primary.

Now the first ground, Very Joyful, is the site. This is because it sees the reality not seen since beginningless time, because joy abounds in it, and because it becomes the basis for fully satisfying limitless sentient beings through meditative absorption, the superknowledges and so on, and through the perfection of generosity.

The ground Stainless is the near-site, because it is near the site. This is because, with the perfection of moral discipline brought to its utmost culmination, the ten virtues are completely pure, and because meditative absorption, the superknowledges and so on have come under its control.

The site and the near-site are near to each other by way of a relationship in which each is benefited by the other and benefits it; they are not near because they are very close in place or time. Why? Because what makes cause and effect near is that, through the very capacity to produce the effect, they come within the range of that conventional term. Outwardly, the site and the near-site benefit each other through the very interaction by which the yoginīs who dwell in them benefit one another. The field and near-field and the rest should be understood in the same way.

The ground Luminous is the field. This is because, with the perfection of patience brought to its utmost culmination, it relies on meditative absorption that never declines, it generates the light of the great Dharma, and it brings the superknowledges and so on to their utmost culmination.

The ground Radiant is the near-field. This is because, with the perfection of diligence predominant, the flames of wisdom blaze forth to burn the firewood of the afflictions, and so generate the light of the dharmas of awakening.

The ground Difficult to Train is chandoha. Through intention it pursues the aim and so accomplishes one's own aims and those of others, and because it does so through thought itself, it is conceptual thought. For on the ground Difficult to Train the bodhisattva, excelling in the perfection of meditative concentration, matures sentient beings through great miraculous powers and through the application of various skillful means. Devoted to maturing beings, and fully applying roots of virtue in a mental continuum that follows the appearance of buddhas and never turns back, he strives in the search for the special Dharma.

The ground Manifest is the near-chandoha. This is because, being by nature the perfection of wisdom made manifest, it possesses intention and thought and so possesses the superior path.

The ground Far Gone is the gathering, since it is gathered together and blended. This is because in it the perfection of skillful means is fully developed, and because it blends together, without signs and without effort, the four immeasurables, the meditative concentrations, the liberations, the meditative absorptions, the meditative attainments, the superknowledges and so on.

The ground Immovable is the near-gathering. This is because, having fully attained the fruit of the perfection of meditative concentration, it engages, without signs and without effort, through loving-kindness and the rest, in purifying sentient beings and the vessel-field, with complete immovability.

The ground Good Intelligence is the charnel ground. This is because it is like a charnel ground of selflessness, and because, through the perfection of strength, the bodhisattva teaches his own higher realization of the dharmadhātu, the selflessness of all phenomena, so that sentient beings may realize it as well, and without effort accomplishes the aims of sentient beings in saṃsāra and perfect, complete awakening.

The ground Cloud of Dharma is the near-charnel ground. This is because, through the perfection of primordial wisdom, it excels in teaching the higher realization that all phenomena lack intrinsic nature, and, having reached the utmost culmination, it fully satisfies sentient beings with the rain of Dharma, like a great cloud.

As it is said in the Saṃpuṭatilaka:

"The site is the ground Very Joyful;
likewise the near-site is Stainless.
The field should be known as Luminous;
Radiant is the near-field.
Chandoha is Manifest;
the near-chandoha is Difficult to Train.
The gathering is Far Gone;
the near-gathering is Immovable.
The charnel ground is Good Intelligence;
the near-charnel ground is Cloud of Dharma.
The ten perfections are the grounds.
The barbarian tongue of the yoginīs,
whatever is taught, the syllable pu and the rest,
should be contemplated outwardly and inwardly."

It is also said that these grounds are to be understood on the basis of their being designated pīlava, near-pīlava and so on, by virtue of the twelve grounds' nature as cause and effect in each successive full arising of the awakening mind. For that very reason, in the Saṃvara, pīlava and near-pīlava are not taught either in the maṇḍala or in the body. In Bhavabhadra, since they are included within the charnel ground and the near-charnel ground, there is a conflict concerning the grounds. In the Saṃpuṭa, Good Intelligence and Cloud of Dharma are classified as the charnel ground and the near-charnel ground.

Here, pīṅa is in the sense of drinking. With "it drinks," kvepa is applied in the sense of the object, giving pīḥ. lunāti, "it accomplishes virtue," gives lava.

Because it drinks and cuts off the heap of afflictions, it is a pīlava, a "drink-cutter." By it one abandons the afflictions through the sequence in which the grounds, beginning with Very Joyful, are brought forth. The twelfth ground, Universal Light, which is buddhahood, abandons without remainder the residual obscurations together with their habitual tendencies, and it is the near-pīlava. In this way, because their results correspond, the twelve, taken from among the forms of awakening whose nature is the ten perfections, should be classified, each as appropriate, as the supports of the yoginīs.

"In the barbarian tongue" means in the language of signs.

For the most part, in the external pīlavas, the pīṭhas and the rest, up to and including the near-pīlava, there arise in due order the yoginīs who are mistresses of the grounds, beginning with the ground of conduct through aspiration (adhimukticaryā). As the glorious Vajraḍāka Tantra says:

"Manifest in land after land,
the self-born abodes endowed with wisdom;
the ḍākinīs that are taught there
are the chief ladies of the vajra maṇḍala."

Therefore the numbers and so on of the places that serve as their supports, the numbers and so on of Pullīramalaya and the others that serve in this way, and the secondary grounds as well, should all be deliberately regarded as the grounds beginning with conduct through aspiration. In more detail: some lesser beings are inclined to be misled by grasping at the external. To remove the attachment to external objects that comes naturally to such beings, the text teaches that the external pīlavas, pīṭhas and the rest are to be regarded as the grounds beginning with conduct through aspiration. For this reason all of this is spoken in intentional language, in the passage beginning "the syllable pu and the rest." "The syllable pu and the rest" means, outwardly, the pīṭhas to be explained, such as Pullīramalaya, which are the levels of a many-storied palace, and inwardly, the awakening mind of the body.

Now the pīṭhas and the rest, taught briefly, are set out definitively in the Two Examinations itself:

"The pīṭha is said to be Jālandhara,
and likewise Oḍiyāna;
the pīṭha is Pūrṇagiri,
and likewise Kāmarūpa too.
The near-pīṭha is said to be Mālava,
and Sindhu, and Nagara.
The kṣetra is said to be Mummuni;
the kṣetra is Kāraṇapāṭaka;
Devīkoṭa likewise is a kṣetra;
the kṣetra is Karmārapāṭaka.
The near-kṣetra is said to be Kulatā,
and likewise Arbuda too,
Godāvarī, and the Snowy Mountain:
this, in brief, is the near-kṣetra.
The chandoha is Harikela,
risen in the midst of the salt ocean,
Lampāka and Kāñcika,
and likewise Saurāṣṭra.
The near-chandoha is Kaliṅga,
and the isle endowed with these;
the near-chandoha is Koṅkaṇa:
so it is declared in brief.
The pīlava is the edge of a village,
and Nagara too is a pīlava;
Caritra and Kośala,
and the town of the Vindhya Maiden.
The near-pīlava lies close to these,
O Vajragarbha, greatly compassionate one.
The charnel ground is a gathering of pretas,
and the ocean shore too is a charnel ground.
A garden and the bank of a pond
are declared to be the near-charnel ground."

Here Kollagiri is Pūrṇagiri. Kollagiri, Pullīramalaya and "the vajra seat" are synonyms. Does the Saṃpuṭatilaka not say,

"The pīṭha is Kollagiri,
and likewise Arbuda too"?

Arbuda, which is taught there as a pīṭha, is here called a near-kṣetra. Likewise Kāmarūpa is here taught as a pīṭha itself. In the Mahāmudrātilaka Tantra too it says, "The pīṭhas are Jālandhara, Auḍiyāna, Pūrṇagiri, and also Kāmarūpa." The Saṃpuṭa says:

"Likewise Kāmarūpa is declared,
and Otre too is declared a kṣetra."

The classification into pīṭhas and so on is also largely the same in the Tilaka and the Vajraḍāka. If one asks how this is not contradictory, the answer is: pīṭha and near-kṣetra do not exclude each other the way hot and cold, or entity and non-entity, do; just as there is no contradiction when a single thing has various names.

Here three, Nagara, Pāṭaliputra and Mālava, are said to be near-kṣetras. The Saṃpuṭa says:

"The near-pīṭha is declared to be Godāvarī,
and Rāmeśvara likewise;
Devīkoṭa is likewise taught,
and Mālava too is likewise declared."

The Mahāmudrātilaka says:

"The near-kṣetra is Mālava,
Nagara and Sindhu,
and Siṃhala likewise,
Devīkoṭa, granter of the supreme;
here they are taught as near to the kṣetra."

Here too, what was just stated is correctly stated.

"Mummuni" means the land of Mummuni. "Kāraṇapāṭaka" refers to Karaṇivaḍa, and so four kṣetras are definitively taught. The Mahāmudrātilaka says, "The kṣetras are Mummuni and Devīkoṭa too." The Saṃpuṭa gives the two explained earlier.

As before, "likewise Arbuda too" refers to the peak of Mount Arbuda. The Mahāmudrātilaka also names two near-kṣetras, Kulatā and Arbuda. The Saṃpuṭa names as near-kṣetras Triśakuni and also Kośala. Here, however, Kośala is taught as a pīlava, as above.

Harikela and the rest, up to Saurāṣṭra, make four chandohas. "In the midst of the salt ocean" describes Harikela. Kaliṅga and the other two are chandohas. The isle endowed with what makes gold is the golden isle; that is, it means Suvarṇadvīpa. In the Saṃpuṭa, Kaliṅga and Lampāka are chandohas, and Suvarṇadvīpa and Malaya are near-chandohas. The Mahāmudrātilaka says that the chandohas are Godāvarī and Harikela, and the near-chandohas the Golden Land and Lampāka.

In this tantra the melāpaka and near-melāpaka are not taught. The Saṃpuṭa says:

"What is declared the abode of pretas,
Gṛhadevatā and Saurāṣṭra,
and Suvarṇadvīpa too, are held to be melāpakas."

The abode of pretas is the town of pretas. Gṛhadevatā too is a melāpaka. The other two are near-melāpakas. The Mahāmudrātilaka says:

"The melāpaka is Karmārapāṭaka;
the near-melāpaka is Suvarṇadvīpa;
Koṅkaṇa and the Vindhya are taught."

Of these, the town of pretas and Gṛhadevatā are here, too, properly included as melāpakas. Saurāṣṭra and Suvarṇadvīpa are taught as chandoha and near-chandoha. Koṅkaṇa and the Vindhya, which the Mahāmudrātilaka gives as near-melāpakas, are here taught as near-chandoha and pīlava.

Therefore, what the Two Examinations teaches as an upamelāpaka should be understood in keeping with the other tantras. It says:

Pīlava is situated at the edge of a village;
Nagara too is a pīlava.

The ground at the edge of the village is the ground at the edge of Nagara. Caritra is the site of Caritra. The Vindhya is the herdsmen's wilderness. Kumārīpaura is the site of the maidens. In the Sampuṭa it says:

Kāraṇapāṭaka and Karmārapāṭaka,
Harikela and the Vindhya,
And Kumārīpaura: these five kinds
Are rightly declared to be pīlava.

Here these are taught as kṣetra. Harikela is itself a chandoha. The upapīlava is the site at its edge, the ground near that pīlava. In the Mahāmudrātilaka the five kinds are named as pīlava, the region of Kashmir, the region of Mön, upapīlava, Nepal, and Kanyakubja. "The Gathering of Hungry Ghosts" (Pretasaṃghāta) means the ground of a charnel ground, that is, a charnel ground. "The ocean shore" means the long shore of a pond. In the Mahāmudrātilaka too, there are two charnel grounds here, and Cari and Kumārīpaura are taught as the upaśmaśāna.

Someone may object: "But in the chapters of the Sampuṭa Tantra, are not Nagara and Sindhu taught as charnel grounds, and Maru and Kulatā explained as the upaśmaśāna?" Indeed, in Lūyipa's Abhisamaya too, they are set out in exactly that way, both in the circle of the maṇḍala and in the body. The Sampuṭatilaka says:

Sindhu and Nagara are charnel grounds;
The Gathering of Hungry Ghosts is an upaśmaśāna;
Likewise the ocean shore,
And the shore of the pond in the pleasure garden,
Are explained as upaśmaśāna.

This too can be explained by bringing both together correctly, making the meaning of both reasoning and the tantras fully clear. For the masters say in the Āmnāyamañjarī: "That Sindhu and Nagara are the śmaśāna and upaśmaśāna, that Maru and Kulatā are principally the Gathering of Hungry Ghosts, and that the ocean shores and the shores of the ponds in pleasure gardens are explained as śmaśāna and upaśmaśāna, is likewise explained as having a hidden intention. Because gods and yoginīs at certain times mingle in these places, for that very reason this was taught afterward: principally, it refers also to the maṇḍala deities born into the families of brahmins and others in lands such as Pullīramalaya, which are included among the twenty-four sites beginning with Pullīramalaya."

Therefore the chapters of Cakrasaṃvara indicate the arrangement of the sites and so on both outwardly and inwardly. Likewise in the other tantras too, the sites and so on are set out outwardly, inwardly, and in other ways as well. The Rigi Aralli Tantra also says:

Eastern Videha, Jambudvīpa,
Western Godānīya,
And northern Kuru are also sites.
In these four sites are the four goddesses,
And on the summit of Mount Meru is the abode of Rigi.

The words "taught everywhere" should be regarded as having the nature of a definite pattern.

Moreover, through a nature of compassion attuned to those to be trained, the Blessed One's body is displayed in many different ways. Those to be trained are divided into the lesser, the middling, the lesser of the great, the middling of the great, and the greatest of the great. Among these, for the sake of the lesser, the outer sites and so on are taught, just as the Stainless Light says: "Here, so that one may wander through the common lands, Jālandhara and the others are taught as the sites and so on. Yet this does not cover everything, for vajraḍākinīs born in the households of brahmins, kṣatriyas, vaiśyas, and śūdras dwell even within a single city, and therefore there are sites and so on in lands such as Tibet and China as well. These are taught within the abridged tantra. In the extensive tantra, the sites and so on are explained specifically for every land and for every city."

For the sake of the middling as well, the sites and so on on the ground of the celestial palace are set out as the goddesses of the maṇḍala. For example, in the maṇḍala of glorious Saṃvara there are eight blue spokes encircled by a blue vajra garland, forming the first, the wheel of mind. On the goddesses' spokes in the east, the north, the west, and the south, as the nature of the sites Pullīramalaya, Jālandhara, Auḍiyāna, and Arbuda, are Pracaṇḍā, Caṇḍākṣī, Prabhāvatī, and Mahānāsā. In the quarters of fire, Nairṛti, wind, and Īśāna, that is, the southeast, southwest, northwest, and northeast, as the embodiment of the upapīṭhas Godāvarī, Rāmeśvara, Devīkoṭa, and Mālava, are Vīramatī, Kharvarī, Laṅkeśvarī, and Drumacchāyā.

Encircled by a garland of red lotuses are eight red spokes, forming the second, the wheel of speech. In the east and north, in the kṣetras Kāmarūpa and Otre, are Airāvatī and Mahābhairavā. In the west and south, in the upakṣetras Triśakuni and Kośala, are Vāyuvegā and Surābhakṣī. In the southeast and southwest, in the chandohas Kaliṅga and Lampāka, are Śyāmādevī and Subhadrā. In the northwest and northeast, in the upachandohas Kāñci and Himālaya, are Hayakarṇā and Khagānanā.

Encircled by a white vajra garland are eight white spokes, forming the third, the wheel of body. In the east and north, in the melāpakas Pretapurī and Gṛhadevatā, are Cakravegā and Khaṇḍarohā. In the west and south, in the upamelāpakas Saurāṣṭra and Suvarṇadvīpa, are Śauṇḍinī and Cakravarmiṇī. In the southeast and southwest, in the charnel grounds Nagara and Sindhu, are Suvīrā and Mahābalā. In the northwest and northeast, in the upaśmaśānas Maru and Kulatā, are Cakravartinī and Mahāvīryā.

These goddesses, Pracaṇḍā and the rest, should be visualized as follows: each is the same color as the wheel that supports her, with one face and three eyes, her face baring its fangs, her forehead marked with a garland of vajras, and her hair loose. Of her two hands, the left holds a skull cup filled with blood, and the right, making a threatening gesture at the wicked, holds a curved knife. She is naked, standing with the right leg extended, wearing the five mudrās, and adorned with a garland of dripping heads.

For the sake of the lesser of the great, the sites and so on are taught within the body, as for example in the Saṃvara maṇḍala.

pu jā o a go rā de ma o tri ko kā la ka hi pre gṛ sau su na si ma ku. At the crown of the head, in Pullīramalaya, is Pracaṇḍā. At the top of the head, in Jālandhara, is Caṇḍākṣī. At the right ear, in Auḍiyāna, is Prabhāvatī. At the nape of the neck, in Arbuda, is Mahānāsā; these are the pīṭhas. At the left ear, in Godāvarī, is Vīramatī. Between the eyebrows, in Rāmeśvara, is Kharvarī. At the two eyes, in Devīkoṭa, is Laṅkeśvarī. At the two shoulders, in Mālava, is Drumacchāyā; these are the upapīṭhas. At the two armpits, in Kāmarūpa, is Airāvatī. At the pair of breasts, in Otre, is Mahābhairavī; these are the kṣetras. At the navel, in Triśakuni, is Vāyuvegā. At the tip of the nose, in Kośala, is Surābhakṣī; these are the upakṣetras. At the mouth, in Kaliṅga, is Śyāmādevī. At the throat, in Lampāka, is Subhadrā; these are the chandohas. At the heart, in Kāñcī, is Hayakarṇā. At the groin, in Himālaya, is Khagānanā; these are the upachandohas. At the sexual organ, in Pretapurī, is Cakravegā. At the anus, in Gṛhadevatā, is Khaṇḍarohā; these are the melāpakas. At the two thighs, in Saurāṣṭra, is Śauṇḍinī. At the two shanks, in Suvarṇadvīpa, is Cakravarmiṇī; these are the upamelāpakas. At the toes, in Nagara, is Suvīrā. At the soles of the feet, in Sindhu, is Mahābalā; these are the charnel grounds. At the big toes, in Maru, is Cakravartinī. At the two knees, in Kulatā, is Mahāvīryā; these are the upaśmaśānas.

Here this is the oral instruction: all of them, puṃ, jāṃ and the rest, are invariably to be taken with the anusvāra. From the transformation of the syllables beginning with puṃ, one should see the sites, the pīṭhas and the rest, within the hollows of the spokes of the wheel, at the crown of the head and the other places. In those pīṭhas and the rest, one should set out and meditate upon the channels that reach each of those places as transformed into the forms of the respective goddesses. For example, just as outwardly the pīṭhas and the rest are nourished by the water of the rivers that lie near them, so too in the body the channels nourish the nails and so on; the correspondence is exact.

With regard to the middling of the great, in the generation stage, even without seed syllables and the like, it is set forth, for the sake of intense devotion, that the pīṭhas and the rest, the crown of the head and the other places as they exist in the body, and the channels in their nature as reaching each of those places, are the vajra goddesses.

With regard to the greatest of the great, the places that are generated are solely inner: the awakening mind, the indivisibility of emptiness and compassion, abiding as the nature that appears distinctly as the body and so on; and they are of the nature of the perfections.

It was with regard to mingling the maṇḍala wheel with oneself that it was said:

"Great wisdom abides in one's own body,
all conceptions utterly abandoned,"

and:

"The lord of the ten grounds, the protector;
these are not to be spoken of by others."

In the glorious later tantra, the Abhidhānottara, it is also said:

"This very one, of all the ḍākinīs,
the certain abode, endowed with loving compassion.
This very one, all the perfect buddhas,
the bodhisattvas, and beings of the three realms,
by their division into twenty-four,
truly abide as the pīṭhas and the rest.
Therefore,
if one possesses that reality, roaming there
only wearies one; there is no need.
If that reality is lacking,
circling them gains nothing at all.
If that reality is present,
what need is there to circle them?
Those external yogins,
by circling, exhaust their elements.
Therefore, abandoning the external,
one should abide in yogic play.
The vajrayoginīs,
who possess the eye of pure wisdom,
look from afar with a mind of compassion
upon the glorious yogins,
and even the four supreme accomplishments
they bestow on them as they wish.
Therefore the venerable wisdom
should be accomplished with great effort.
What is endowed with various exertions
is called the observance of conduct.
This very observance of conduct,
that observance, should be done with joy.
Having done it in whatever way,
endowed with the observance of food and dwelling,
with meditation as the supreme food,
one should abide with an unmaddened mind.
Naked or else not naked,
wherever one is, just as one is,
focusing on one's own chosen deity,
contemplate that alone, single-pointedly.
In about six months that very one,
even if mute, gains mastery.
In the forms of great yogins and yoginīs
they make it clear to him.
Seeing the supreme clarity of wisdom,
the yogins gather close,
just as flocks of geese
enter a delightful lotus pool.
The glorious Vajradhara
becomes manifest to him, without doubt.
Like a magical illusion in the world,
he acts for the benefit of all beings.
Thus the benefit of self and of beings
is accomplished with a pure mind."

For this very reason, the teaching of the pīṭhas and the rest to those of lesser capacity and so on is of provisional meaning. The pīṭhas and the rest that are the very nature of the Very Joyful and the other grounds are of definitive meaning. And whatever presentation of the Very Joyful and the rest is made outwardly, in the maṇḍala wheel, and in the body, that too is of provisional meaning. Translator's notes

The twelve terms are the Hevajra system's sacred sites: pīṭha, upapīṭha, kṣetra, upakṣetra, chandoha, upacchandoha, melāpaka, upamelāpaka, pīlava, upapīlava, śmaśāna and upaśmaśāna. Chandoha and pīlava are kept in Sanskrit because the commentary explains them through the Tibetan words "intention and thought" and "drinking and cutting." The commentary's own correlation and the Saṃpuṭatilaka verse disagree on the order of Difficult to Train and Manifest; both are given as the text has them. For the eighth ground the text names the perfection of meditative concentration, although the usual scheme would have aspiration; the text is translated as it stands. The gloss of lunāti as "accomplishes virtue" is likewise kept as written. "In Bhavabhadra" presumably refers to Bhavabhadra's commentary. The twenty-four sites are named here by their Sanskrit category terms: pīṭha (seat), kṣetra (field), chandoha, melāpaka (meeting place), pīlava, and charnel ground, each with a "near-" counterpart. The text etymologizes pīlava as "drink-cutter."

Tibetan "byed pa'i brang" has been rendered Kāraṇapāṭaka, following the Tibetan sense. Some Sanskrit witnesses of the Hevajra read Kāruṇyapāṭaka. The commentary glosses the name as "Karaṇivaḍa."

"Saṃhaṃla" in the Mahāmudrātilaka quotation is taken to be Siṃhala; this identification is uncertain.

The text says Kaliṅga and the others are "chandohas," although the root verse calls Kaliṅga a near-chandoha. It also lists Nagara and the others as "near-kṣetras" where the root verse has near-pīṭha. Both are translated as written.

"The isle endowed with these" in the root verse translates the Tibetan as it stands. The Sanskrit Hevajra has "the isle endowed with gold," which the commentary's gloss (Suvarṇadvīpa) reflects.

The three lines after the Saṃpuṭa quotation ("The abode of pretas is the town of pretas…") are in seven-syllable verse in the Tibetan and may continue the Saṃpuṭa quotation rather than being the commentator's gloss.

The Tibetan spells the deity's name both Gṛhadeva and Gṛhadevatā; the single form Gṛhadevatā is used. In the wheel of mind the Tibetan names only east, north, and west alongside the "goddesses' spokes." South has evidently dropped out, since four goddesses follow in the standard order ending with Mahānāsā in the south, and I have supplied it. The phrase rendered "both reasoning and the tantras" reads literally "cause and tantra"; rgyu may be a corruption of rgyud. The Tibetan has both gzhon nu ma'i grong and the shortened gzhon nu'i grong for what appears to be the same place, so I have rendered both as Kumārīpaura. The text reads "forms of the view" in "transformed into the forms of the respective goddesses." This has been read as an evident slip for "deity," which differs by one letter.

The sentence on the greatest of the great is garbled in the Tibetan. Its odd "abiding" followed by "and" may hide a reading "the grounds and the perfections." The draft's rendering has been kept.

Several names in the Sampuṭatilaka and Vajraḍāka lists could not be identified with confidence and are translated by meaning: - "the Secret One" (goddess of Auḍiyāna) - "the Fire-Faced One" (field protector of Pūrṇagiri) - "the Excellent Land" (possibly Varendra) - "the tree with matted locks" (perhaps the banyan)

The "lesser, middling, great" grades refer to the graded capacities of practitioners set out earlier in the text. Several goddess and field-protector names in this list could not be identified with confidence and are translated by meaning: She Who Dwells with Donkeys, He with the Veins of the Calf, Snarling, the Hairy One (the Tibetan "byi na spu can" is obscure), Vase-Bearer, Bell-Sounding, She of Damaged Ears, Tinkling-Bell-Sounding, He of Enjoyments, She Who Empowers, and Lord of Men. "The great place of sorrow," "the cane hollow of Janaputra" and "the Milky Place" are likewise literal renderings of place names that could not be securely identified. The text says the greatest practitioners abide "in the generation stage," and this is translated as written. In the colophon, Yangpachen is the Tibetan monastery of that name, not the Indian city Vaiśālī. "The Eight Sky Gods" and "Ridrang Gyé" are tentative renderings of local Tibetan toponyms.
