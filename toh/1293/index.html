About this text

The Illumination of Reality, known in Sanskrit as the Tattvāloka and in Tibetan as Dekho Nanyi kyi Nangwa, is an Indian commentary on the practice of Hevajra, the wrathful deity of the Hevajra Tantra, and it is Toh 1293 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the section of tantra commentaries. It opens with homage to Hevajra and verses praising the dance of Heruka, then explains the meditation step by step, reading each feature of the deities in doctrinal terms: the deity's arms as the sixteen emptinesses, his faces as the eight liberations, the maṇḍala's gates and pillars as the four truths and the eight consciousnesses, together with the goddess Nairātmyā and her circle of yoginīs and the eight charnel grounds. It closes by decoding the tantra's secret "intentional language," the code words for ritual substances and consorts, and a final verse credits the work to the glorious Lokanātha. It was translated into Tibetan by the Kashmiri paṇḍita Somanātha.

Translation

In the language of India: tattvāleka nāma. In the language of Tibet: The Illumination of Reality.

Homage to glorious Hevajra.

Whirling the edges of his skeleton ornaments, he rends the massed clouds along the stainless pathway of the sky; radiating vajra light, he terrifies lions, Indra and Brahmā, and is worthy of praise; like the thunder of the rain clouds at the end of the eon, his sweet and gentle voice resounds as the syllable hūṃ; with dread he terrifies the four māras, and the three realms of existence bow to him: may the dance of Heruka protect you!

Black as a mountain, his two knees planted with a soft jingling sound;
gazing with three eyes fearsome to armies, he strikes terror into the gods;
showing a spreading, rigid, wrathful frown that crushes, his face unbearable to behold:
may glorious Vajraḍāka, like a blue utpala lotus, by his own body protect all embodied beings.

By the permission of Vajrasattva,
out of compassion for sentient beings,
the work called The Illumination of Reality
I shall now write.

In order to set upon the path
minds that have become hard to tame,
I make this earnest effort;
may those who know reality bear with it.

The tantras, the Hevajra Tantra and the others, are the very essence;
having earnestly grasped what the Sugata (the Buddha) taught,
this treatise, The Illumination of Reality, which brings forth manifold good,
I shall teach for the benefit of beings.

First of all, the disciple, facing toward the guru, performs the maṇḍala rite. Then he should make the vow: "Because sentient beings are in sorrow, may I become a buddha for the sake of benefiting beings."

Next, "one should offer oneself to the guru": this is generosity. "Turning back from the mind's entering a path that is not holy": this is moral discipline. "The arising of body, speech and mind": this is patience. "Accomplishment that completely fulfills one's former vow": this is diligence. "The mind become one-pointed": this is meditative concentration. "Knowing with certainty that all of these are without intrinsic nature": this is what receives the name of wisdom. Therefore it is said, "generosity and the other four perfections receive their name by being endowed with wisdom." That is, the bodhisattva, the great being, relies on the six perfections and should entirely abandon the ten nonvirtuous actions.

These too come to rest on body, speech and mind. The body rests on speech, speech rests on mind, and mind rests on space, while space rests on nothing whatsoever. Therefore, with every effort, one should make these three stainless, and further, an explanation of these three should be given: namely, the body is like a reflection, speech is like an echo, and mind is equal to space. These three become completely pure through being endowed with wisdom. Thus, "they are not harmed by the ten nonvirtues" means that the bodhisattva should first practice recollection of karma.

This is the first section, the section on the beginner.

Next, as for the accomplishment of mantra attainments and the like from the four classes of tantra that teach mantra: everything the Blessed One spoke was taught in order to draw in the sentient beings dwelling in the lower realms and set them on the holy path. The teaching, moreover, becomes manifold through distinguishing the lesser, the middling and the superior, and the dispositions of sentient beings, just as a single thing becomes not single. If one asks how this comes about: just as, though the moon is one, it becomes many reflections in vessels filled with water. Therefore, understand that out of all the vehicles there is only one reality, and no other.

Having settled this, in order to fulfill one's former vow, one should strive for awakening day and night in this very life. Then, if one is unable to accomplish it, one should devote oneself to merit, for by the power of that merit one will accomplish awakening in another life in the future.

Those who are possessed of anger, avarice and pride, who are attached to liquor, meat and the like, and who interpret the teaching each in their own way, are certainly unable to teach the characteristics of the cause and result of unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. Because they take on disciples for the sake of wealth, sell the holy Dharma, live by wrong livelihood, and are devoid of blessing, they fall into hell, and all becomes meaningless.

Next, the five elements, the five constituents and the rest are nothing but the abundance of mind, and nothing other. For example, whatever transformations devoid of self arise in a dream are to be understood as like the reflection of the moon, which is beyond grasped and grasper, like an echo, and like a city of gandharvas. Mind too is beyond arising, abiding, perishing, going and coming, and is free of beginning, middle and end; it is not found in any location whatsoever. This very mind, endowed with wisdom, sports in the three realms of existence. Just as a magician displays wonders to people by way of arising, abiding and perishing, so too should mind be known.

If the yogin knows the method thus described, no harm comes to the ground of mind. When the yogin knows mind, he becomes free of attachment, like space, and like a precious wish-fulfilling jewel, his nature being emanation and withdrawal. Whether he dies, or whether he lives again in an instant, what need is there to speak of not being sustained by time?

Merely hearing of such experience is of no use, just as merely reading a medical treatise does not cure a sick person's illness. Therefore the result is perfected through the practice of experience, and for that reason, when mind is realized, everything arises.

It is said that the characteristics of wisdom and of mind come to be known through empowerment and through the kindness of the guru.

This is the second section, the section on the general setting.

Next, the correct bestowal of empowerment on the disciples is to be explained. What is called "scattering the colored powders" is, in its definitive meaning, wisdom, because it represents all beings. There, as for "scattering," because it fully encompasses, it is rajavada. That itself is the maṇḍala of the deities; it is not the inviting of the deities, the requesting of them to depart, and so on. Why is that? Because the buddhas, the Blessed Ones, are not known to come or to go, just as in a dream there is no arising, abiding or ceasing. For that reason one should not perform the inviting of the deities, the requesting of them to depart, and so on. Through meditative concentration that has an object, one is not liberated from the bonds of saṃsāra; what need is there to speak of a thing that does not exist? Therefore, by not apprehending any thing at all, it extends to all. The mind that is freed from both thing and non-thing is what is called buddha. To such a principal one, whose nature is method and wisdom, one should make offering with mantras, mudrās and the like.

If one asks how the offering is to be made: making the pure and the impure mind one is mantra. Likewise, the birth of creatures is recitation, and because it seals the three worlds it is mudrā, and that is called wisdom. Placing that maṇḍala of wisdom before oneself, one should bestow the various empowerments on the disciples. Apart from empowerment there is no attainment; therefore, since they desire the fruit of buddhahood, one should bestow empowerment. The disciple too should make this request:

Sunk in the mire of saṃsāra,
covered by the terrible misdeeds I have done,
so that I may emerge from this,
Tathāgata, please protect me!

Then the guru should bestow the seven empowerments on the disciple: the water empowerment, the crown empowerment, the vajra empowerment, the master empowerment, the name empowerment, the secret empowerment, and the wisdom-gnosis empowerment.

First of all, the nature of mirror-like gnosis is Vairocana; because he is free of attachment, he is Vairocana. Conferring that is called empowerment: it is the water empowerment. Next, the nature of the utterly pure sphere of phenomena (dharmadhātu) is Akṣobhya; because he cannot be shaken, he is Akṣobhya. He is to be held upon the head: this is the crown empowerment. Next, the nature of the gnosis of equality is Ratnasambhava; because he arises without attachment to any of the two whatsoever, he is Ratnasambhava. Empowering by him is the vajra empowerment. Next, the nature of discriminating gnosis is Amitābha. "Limitless" means immeasurable, and "light" means light rays: the one in whom these are present is Amitābha. Empowering by him is called the master empowerment. Next, the nature of the gnosis that accomplishes deeds is Amoghasiddhi. "Accomplishing the aim" means accomplishment together with its fruit. Conferring a name by him is called the name empowerment. Vajrasattva is the nature of the five gnoses. "Vajra" is indivisible emptiness, and "sattva" is compassion, the oneness of the three realms of existence: thus Vajrasattva. The secret, because it gives rise to the two without exception, establishes nondual gnosis; empowering by it is the secret empowerment. As for "wisdom-gnosis," wisdom is the sphere of phenomena without intrinsic nature, the element of space, and gnosis is what is beyond grasped and grasper. Empowering by it is the wisdom-gnosis empowerment. As it is said:

Emptiness is spoken of as wisdom,
and the clarity of compassion is method;
from joining the two as one,
the supreme nectar flows down.

Whatever other empowerments there are should be understood in just this way. It is said that, whether outwardly or inwardly, their meaning should be settled once by bestowing them correctly.

This is the third section, the section on empowerment.

Now, in order to settle its meaning, I shall write of the division of the three channels. lalanā, which flows from the left and has the nature of the āli vowels, is method. rasanā, which flows from the right and has the nature of the kāli consonants, is wisdom. In the center, the oneness of the two, is avadhūtī. In lalanā is the Dharma body, in rasanā the enjoyment body, and in avadhūtī the emanation body, according to the division into body, speech and mind. In the three channels and the four cakras, through nondual union, there is great bliss. Of these four, loving-kindness pertains to the emanation body, compassion to the Dharma body, joy to the enjoyment body, and equanimity to great bliss.

Likewise, it arises through four moments: the varied, maturation, consummation, and that which is free of characteristics. There are the Four Truths: suffering, origin, cessation and the path. There are the four realities: the reality of self, the reality of mantra, the reality of the deity, and the reality of gnosis. There are the four joys: joy, supreme joy, the joy of cessation, and coemergent joy. There are the four schools: the Sthaviras, the Sarvāstivādins, the Sāṃmitīyas and the Mahāsāṃghikas.

If one asks how loving-kindness and the rest come about: the mind that comes to abide in the equal taste of all things is loving-kindness. The mind whose nature is one's former aspiration is compassion. The oneness of these is joy. The mind that depends on nothing at all is equanimity.

If one asks what the emanation body and the rest are like: wherever all sentient beings without exception are said to be born, there is the emanation body, and that is called emanation. Or else, because it sends forth, it is emanation. The Dharma body, because it holds its own characteristic, is the characteristic of mind. The enjoyment body is so called because it enjoys the six tastes, sweet and the rest. Through the division of the three bodies these come about, and through fully knowing them there is great bliss.

In these cakras the moments, the varied and the rest, should be known. If one asks what the varied and the rest are like: because embracing, kissing (cumbana) and the like are made varied, it is the varied. Through enjoying the bliss of gnosis, it is maturation. Because it satisfies everything, it is without aspect, that is, the very absence of intrinsic nature. Because it has become free of attachment, it is free of characteristics.

From these moments the joys and the rest arise. Joy pertains to the varied. Supreme joy pertains to maturation. Varied joy pertains to consummation. Coemergent joy pertains to the moment free of characteristics. Joy gives rise to a little bliss; supreme joy is a bliss that surpasses it; varied joy is varied attachment itself; and what remains beyond all of them is coemergent joy. With the first there is desire for the tangible; with the second, attachment to bliss; with the third, the waning of attachment; and with the fourth, supreme bliss. Between the supreme and the varied is the coemergent; it is free of these three, it cannot be taught by anyone, and it is not obtained from anything. As it is said:

It is known through reliance on the guru
and through one's own merit.

In these joys the Four Truths, suffering and the rest, should be known. If one asks how these too are known: in the first arising there is the truth of suffering. In the supreme arising there is the truth of origin. In consummation, through the absence of intrinsic nature, there is the truth of cessation. Therefore, through the coemergent itself there is the truth of the path.

Next, the four realities. Because joy arises, there is the reality of self. Because it speaks secretly, in the supreme joy there is the reality of mantra. Because it conceives the nature of emptiness, in consummation there is the reality of the deity. Through meditating on the dawning of the coemergent, there is the reality of gnosis.

Next, the four schools. In the first joy are the Sthaviras. In the second joy are the Sarvāstivādins. Of the nature of the varied are the Sāṃmitīyas. Of the nature of the coemergent are those called the Mahāsāṃghikas. All of these come to be produced from the joys. These too should be known in the cakras of emanation, enjoyment, equality and great bliss. Emanation, Dharma and enjoyment are lalanā, rasanā and avadhūtī. All of these, through their equal taste, are great bliss. That too should be known, outwardly and inwardly, as the play of the king of mind.

The gnosis graced by the guru
should be taught to the disciple.
In terms of the ultimate,
it lies between the two channels.
Uncontrived and not impermanent,
thing and non-thing, undefiled,
beyond grasped and grasper,
that in which the three realms arise,
and which, benefiting the three realms,
transcends all verbal expression:
the person who has obtained empowerment
should come to know this gnosis.
Then, in order to realize that reality,
I shall explain the practice.

Through attachment to the mudrā whose nature is wisdom, the bliss that arises in the mind arises as the first joy. Through embracing her, the senses become one and the bliss increases: this is supreme joy. Being free of characteristics, like space, through the absence of intrinsic nature that satisfies everything, is varied joy. What passes beyond these three is the joy called coemergent, and this too the yogin should know in the state of suchness.

Then, so that those of dull faculties may become buddhas, the guru should teach the characteristic of gnosis through union with an outer wisdom consort. Or else, as it is said:

caṇḍalī blazing from the navel
burns the five Sugatas;
having burned Locanā and the others,
haṃ is burned, and the hare-marked moon drips.

Here, caṇḍa is wisdom and āli is method; having the nature of the āli vowels and the kāli consonants, she is the essence of lalanā and rasnā. These, whose nature is method and wisdom, are caṇḍalī. "From the navel" means in space, that is, in the avadhūtī in the middle of the two. When she blazes, "the five Sugatas" are the five aggregates, and it is said that she burns them. Then "Locanā and the others" means that, having burned the five elements, they become without aspect. "haṃ is burned" means having burned self and what belongs to self. When this has come about, "the hare-marked moon" is the awakening mind, and it drips for the sake of all sentient beings, and this is attained by oneself. For example, a flower is grasped merely by smelling it with the nose, yet the scent is not seen anywhere and does not come near in any form. If one asks what it is, then: just as it comes to be known by oneself, so too is the coemergent taught. The mind that abides in such a coemergent is like a city of gandharvas, like an illusion and a magical display, like a mirror and a reflection, and like an echo; it displays emanation and withdrawal, the varied, and the rest.

This is the fourth section, the section on correct bestowal.

Now the wisdom women are to be assigned. Because through the absence of intrinsic nature she shows distinctions, she is the sister, assigned to the eye. Because she is untouched, she is the daughter-in-law, assigned to the ear. Because, like the transformations born of a dream, she gives birth to beings, she is the mother, assigned to the nose. Because she destroys all things, she is the daughter, assigned to the tongue. Because she bends the hard mind as one bends bamboo, she is the bamboo worker, assigned to touch. Because she attends to bliss, she is the wife, assigned to slander. Because she makes the vajra Dharma free of obscuration, she is the leatherworker, assigned to envy. kaṃ is space, and because in it there is the discipline of protecting, she is kapāli, assigned to the region above. Because she makes the fringe of all conceptual thoughts without exception her garment and wanders, she is Śabarī, assigned to water. caṇḍa is wisdom and āli is the source of all phenomena; she is called caṇḍalī, assigned to fire. Because she does not know, she is the low-caste woman, assigned to wind. Because, like a false drawing, she establishes the threefold world of beings, she is the dancer, assigned to space.

Because she makes the variety of beings false, she is the painter, assigned to form. Because she tears apart all afflictions without exception, she is the scraper, assigned to feeling. Because she works constantly on the tusk of the elephant of the mind, she is the ivory carver, assigned to perception. Because she shelters beings with the parasol of non-objectification, she is the parasol maker, assigned to formations. Because she slays ignorance, she is the butcher, assigned to consciousness. "Arrow" means the five senses, and because she makes them one, she is the arrow maker, assigned to generosity. Because she contracts all conceptual thoughts without exception, and this discipline is present in her, she is kucini, assigned to moral discipline. Because she gives delight, she is nandi, assigned to patience. Because she accomplishes the two without remainder, she is the niece, assigned to diligence. Because, like a reflection, she displays variety, she is the dancer, assigned to meditative concentration. Because with the net of the absence of intrinsic nature she binds the conceptual mind that spreads outward, she is the fisherwoman, assigned to wisdom. Because she seals the true phenomena, she is the mother's mother-in-law, assigned to loving-kindness. Because she strings varied flowers on the thread of non-objectification, she is the garland maker, assigned to compassion. Because she cuts down the tree of conceptual thought, she is the woodcutter, assigned to joy. Because she is without any obscuration whatsoever, she is the eater of leftovers, assigned to equanimity. Because she holds the two sides with the churning stick, she is the potter, assigned to body. Because by her own taste she ripens beings, she is the wine seller, assigned to speech. The brahmin is the gnosis of suchness, and because she brings about wisdom, she is the brahmin woman, assigned to mind. So it is said.

These mudrās, by their nature, become ḍākinīs, and they are to be set down as follows. For form, Gaurī. For sound, Caurī. For smell, Vetālī. For taste, ghasma. For touch, Bhūcarī. For mind, Khecarī. For earth, Pukkasī. For water, Śabarī. For fire, caṇḍalī. For wind, Ḍombī. For space, Nairātmyā. For desire, Vetālī. For aversion, Vajriṇī. For delusion, Vajrā. For slander, Secret Gaurī. For envy, Vajraḍākinī. For form, Vajrā. For feeling, Caurī. For perception, Vāriyoginī. For formations, Vajraḍākinī. For consciousness, Nairātmyā. For generosity, Gaurī. For moral discipline, Caurī. For patience, Vetālī. For diligence, ghasma. For meditative concentration, Bhūcarī. For wisdom, Khecarī. For loving-kindness, Locanā. For compassion, māmakī. For joy, Pāṇḍaravāsinī. For equanimity, Tārā. For body, Bhūcarī. For speech, Khecarī. For mind, Nairātmyā.

Having shown the various families of ḍākinīs for these goddesses, their Tathāgatas are to be stated. For form and the rest: Vairocana, Akṣobhya, Amitābha, Amoghasiddhi, Ratnasambhava and Vajrasattva. For earth and the rest: Vairocana and the others. For desire and the rest: Amitābha, Akṣobhya, Vairocana, Ratnasambhava and Amoghasiddhi. For form, feeling and the rest: Vairocana, Ratnasambhava, Amitābha, Amoghasiddhi and the Tathāgata Akṣobhya. For generosity and the rest: Vairocana, Akṣobhya, Amitābha, Amoghasiddhi, Ratnasambhava and Vajrasattva. For loving-kindness and the rest: Vairocana, Akṣobhya, Amitābha and Amoghasiddhi. For body and the rest: Vairocana, Amitābha and Akṣobhya.

Now the sealing of the ḍākinīs is to be explained. Gaurī and Pukkasī are sealed by Akṣobhya. Śabarī and Caurī are sealed by Vairocana. Vetālī and caṇḍalī are sealed by Ratnasambhava. ghasmarī and Ḍombī are sealed by Amitābha. Amoghasiddhi and Ratnasambhava are sealed by Amitābha. Vairocana and Amitābha are sealed by Akṣobhya. That one is sealed by Vajrasattva. All the ḍākinīs are sealed by one another through union with the Tathāgatas. Then Nairātmyā is, by nature, equal to Vajrasattva.

The single reality of all these is the glorious hero Heruka, who has the form of the five great elements; the five Tathāgatas whose nature is the form of desire and the rest, holding the mudrās and pervading the six objects; who, having become the nature of the six objects, perfects the six perfections; surrounded by the sixteen emptinesses; adorned with the ten virtues; subduing the four māras; teaching the Four Truths; having the nature of the four bodies; entered into the center of the four realities; bestowing the four joys; having the nature of the four moments; abiding in the four abodes of Brahmā; the lord joined in union with Nairātmyā, who sports in the three realms of existence.

This is the fifth section, the section settling method and wisdom.

Now I shall write the stage of generating the circle of Nairātmyā:

Having first meditated on a sun before one, arising from repha,
upon that sun, from hūṃ, a crossed vajra arises;
with that very vajra one should meditate
on the fence and the binding net.

First of all, one meditates on a corpse whose nature is the sphere of phenomena, and then one should meditate on Heruka himself. The circle born of the source of phenomena (dharmodaya), shaped like the fire maṇḍala joined face to face: in its center one should meditate on the corpse, whose nature is that of the fifteen seats. Upon it one should imagine a moon disc arising from the first vowel, and upon that a sun disc arising from repha. These two, having the nature of mirror-like gnosis and the gnosis of equality, are the essence of method and wisdom. Through their being perfectly joined, this is the seat of Vajrasattva.

In that maṇḍala, by that same sequence, one should meditate on the Blessed One, arisen from the syllable hūṃ, with one face, holding a vajra in his right hand and a skull cup in his left, adorned with a garland of heads, his body the color of crystal, embracing the body of Nairātmyā.

Then, as before, through the sequence of the arising of moon and sun, the fifteen syllables beginning with the letter a become Nairātmyā and the others. In the center is Nairātmyā. In the east is Vajrā. In the quarter of Yama, the south, is Gaurī. In the quarter of Varuṇa, the west, is Vetālī. In the quarter of the yakṣas, the north, is Vajraḍākinī. Outside, at the eastern gate, is Gaurī. In the south is Caurī. In the west is Vetālī. In the north is ghasmarī. In the quarter of Īśāna, the northeast, is Pukkasī. In the quarter of Agni, the southeast, is Śabarī. In the quarter of Nairṛti, the southwest, is caṇḍalī. In the quarter of Vāyu, the northwest, is Ḍombī. Above is Khecarī, and below is Bhūcarī. One should meditate on these goddesses as having one face and two arms, black in color and fierce, with red eyes, bearing the five mudrās, holding a curved knife in the right hand and in the left a skull cup and a khaṭvāṅga, wearing a tiger skin as their garment, with tawny hair standing on end.

The curved knife is what cuts away faults such as the māras. The skull cup is what destroys the conceptual thought of meditation and meditator, and its being filled with blood is the devouring of the four māras. The khaṭvāṅga is emptiness, and the corpse is method. Because it devours the three kinds of beings, whose nature is attachment, aversion and the rest, it is a tiger, and its skin they wear as a garment. Whatever yogin meditates on a circle such as this will swiftly attain accomplishment; so it is said.

All those goddesses, having been completed by the sequence just described, dissolve into the venerable Blessed One Vajrasattva and become of his very nature. He is the moon with the nature of fifteen digits, that is, the awakening mind; and that awakening mind, whose nature is both conventional and ultimate, arises through the method of the maṇḍala circle and so on, and through the stages of blessing. Its fifteen digits are held to be the fifteen yoginīs. How do they come about? Because it performs the function, the moon is the awakening mind, and it is called great bliss in the form of the vowels (āli). Therefore each goddess is generated as having the nature of method and wisdom. The conventional is the compounded; therefore the words "like the kunda flower (white jasmine)" refer to the abiding of method itself. The ultimate is the discerned; this is what is meant by "the form of bliss is called the wisdom consort." This is the section on Nairātmyā, the sixth.

Next, I shall explain the practice of the sixteen-armed Blessed One, who has become of nondual nature. The arms are taught as the sixteen emptinesses. What are they? The emptiness of the internal, the emptiness of the external, the emptiness of the internal and external, the emptiness of emptiness, the emptiness of the great, the emptiness of the ultimate, the emptiness of the conditioned, the emptiness of the unconditioned, the emptiness of what transcends the extremes, the emptiness of what has neither beginning nor end, the emptiness of all phenomena, the emptiness of own characteristics, the emptiness of the unapprehendable, the emptiness of non-entities, the emptiness of essential nature, and the emptiness of the essential nature of non-entities. These sixteen emptinesses are said to be the sixteen arms.

The four means of gathering are the four feet. What are they? Generosity, pleasant speech, beneficial conduct, and consistency in aims; these are the feet. The seven limbs of awakening are the skin. What are they? Mindfulness, diligence, joy, pliancy, meditative absorption, equanimity, and discernment of phenomena; these are the skin. For the flesh there is Pukkasī, for the blood Śabarī, for the semen caṇḍalī, and for the marrow and brain Ḍombī; these are imagined as the flesh, the blood, the semen, and the marrow and brain. The bones are the four truths: suffering, origin, cessation and the path become the bones.

The eight liberations are the eight faces. What are they? The limbs of samādhi are the three samādhis: the supreme king of maṇḍalas, and the yoga of the drop, that is, the yoga of the subtle. The five mudrās are the five views: the view of the transitory collection, wrong view, the view that holds to extremes, holding views as supreme, and the view that holds discipline and vows as supreme; these become the five mudrās. As for "madana": it is that all three have become deluded, and these five senses have become one. "Strength" is the class of attachment and the rest. "Meat" is the evil mind bent on destroying intention. "Malayaja" is what makes method and wisdom one, and that itself is the meeting. The dog is entering the path of no intrinsic nature. The corpse is the mind, and "dripping" means that it drips through the intention of wisdom. The ornament of bones is the beautiful. "Dustless" is the mind free of the dust of habitual tendencies. "Untouchable" is the vajra of the mind. "Ḍiṇḍima" is what possesses the vajra of sound. "Bhañjama" is freedom from stains. "Malatindana" is the nature of wisdom, which burns away the stains of the mind. "Food" is what is found in the mind of moral discipline. The "satisfier" is wisdom. In the word "kapāla," "ka" means what protects space. The lotus vessel is what has become a vessel for wisdom. Camphor is great flesh. "Born of śāli rice" is that in which there is no conception of the parts of the limbs of both, and so it is free of obscuration. The union of the two organs is the perfect union of emptiness and compassion. "Kunduru" is what is divided in the manner of the indivisible. "Bolaka" is the attainment of wisdom. The lotus is sihla that has become the nature of space. "Kakkolā" means the bestower of supreme bliss. "Sandi" is gathering in, and what is called "apaśa" is what gives rise to it; thus it is called sanadhyabaśa, intentional language.

the union of supreme compassion, which brings benefit to the many;
by whatever I have gained that has become the nature of nectar, may the three classes of beings
in half an instant come to the unsurpassed state of Hevajra.

By the glorious Lokanātha,
whose heart was moistened by compassion,
for the benefit of the many,

Translator's notes

The Sanskrit title is given as it appears in the Tibetan spelling; the Tibetan rendering points to Tattvāloka. In the second verse, the onomatopoeia for the jingling of the knees' ornaments is rendered in English. The sentence about dying or living again in an instant and "not being sustained by time" is obscure in the Tibetan and is rendered literally. This text pairs lalanā with method and rasanā with wisdom, the reverse of the more common Hevajra assignment; it is translated as it stands. The third of the four moments is written rnam par nyid pa, which makes no sense here; it is read as rnam par nyed pa, "consummation" (vimarda), the standard third moment. In the list of the joys, the third joy here is "varied joy" where the earlier list gave the joy of cessation; the text is followed as it stands. The assignment of the wife to "slander" follows the Tibetan, which is unexpected in a list of senses and elements. The final word, written na ma mkha', is read as nam mkha', "space," which the list of elements requires. The identity of the "mother's mother-in-law" (ma'i sgyug mo) as a kinship term is uncertain, and rdo rje can ma, the ḍākinī for aversion, is rendered Vajriṇī with some doubt. The corpse-burner goddess (ro sreg ma) assigned to earth is identified as Pukkasī, matching the standard Hevajra pairing of the four elements with Pukkasī, Śabarī, Caṇḍālī and Ḍombī. The lists of Tathāgatas and the sealing scheme, including the sealing of buddhas by buddhas, are translated as they stand. The inner western goddess is given here as Vetālī, though Hevajra sources usually place Vāriyoginī there; the text is translated as it stands. The two lines on the conventional and the ultimate allude to the Hevajra Tantra's "the conventional is like the kunda flower, the ultimate has the form of bliss." In the list of nine dramatic sentiments, "that of Yama" stands where the classical list has the compassionate (karuṇa), and the eighth liberation reads literally "the cessation of what is known through perception," rather than the usual cessation of perception and feeling; both are translated as written. The list of the three samādhis names only "the supreme king of maṇḍalas" and "the yoga of the drop, that is, the yoga of the subtle"; it is translated as it stands. Among the ten non-virtues, "the intent to torment" stands where lists usually have covetousness. Hāra, "necklace," is glossed by the Tibetan as "destroying," playing on the Sanskrit verb "to take away." The verse line "free of doubt" reads the first occurrence, spelled so ma nyi, as som nyi, "doubt," as in the following line. Vetālī's emblem is written as a frog here, where Hevajra sources usually give a tortoise; it is translated as written. The tree names barkati and arjaga are given as the Tibetan spells them (arjaga may stand for the arjuna tree). Several charnel-ground and cloud names that could not be confidently matched to Sanskrit forms are translated by meaning. The list of the field protectors' faces has ten items, including "a cloud," for eight protectors, and the lists of colors are likewise uneven; all are translated as they stand. The code words of the intentional language are given as the Tibetan spells them, and their spellings vary between the verse and the commentary (kaleṃjara, kaliṃja, kalijara; mālatinta, malatindana). The commentary's first heading reads mang na, evidently standing for madana, the code word for liquor in the verse. The verse line on the lotus family appears to name only "a family" for the brahmin woman. The closing etymology explains sandhyābhāṣā, "intentional language," spelled in the Tibetan as sanadhyabaśa. Lokanātha in the closing verse appears to name the author.
