About this text

Toh 592 in the Kangyur is the Noble Dhāraṇī Called the Invincible White Parasol Arisen from the Uṣṇīṣa of the Tathāgata, known in Sanskrit as the Āryatathāgatoṣṇīṣasitātapatrāparājitanāmadhāraṇī and in Tibetan as the Phags pa de bzhin gshegs pa'i gtsug tor nas byung ba'i gdugs dkar po can gyi gzungs. It is a dhāraṇī text — a genre of scripture presenting sacred formulas of great protective and purificatory power — and it belongs to the broad class of tantric and incantation literature within the Kangyur. The text opens with an extended series of homages to the Three Jewels, to vast numbers of buddhas, and to the various grades of noble beings on the path to liberation, establishing the devotional and doctrinal context in which the dhāraṇī's power is grounded. This version was finalized by the paṇḍita Parahitabhadra and the Tibetan translator Gzu Dga' Rdor, who collated their work against an old manuscript preserved at the temple of Amṛtabhava in Kashmir, attesting to the care taken in establishing an authoritative text.

Translator's notes

The colophon names the translator as "Gzu Dga' Rdor," a contracted form, likely Gzu dGa' ba'i rdo rje. The temple name rendered as "Amṛtabhava" (Tibetan: bdud rtsi 'byung gnas) in Kashmir is a Sanskrit reconstruction based on the Tibetan. The phrase "divine sages who dispel curses" refers to brahminical ṛṣis credited with both harmful and beneficial mantra power, a standard motif in this genre of dhāraṇī literature. The "Blessed Fierce One, sovereign who averts ascetic practices" is almost certainly Śiva in his Rudra aspect, paired with his retinue. The "Blessed Nārāyaṇa who paid homage with the five great mudrās" is a well-attested formula in Uṣṇīṣasitātapatrā literature. The "King of Weapons, Steadfast Army" (dpa' brtan pa'i sde mtshon cha'i rgyal po) is a less commonly cited buddha name; the rendering follows the Tibetan literally. The epithets in the verse section list a series of wrathful and semi-wrathful goddess figures associated with Tārā and the Uṣṇīṣa Sitātapatra cycle. Several names resist fully transparent rendering because they function as tantric epithets rather than descriptive phrases; the translation renders their literal meaning while preserving the liturgical character.

The term rendered "skies-corner spirits" for nam gru'i gdon is unusual. Nam gru likely refers to spirits of the cardinal or intermediate directions of the sky; this rendering is provisional.

The passage ends mid-sentence in the Tibetan provided; the translation reflects this. The passage is from the Uṣṇīṣasitātapatrā dhāraṇī cycle. The mantra section from "Oṃ asitānalārka" onward is a transliteration of Sanskrit mantric syllables into Tibetan script; these are rendered here by reconstructing the underlying Sanskrit forms as closely as the Tibetan orthography permits, following standard scholarly editions of this dhāraṇī. Minor orthographic variations in the Tibetan are expected and do not affect the reconstruction materially.

"The Great Lord of Cattle" renders ཕྱུགས་བདག་ཆེན་པོ, a title that in this apotropaic context designates Śiva in his form as Paśupati, lord of animals and herdsmen.

"The Son of Non-Craving" renders སྲེད་མེད་ཀྱི་བུ, a standard Tibetan gloss for Indra (Śakra), whose name is etymologized as "son of non-craving" in the Tibetan tradition.

"Bhṛṅgiriṭi" is one of Śiva's attendants; "the Bliss-Giving Lord" is Nandīśvara; "the Lord of the Assembly" is Gaṇeśa — all members of Śiva's retinue invoked here for their mantra-authority to be ritually severed. The Tibetan passage is a continuation of a Sanskrit dhāraṇī text interleaved with Tibetan prose. The opening lines are Sanskrit mantric vocatives rendered in Tibetan transliteration and are kept in romanized Sanskrit in the translation, consistent with the preceding passage.

The term grulbum is a specific class of demon in Tibetan demonology with no standard Sanskrit equivalent; it is left in transliteration. Similarly srul po and lus srul po denote classes of zombie or corpse-animated spirits; these are rendered descriptively.

The insects treykhuka and trailatāka are transliterations of Sanskrit terms for specific harmful creatures that are not fully identified in the commentarial literature; they are kept in transliteration.

The phrase yi ga 'chus pa is literally "convulsion or contortion of the syllables," likely referring to a condition affecting speech or the jaw; it is rendered descriptively.
