About this text

Toh 377 in the Kangyur is a tantric text known in Sanskrit as the Ākhyāta Tantrottara Vajra Vāhya Abhidhānāda Vārāhya Vibodhanam, and in Tibetan as a chapter on the manifest awakening of Vajravārāhī drawn from the Subsequent Tantra of the Explicit Explanation of Vajravārāhī. It belongs to the class of yoginī tantras within the Vajrayāna tradition, centering on the deity Vajravārāhī and presenting a condensed yoga practice said to fulfill all desired aims, illuminating the guru lineage for the benefit of practitioners. The text takes the form of a tantric chapter in verse, describing esoteric teachings associated with the ḍākinīs and the attainment of siddhi for yogins. It was translated into Tibetan by Chingwa Yönten Bar and subsequently reviewed and finalized in consultation with two paṇḍitas of that period.

Homage to the buddhas.

Then the yoga to be explained,
the one that accomplishes every desired aim,
the last of the last,
the binding net of the ḍākinīs,
the sovereign of Vajravārāhī,
the one who bestows attainment on yogins,
the very essence of all yoginīs,
the condensed yoga,
for the benefit of the practitioner
it illuminates the guru lineage.

Free from outer conceptual elaboration,
ever abiding in the Dharma of samaya,
free from conduct bounded by fear,
having abandoned all miserliness,
without counting of dates or stars,
without the ritual of the restorative fast,
without distinction of pure and impure or clean,
without the practice of water purification,
liberated from times and dates,
having abandoned conduct tied to cleanliness,
knowing one's own nature, mantra, and yoga,
striving for the welfare of all sentient beings,
in mountain gorges, dense forests, and caves,
on riverbanks, in gatherings,
on the shores of the great ocean,
beneath a single tree, on open ground, in an empty house,
in the abode of the Mātṛkās, in a charnel ground,
in the finest and varied pleasure groves,
in a temple and at a stūpa site,
in a home, or at a crossroads,
the practitioner should accomplish the siddhi
that supremely grants all desired aims
at a place that has been indicated
or wherever the mind takes delight,
and there, for one who wishes to keep the various vows,
the true attainment will be perfectly accomplished.

Through the connection with the aspiration of those to be trained,
there are many divisions of meditation.

Anointing the ground with the nectar of the five wisdoms,
spreading flowers in a heap with diligence in the perfection of patience,
on the seats of the various mudrās
one should rest in perfect meditative equipoise.
Focusing on the perfection of meditative concentration,
with one-pointed mind,
one should meditate on the four divine abodes,
the four means of gathering disciples,
and the ten virtues from which happiness arises for all sentient beings.

All phenomena are to be seen as empty,
and the self, outer and inner, perceived as such.
Then, resting in mind-only,
it is the supreme accumulation for awakening.

In the sky-space of the heart, at the center of a lotus,
one should meditate on a solar disc.
The intrinsic nature of the five wisdoms,
the supreme reality, is seen there.
From the brilliant array of multicolored rays of excellent nature,
pervading all manifestations and elaborations,
the previously accomplished ones are truly invoked.
In the delightful expanse of the sky,
the sacred maṇḍala is beheld,
and blessings too are bestowed.
Seen like a sesame seed pod,
all wrathful goddesses,
buddhas, bodhisattvas,
guru bodhisattvas,
the lord of offerings and the making of offerings,
the nine forms of the goddess are seen.
The emanations of the buddha assembly unfold.
The queen of wrath, the yoginī,
and the sentient beings of the six classes of existence,
all of these are established as buddhas themselves.

In the middle of what is called the sky-expanse,
one enters into and beholds suchness itself.
Until the heart of awakening is reached,
in every direction one takes refuge.
All beings without exception
are to be established at the level of buddhahood.
The practitioner should make supplication to that,
and always seek refuge.

Toward the five aggregates, the pride
of a good and noble mind should be generated.
Form, feeling, perception,
formations, and consciousness itself:
Brūṃ, Aṃ, Hrīḥ, Ho, Hūṃ —
Vairocana, Vajra, the Sun,
likewise Padma, Lord of Dance,
and the one renowned as Vajrarāja —

Likewise, the supreme Vajrasattva (bodhisattva),
who is none other than all the Tathāgatas —
the glorious Vajra Heruka.

Eye, ear, and nose,
body, speech, and likewise mind:
Oṃ Hūṃ Trāṃ Āḥ Khaṃ Hūṃ.

Delusion, aversion, envy,
attachment, and miserliness as well —
over all the sense bases, Vajra sovereignty.

Earth, water, fire,
wind, and space itself:
Lāṃ Vāṃ Pāṃ Tāṃ Khāṃ —

she who binds, slays,
who summons and who dances,
the goddess of the lotus-net
is like the sky itself, immeasurable.

Thus over the aggregates, elements, and sense bases, the ordinary yogin's intrinsic nature is thoroughly purified in its natural state. One should generate this pure pride.

After that, one takes refuge in the Three Jewels and transforms the body into a body of mantra. Having meditated on oneself as Heruka, one acts for the benefit of all sentient beings. Having attained the state of Heruka, one should establish all sentient beings in the unsurpassed rank.

Oṃ svabhāvaśuddhaḥ sarvadharmāḥ svabhāvaśuddho 'haṃ.

This is recited three times.

Like a garland of rays from the sun,
all things in every direction
are to be seen as fence-posts and nets
arising from those rays.
Consecrate the vajra ground.
Imagine the enclosure of an arrow-fence above,
and beyond that a net of blazing vajra-fire —
extending far outward, conceive this well.

Oṃ medinī vajrī bhava vajrabandha hūṃ.
Oṃ vajraprākāra hūṃ vaṃ hūṃ.
Oṃ vajrapañjara hūṃ vaṃ hūṃ.
Oṃ vajravitāna hūṃ khaṃ hūṃ.
Oṃ śarajāla traṃ saṃ traṃ.
Oṃ vajrajvala analārka hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ.

Joining the index fingers and thumbs, snapping the fingers, one sends them out to the ten directions:

Oṃ sumbha ni sumbha hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ — to the east.
Oṃ gṛhṇa gṛhṇa hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ — to the south.
Oṃ gṛhṇāpaya gṛhṇāpaya hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ — to the west.
Oṃ ānaya ho bhagavān vajra hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ — to the south.

With this king of mantras, one drives a stake into the heads of all obstructors, turning clockwise and not counterclockwise.

Oṃ gha gha ghātaya ghātaya, sarvaduṣṭān vighnan hūṃ phaṭ. Kīlikīlaya, sarva pāpaṃ hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ. Vajrakīlaya vajradhāra ājñāpayati. Sarvaduṣṭān vighnan kāyavākacittaṃ vajrakīlaya hūṃ phaṭ.

One strikes. Gazing upon the vajra stake with a wrathful look, the vajra practitioner strikes it rhythmically and irregularly in turn:

Oṃ vajramuṅgara vajrakīlā koṭaya hūṃ phaṭ.

There is no māra, and there is no action of māra.
It is as though one is bound by one's own mind.
Whoever tames their own mind —
from that point, where could māra abide?

The vajra of wisdom realizing emptiness
is to be recalled with greater care than meditative equipoise itself.

Oṃ mahāśūnyatājñānavajrasvabhāva ātmako 'haṃ.
Oṃ animitajñānavajrasvabhāva ātmako 'haṃ.
Oṃ apraṇihitajñānavajrasvabhāva ātmako 'haṃ.

The wisdom that is no more than a wink of the eye
is to be examined as mind-only, conceptual thought.
These phenomena that are like the sky
have no bearer of qualities and no nature of reality.
Like the sky, they are unstained,
without substantiality, without conceptual thought.
Being the very nature of compassion, for the sake of benefit,
by the power of aspiration, objects are to be visualized.

As long as what is beneath the earth is to be visualized,
one should meditate on the maṇḍala of wind.
Arising from the syllable Yaṃ,
it is bow-shaped and endowed with blue color,
to be visualized as marked with victory banners,
adorned with a swirling garland of smoke.

Above that, arising from Raṃ,
is the supreme maṇḍala of fire,
triangular in shape and red in color,
blazing and churning with a red, flaming garland,
abiding as though a billion suns,
to be visualized as marked with vajras.

Above that, water too
arises from Vāṃ as a supreme vase,
round and endowed with a garland of rays,
appearing like snow itself.

Above that, what is to be visualized —

arising from Laṃ, it is endowed with yellow color,
square in shape and marked with vajras,
stirring with a host of golden rays.

Above that, what the yogin is to meditate upon —
the supreme king of mountains, the supreme Meru,
its four corners bearing the nature of four precious substances,
adorned in proximity by eight summits —
arises from Suṃ, and is excellent,
supreme among supreme, with its excellent rays.

Above that, what is to be meditated upon —
arising from Hūṃ and to be contemplated —
from that, completely transformed, one is to visualize
the supreme wheel, the supreme palace:
square in form, with four gateways,
beautifully adorned with four archways,
decorated with latticed netting and half-lattice,
ornamented with garlands of silk and flowers.
All the corners and the intervals of the door-frames
are adorned with vajras and precious jewels.

For the outer maṇḍala, one draws the lines,
and draws an eight-petalled lotus —
supreme among supreme in its manifold and excellent colors —
together with its hub and filaments,
all of them a pure and immaculate seat.

In the center of that, a portion of the moon,
luminous by nature and without stain.
Upon that are the vowels and consonants,
adorned in the center by the syllable Vaṃ.

Arising from the center of that stands the chief deity:
Vajravārāhī, the Vajrayoginī,
with four faces and twelve arms,
her three eyes intensely passionate.
She abides upon the seat in the posture of ardhaparyaṅka,
dancing with exceeding beauty.
She wears directional garments and her hair streams loose.
One half, the central, sovereign face
is white and red, of such a form,
adorned beautifully with a crown of skulls.
She holds vajra and bell as mudrā,
surrounded and encircled with lotuses.
At her forehead blazes a mudrā seal.
She wears an upper garment of human skin
and holds a skull-cup and a khaṭvāṅga,
and bears in her hands a lasso and an iron hook.
She also holds a flaying-knife and a severed head.

Her four faces are adorned in proximity
by the auspicious faces of Brahma,
blue, yellow, and green in color,
their faces bearing fangs bared,
adorned above by the crescent moon,
marked by a crown of manifold vajras —
she who grants all accomplishments.

The goddess is ornamented with the six mudrās.
Her girdle consists of eight pieces,
the girdle tinkling with a rustling sound.
She is adorned in proximity by anklets on her feet,
beautified by fine armlets,
with a vajra garland at her forehead —
marked by a mudrā of vajra.
She is exceedingly fine in form, delightful to the mind.

On the eastern petal is a ḍākinī:
the Lion-Faced One, terrifying.
On the northern direction is the Sow-Faced One,
a goddess, a terror-maker.
In the west is a goddess with an elephant's face,
born of a fragment — she is fierce.
In the south is a goddess possessed of form,
with the superior face of a horse,
three-eyed, possessed of manifold form.
All hold skull-cups and khaṭvāṅgas,
grasping a severed head and a curved knife,
naked, with hair streaming free,
exceedingly beautiful in their dancing.
They sit upon the corpse-seat in ardhaparyaṅka,
fierce, wrathful, and terrifying.

Frightener and Kālī (the Time-Marked One)
are pressed down under the goddess's feet.
At the four intermediate directions — east, south, and so on —
vessels containing bodhicitta and other substances
are arrayed upon vases.

In the four directions — north-east and the rest —
white as conch and jasmine and moon,
one draws wheels of six spokes.

In the center of the wheel, what is to be drawn
is the four clan-goddesses:
the goddess Māmakī and Locanā,
Prajñālokā and Śvetavāsanā —
blue, white, and red.
The goddess sovereign of the Vajra clan
is white, blue, and red in color —
she belongs to the clan of the Eternal.
Green, white, and red in color,
the goddess belongs to the Karma clan.
Red, yellow, and green in color,
the goddess arises from the Lotus clan.
All are three-eyed and wear their hair loose,
adorned with directional garments.
All their ornaments are fully perfect;
they are beautifully adorned with the six mudrās.
They hold skull-cups and khaṭvāṅgas,
each bearing her own four kinds of emblems:
vajra, wheel, and sword,
and the supreme of supreme red lotus.
Likewise, all hold bells.

They encircle the lotus.
The four māras are trodden underfoot.
They are dancing, with laughing faces,
seated in the center of a variegated lotus and moon-disc.
They perform the siddhis (accomplishments) of their respective clans.
They hold severed heads and khaṭvāṅgas in their hands,
and are beautified with vajras and curved blades.
They wear skull-garland crowns
and are adorned with garlands of heads.

In all the intermediate directions,
the arrangement proceeds in order, beginning with the site.

On the six spokes of the eastern wheel,
the goddesses are arranged in corresponding sequence:
On the first of the six spokes of the wheel, at Pūllīra, she is the supremely fierce one.
On the second, at Jālandhara, she is the wrathful-eyed one.
On the third, at Oḍiyāna, she is the radiant one.
On the fourth, at Arbuda, she is the great snub-nosed one.
On the fifth, at Godāvarī, she is the one of steadfast mind.
On the sixth, at Rāmeśvara, she is the short one.

On the second, the northern wheel with six spokes:
On the first, at Devīkoṭa, she is the sovereign of Laṅkā.
On the second, at Mālaya, she is the shade of the forest.
On the third, at Kāmarūpa, she is the guardian of the earth.
On the fourth, at Oḍe, she is the greatly terrifying one.
On the fifth, at Triśakuni, she is the one possessed of wind's force.
On the sixth, at Kosala, she is the one who drinks ale.

On the third, the western wheel's six spokes — on the first spoke of the eastern wheel: at Kaliṃka, she is the green goddess. On the second, at Lampāka, she is the supremely beautiful one. On the third, at Kañcikā, she is the horse-eared one. On the fourth, at the Snow Mountain, she is the bird-faced one. On the fifth, at Pretapuri, she is the one possessed of wheel-force. On the sixth, at Gṛhadeva, she is the dismembered one.

On the fourth, to the east of the southern wheel's spokes: at Saurāṣṭra, she is the ale-seller.
On the second, at the Golden Island, she is the wheel-armored one.
On the third, at Nāgara, she is the supremely courageous one.
On the fourth, at Sindhu, she is the greatly powerful one.
On the fifth, at Maru, she is the wheel-turning one.
On the sixth, at Kulūta, she is the one of great diligence.

The first is white-green, holding a skull-cup,
khaṭvāṅga, wheel,
khaṭvāṅga, and curved blade.
The second, in the north, is green-yellow,
holding a vajra, skull-cup, khaṭvāṅga,
khaṭvāṅga, and curved blade.
The third, in the west, is red-yellow,
holding a skull-cup, khaṭvāṅga, lotus,
khaṭvāṅga, and curved blade.
The fourth, the southern goddess, is yellow-red,
holding a variegated vajra, khaṭvāṅga, curved blade,
skull-cup, and khaṭvāṅga.

All the goddesses are half-feminine and half-lord, three-eyed,
four-armed, naked, with hair loose,
standing in a dancing posture with one leg raised in a half-seated stance,
above a preta (hungry ghost) seat,
crowned with a skull-garland,
adorned with the five mudrās.
Their anklets, girdles, and crowns are marked with vajras.

Then, encircling the wheel outward:
a garland of vajras to the east,
a garland of wheels to the north,
a garland of variegated vajras to the west,
a garland of lotuses to the south.
In the center, beautified by a vajra fence.
In all the intermediate directional sections,
draw the variegated vajras correctly.
That wise one is encircled on the outside
by a variegated garland of pretas.
At the outer maṇḍala gates
and in all four intermediate directional sections
are ḍākinīs in fierce form.
These are to be arranged in meditative equipoise.

The crow-faced one is placed in the east —
blue in color, she is mind-captivating.
The owl-faced one is at the northern gate —
red and green, she is beautiful.
The dog-faced one is at the western gate —
fierce and yellow, like smoke.
On the southern side is the pig-faced one —
green and blue in appearance.

All are three-eyed, fierce, and terrifying.
Supreme among those who wear directional garments,
they gaze with intent, standing short of stature,
dwelling with one leg extended.
They are seated in the center of a variegated lotus and sun-disc.
They are adorned with the five mudrās,
and crowned with garlands of skulls.

With hair loose and gazing intently — the Yamadūtī (messenger of Death) with the face of a buffalo dwells in the direction of fire. The Yamadūtī with the face of a donkey dwells in the direction of truth-straying. The Yamadūtī with the fangs, bearing the face of a sow, rests on the wind and possesses fearsome power. The Yamajit (vanquisher of Death) with the horse-face is possessed of lordly power, ever turning.

She is adorned with a crown of skull garlands, manifold in form and mind-captivating. Naked, with hair unbound, she moves in the dance with one knee raised. Her face is set with gnashing fangs; her three eyes roll and flash. She dwells at the center of a variegated lotus and sun-disc, gazing with fierce desire.

All the goddesses of the retinue maṇḍalas are seated above a row of pretas (hungry ghosts). They hold skull-cups and khaṭvāṅgas, and grip severed heads with their hands. In their left hands they beat small hand-drums, utterly terrifying. The forms of those who encircle the outer ring are many and various.

With implements of song and dance, with the supreme and various sacred commitments (samaya), with incense, flowers, lamps and so forth, with perfume, garlands, and ointments, with divine garments and palace halls, with the finest divine music — surrounded on all sides by these offerings, the outer yoginīs make worship. Such are the supreme offerings without limit, filling the entire expanse of space.

This supreme practice of the yoginī, the supreme arising of Vajravārāhī, this supreme great secret — it must not be spoken of even in the slightest. One should meditate upon the outer preta-garland and the sacred maṇḍala.

Having thus arranged them in place, and having beheld that wisdom wheel, one should commence the yoga of that practice. By this essential nature of all things, interlock the fingers and join the middle fingers firmly; apply the thumbs as firm vajras, set it thus in the middle of the forehead, and revolve it, revolve it, revolve it on all sides. Pressing down with the feet, gaze upward; sounding upward the cry Pheṃ, summon the heroes and yoginīs dwelling in the ten directions throughout the world-realms.

Jaḥ Hūṃ Baṃ Hoḥ.

By the application of the vajra hook and the rest, one hooks, introduces, binds, and brings under control.
