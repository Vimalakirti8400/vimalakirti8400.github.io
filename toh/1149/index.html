About this text

Verses in Praise of the Gaṇḍī, known in Sanskrit as the Gaṇḍīstotragāthā and in Tibetan as Gaṇḍī'i bstod pa tshigs su bcad pa, is a devotional hymn honoring the gaṇḍī, the wooden gong struck to summon the monastic community. Opening with homage to the Buddha, the poem recalls his awakening beneath the Bodhi tree, where armored hosts and alluring women advanced on him with clamorous shouts and laughter, yet he remained entirely unshaken. Its verses are filled with rhythmic, onomatopoeic syllables that seem to echo both the din of those assailants and the resounding beat of the gong itself, and they close with appeals for the Lion of the Śākyas to protect those who recite them. The work is Toh 1149 in the Tengyur, where it belongs among the collected hymns of praise.

Homage to the Buddha.

Those who of old, at the foot of the Bodhi tree, came by the path of the sun, crying gā gred gā gred, gā gā gred gā ga gā gred gha na gha gha na gha gred, those with armor bound upon their bodies, and the lovely-formed women, with their ḍhu ḍu pa ti ḍhu ḍu ḍu bhiḥ dhu ḍu bhiḥrnamas, he who was not shaken by them in the least, to whom gods and humans bow, may that Lion of the Śākyas himself protect us.

The women with the bodies of the love god's daughters, laughing ka ha ka ha ka ka hā hā hī ti,
and when the arrogant ones loudly cried out ta ṭi ta ta ṭi ta tī tā ṭa ṭā ti,
and the words of the servants, kud kud bud bud ku kū ci ta ku ha ra ci ta:
he who was not frightened, by hearing of whom all stains are stilled, may that Lord of Sages bring peace.

Those who moved their curving brows and, sporting with the arrows of Smara (Kāma, the god of love), lowered lashes and eyes, the women of eager bodies whose limbs played like swaying branches, speaking bashfully and sidelong, with words charming, sweet and soft, and delightful in every way: he whose mind was not led astray by their words, the victor over Smara, to him I bow.

The earth quaked all around, the sky was filled with hosts of sharp arrows,
blazing things and fires of wrath flared in every direction, so that the waters churned,
and the warriors of Māra roared, bearing javelins, swords, discuses and sharp saws:
you who with the weapon of loving-kindness wholly stilled them and were victorious, Lord of Sages, protect us.

Born of radiance, wrath blazing bright, fangs bared, loudly proclaiming, dreadful, thundering nets of rain clouds flashing, elephants with swelling temples bound for war, thick black darkness, and the hosts of the love god's army, with their destroying fire flaring and the gleam of swords lighting all things without exception: the troops of the Flower-Arrowed One, whom you crushed in an instant, Buddha, please protect us.

Eyes like lotus petals reaching to lovely ears, lashes and pupils fluttering,
with intense passion, artful, wide open and in concert, with brows at play,
the eyed women of Māra's train, utterly enamored, red at the corners of their eyes, never at rest,
could not entice him at all; to that supreme sage, free of faults, I bow.

He whose mind was not disturbed, who dispelled the māras with fangs bared, staring narrowly, wagging their tongues, those with glorious pointed weapons in hand, those with the faces of elephants and horses, with the faces of lions and tigers, and the most beautiful god of desire, he counted as not even grass; he who, wary of saṃsāra, dispelled evil, protector of the world, Lord of Sages, Buddha, protect us.

He whose mind was unshaken by the earth, the courses of rivers, the oceans and the clouds,
by the thunder, by Māra's warriors of many kinds with hundreds of faces and countless fierce forms,
who did not count the Flower-Bannered One even as grass, omniscient and free of desire:
may that glorious Buddha, the hero, remover of the fear of evil, unchanging, protect us.

The māras' great host, swords, axes and bows in hand, and short spears with exceedingly sharp points,
hurling meteors, many fires, roaring loudly, with destroying, terrifying sounds:
he whose mind was undisturbed, unmoving as a mountain, the holy one seated cross-legged,
remover of the fears of the three realms, Buddha, hero, most heroic, to him I bow and pay homage.

Laughing loudly aṭṭaṭa, with bells bound on, swiftly ringing piercing and clear,
swaggering, charging, fangs bared, their hair in twisted locks, the squint-eyed servants
could not overcome him; he who, sharply beating the clear drum, at his awakening
sounded for the proud the drum ringing clear as on Vulture Peak, may that Buddha, the hero, bring well-being.

ko ka ṇḍi rā ma ra ṇa hi pra ti bha ya ku ha raṃ darpa ṇa ṇe ḍaṃ ra ṇṇa ḍaṃ ḍo mba ṇḍa ḍu ḍi mba ṇa ḍa ha ḍhu ha ka ḍhu haṃ straiṅ kha la straiṅ kha la straiṅ ma jim bra ña ja ji ma braṅ kha mu kha mu khu khu māṅ khuḥ khu maṅ khuḥ khu maṅ khuḥ khu maṅ khu, and the rest: unafraid of these, bowed to by gods and humans, Lion of the Śākyas, protect us.

He by whom the night of pride was dispelled, swollen with bodies bearing Māra's clouds,
the sole adversary to the glory of the lotus forests that were the faces of Māra's women,
who attained perfect and complete awakening, like the moon and the autumn night lily:
here, proclaiming as the messenger of the Dharma, is the gaṇḍī of that Blessed One, the King of Dharma.

Mindful long since of this Antaka, the Ender, the examiner, who never finds satisfaction and is insatiable not even for an instant, so that minds are absorbed in it and practice well at all times without interruption, this which here, entering upon the command of the Three Jewels, teaches living beings again and again, this is the gaṇḍī of the Dharma, harsh and undiminished, resounding far through the circle of the directions.

Like the disc of the sun, victorious over the hosts of stars,
it blazes here over the non-Buddhists (tīrthikas) and in the Victor's teaching;
this gaṇḍī, the heart-ornament of the circle of the earth,
resounds like the fierce hand-drum of victory in battle.

You are not afraid of anything at all:
ja mi ja mi ḍhu ḍhu ma ra ñi ja ra ñi ja ḍe na ṇā ḍi ṇā ḍe

Let living worldly beings behold how, by the strength of the one who has the ten powers, the armies of Māra are crushed! From the quaking of the violently quaking earth the mountains are shaken, and Meru, king of mountains, trembles. The hosts of gods, the light of the grahas, and all the nāga kings without exception are terrified, and the non-Buddhists, hearing the fierce gaṇḍī that terrifies in so many ways, are terrified; for the Buddhists it becomes a cause of peace; it sounds forth in the four directions, and here it calls the Saṅgha. This is the gaṇḍī that resounds from the rooftop of the monastery; its sounds, like the sound of thunderclouds, are delightful today; like a mother, most loving and tender, calling well her children who have gone outside, it is the gaṇḍī that calls them home at mealtime.

It is supreme in utterly destroying the wheel of saṃsāra;
proclaiming a sound not encompassed even by all the precious qualities of the Buddha,
equal to the great drum of the gods that sounds forth, the gaṇḍī with its great sound
utterly destroys every evil without exception.

Through this, the Tathāgata, worshipped by gods, asuras and great mahoragas,
who has gone to supreme peace:
his gaṇḍī sounds forth with a sound equal to the great drum of the deathless,
and shatters the hearts of the other non-Buddhists.

Here, by the sounding of this gaṇḍī, humans,
gods, humans and asuras are summoned:
"Good ones, listen to this gaṇḍī of the Sugata,
surrounded by the whole assembly of monks without exception!"

Like the clouds that fill the sky in the season of turmoil, when elephants bring terror,
it is the cause that averts the destruction, the dread and the trembling with fear of beings;
bringing peace, it utterly annihilates the pride of all the non-Buddhists without exception:
you, that gaṇḍī which all the hosts of sages established for the increase of the Dharma, protect!
May that supreme merit be accomplished, granting heaven and liberation
to worldly beings whose lives are unsteady; may it not become evil that grants the lower realms!
Here what I have spoken, like a bee humming at the center of a flower,
I scatter as a handful of flowers on the lotuses of the feet of Māra's foe: you, protect!

With the flowers scattered with deep reverence by the hosts of gods such as Śakra (Indra),
and the flowers scattered reverently, filling all, by those sounding cymbals in the sky,
he who is famed to have descended from heaven to earth, escorted at length:
the Blessed One, great treasury of compassion, may that utterly fierce gaṇḍī of his protect beings!

He who, as soon as he emerged from his mother's womb, walked seven steps on his own,
who proclaimed his own words putting an end to saṃsāra, he of no small wisdom,
who declared that becoming in any further birth was removed:
through that gaṇḍī of the Sugata, which cuts darkness to pieces, may there be victory!

The thousand supreme Merus, all the slopes of the jewel mountains glowing with gems, the summit of the snow mountain, lord and king of mountains, the lands of humans and the worlds of the nāgas: dwelling there, the deathless ones, the lords of the desire realm, the human rulers of the four continents, the sun and moon, and the lords of the asuras, all of them, hearing the gaṇḍī of the Sugata's Dharma, are called to the Sugata's excellent intent.

Mounted on many proud elephants of many hideous kinds found throughout the three worlds,
all with long fangs bared, holding the emblem of a garland of blazing vajras,
all the hosts of evil that came were terrified by the hūṃ sound of the wise guru who utters the vajra hūṃ;
proclaiming, "The Victor has arisen over all the worlds," they bow at his feet, their crowns adorned with the Buddha.

The daughters of Māra, sweet-voiced, with eyes like the sāla blossom, their breasts firm and full,
with a gait like a swan's, riding upon servant women, singing, beating hosts of drums, moving swiftly,
coming in multitudes without cease like the four rivers swelling the great ocean:
to them it is the gaṇḍī itself that unceasingly proclaims the excellent sounds ṭuṃ ṭuṃ bhraṃ bhraṃ.

Having conquered the power of Māra and displayed great terror, he whose faults are exhausted,
who here has attained the state of omniscience, likewise most beautiful,
the Buddha endowed with every excellent quality, pure and compassionate:
may his fierce gaṇḍī, stealing away evil, break to pieces the evils of humans!

Brahmā became as if mute; the pride of the guru of the gods (Bṛhaspati) vanished everywhere;
the Life-Giver became small-minded; fortunate Viṣṇu went off without a word;
here the gods, by the fame of whose qualities they were filled, shrank their limbs in shame:
the gaṇḍī of the Sage, making beings fearless, protects beings from the lower realms.

Brahmā, the thousand-eyed lord, Viṣṇu, the sun and moon, rākṣasas and yakṣas,
gandharvas, mahoragas, kiṃnaras, asuras, humans, the māra classes and pretas,
and all those wearing yellow robes, by whose purity the grounds of the earth blaze with golden light:
all of them without exception the Tathāgata, the supreme guru, summons to listen to the Dharma.

By it the non-Buddhists (tīrthikas), petty in birth and base in their intent, are struck with fear;
by it the Buddhists, whose minds grow ever more excellent, gain a joy that greatly increases;
its qualities, once gained, are vast, and through it all faults come to their end:
may that gaṇḍī, the wooden gong, ever exhaust the sins of beings in existence in this age of strife.
Bowing to it with a pure intention, according to the rite, one travels to the higher destinies,
and it swiftly brings all adverse factors to their exhaustion:
may that gaṇḍī of the Sage's Dharma destroy the turmoil and the clouded sight of delusion.
The fear that arises from existence for those who possess life, you vanquish.
Through the virtue of hearing this, the mountains, Brahmā and the rest, swiftly fall to the depths of the ground;
the mountains tremble, and the earth swiftly sinks beneath the ground.
Terrifier of the non-Buddhists, devoted to the good of others, pure in its very nature,
through which the Buddhists swiftly come to peace: please let that gaṇḍī call the assembly together.

The Verses in Praise of the Gaṇḍī, composed by the master Aśvaghoṣa, is complete. It was translated by the great scholar Jinasena and the translator, the monk Dharma Yönten.

Translator's notes

The gaṇḍī is the wooden beam or gong struck to assemble the monastic community; the poem imitates its sounds and those of Māra's assault with strings of onomatopoeic syllables, which are reproduced as written. The Tibetan is a close rendering of an ornate Sanskrit poem, and several long verses (notably the one on Antaka, the Ender, and the opening of the verse on wrath "born of radiance") are syntactically obscure; the translation follows the most natural reading of the Tibetan. "Protect us" renders the bare imperative "protect"; the object is unstated. "Not encompassed even by all the precious qualities of the Buddha" renders the obscure མ་ཀླུབས་པ ("not covered"); the sense of the line is uncertain. "Sweet-voiced, with eyes like the sāla blossom" renders the unclear ཁྲི་སྙན་ས་ལ; uncertain. "With a gait like a swan's" reads ངང་པའི for the written དད་པའི ("of faith"), an evident scribal slip. "The Life-Giver" renders འཚོ་བྱེད (perhaps Jīva, a name of Bṛhaspati, or another deity); its identity is uncertain. "The thousand-eyed lord" is Indra. "The thousand supreme Merus" takes སྟོང literally as "thousand"; it may be a slip for སྟེང "summit." The phrase rendered "the mountains, Brahmā and the rest" is obscure. The Tibetan "earth-holders" normally means mountains, and the line could also be read as "Brahmā and the others who uphold the earth." In "for those who possess life," the instrumental particle has been read as a genitive. The gaṇḍī is the wooden beam or gong struck to summon the monastic community.
