About this text

The Six-Branched Yoga, known in Sanskrit as Ṣaḍaṅgayoga and in Tibetan as Sbyor ba yan lag drug pa, is a short tantric commentary attributed in its colophon to the bodhisattva Vajrapāṇi, the Lord of Secrets. It explains the six branches of yoga taught in the Later Tantra of Guhyasamāja, namely withdrawal, meditative concentration, breath control, retention, recollection, and absorption, showing how the root tantra's terse mention of three of them implies all six, and relating them to the four phases of approach and accomplishment, to the signs such as smoke that arise in meditation, to the work with the channels and winds, and to the inner fire and the innate bliss of the completion stage. The text draws throughout on other scriptures, including the Ḍākinīvajrajāla, the Māyājāla, and the Perfection of Wisdom, and stresses that its meaning is to be learned from a guru's oral instruction. It is Toh 1364 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the section of commentaries on the tantras, and it was translated into Tibetan by Chokro Tingdzin Zangpo with the Indian preceptor Subhūtiśānti.

Translation

In the language of India: ṣaḍaṃ gayoga nāma. In the language of Tibet: The Six-Branched Yoga.

Homage to the Lord of Secrets, Vajrapāṇi.

Now, the method here is the yoga of six branches. Here it is taught in reverse of the intermediate sequence, and each inner branch that is left out, an earlier branch that is not taught, is to be understood by the very implication of the later branch that is taught. For example, when one says, "This one goes about by day," then even though the other, called "one who goes about by night," is not stated, it is apprehended by implication itself. In the same way, where phenomena depend on one another, even the one that is not taught is understood through the one that is taught. Therefore in this tantra three branches are taught, within each of which another branch is included.

That the branch explained in the middle is to be done first is here the guru's oral instruction. Otherwise, if one were to take it in the sequence in which it is spoken in the tantra, what need would there be for the guru's oral instruction? Therefore everything, what is spoken with hidden intent and so forth, is to be understood from the guru's oral instruction, or else to be known from the commentaries composed by bodhisattvas, but not from what has been composed by those who are proud of their learning.

Thus, by the passage beginning "meditative concentration," the other three branches are to be known. The six branches spoken of in other tantras are to be understood here as three branches, by way of a condensed meaning. Here, in the Later Tantra of Guhyasamāja, it is said:

Withdrawal and meditative concentration,
Breath control and retention,
Recollection and absorption:
These are held to be the six branches of yoga.

Accordingly, what goes before meditative concentration is withdrawal. What goes before mantra recitation is breath control. Here the word "mantra recitation" refers to neuter recitation, or vajra recitation, that is, the retention of the vital wind. What goes before bliss is recollection, and there the word "bliss" teaches absorption. Thus, for the one who is a yogin of the six branches, the six come to be accomplished as buddhahood itself.

The accomplishment of ordinary, outer, mundane yoga comes about for beginners, childish yogins, through mantra recitation and meditative concentration, that is, the meditating mind that imagines the wheel of the maṇḍala, and through bliss, which is the mundane bliss arising from the action seal (karmamudrā) and the wisdom seal (jñānamudrā). Through the bliss whose nature is descent, a mundane bliss, there is a mundane fruit reaching up to the abode of Akaniṣṭha. In this way, by the two stages, the state of a mundane yogin and of a supramundane yogin is accomplished. Here I shall teach just how the state of a supramundane yogin is accomplished through the supreme yoga of six branches.

This withdrawal is as follows. The consciousnesses of the eye and so on do not engage the outer objects, form and the rest, while the consciousnesses of the faculties such as the divine eye engage the inner objects. The inner one is seeing, without imagining it, the object that is the emptiness of all phenomena; it is seeing all things by way of emptiness. Just as a maiden sees a reflected image, so seeing the reflected image of the buddha of the three realms is the first branch, called withdrawal.

Then, meditative concentration is the mind's engagement with the emptiness of all existing things, gathered into wisdom. What is called "examination" is the mind's grasping the meaning of a thing. What is called "analysis" is grasping the particular features of a thing. What is called "joy" is the mind's abiding in all things. Bliss is the unmoving perfection of all things. Thus the five limbs of meditative concentration are taught.

Then, what is called "breath control" is this: lalanā and rasanā, that is, the right and the left channels, are stopped and dissolve into the avadhūtī, so that the vital wind constantly enters the central path. Through the practice of inhaling, abiding, and exhaling, one enters the avadhūtī with oṃ, brings it to cessation with hūṃ, and emerges with aḥ, and one becomes familiar with this as having the nature of the moon, Rāhu, and the sun. This is taught as the nature of breath control.

Then, what is called "retention" is this: the vital wind enters the navel, the heart, the throat, and the forehead, the maṇḍalas of Mahendra, of water, of fire, and of wind, so that the life force abides in the drop and does not come out again. This is taught as the branch of retention.

Then, what is called "recollection" is the abandoning of conceptual thought through seeing the reflected image of one's chosen deity. From that, radiating into the three realms an embodied form from which many rays of light stream out, brilliantly shining, in the manner of various maṇḍalas: that is the branch of recollection.

What is called "absorption" is the fearless bliss of attachment to one's chosen deity, the one-pointedness of mind without grasped and grasper. So the Tathāgata spoke of the branch of absorption.

Here the six branches are the yoga stated in brief. In detail, the yogin who desires the accomplishment of the Great Seal (mahāmudrā) should come to understand them from the Hundred-Thousand Collection, or from the Glorious Paramādya, or from the guru's oral instruction.

Here, the instruction on meditating on the yoga of six branches as the initial path, as spoken in other tantras, is, from the Later Tantra of Guhyasamāja, approach, near accomplishment, accomplishment, and great accomplishment. As it is said:

At the time of approach, the form
Of the Great Uṣṇīṣa should be meditated on with effort.
Likewise, at the time of near accomplishment,
The form of Amṛtakuṇḍalin should be meditated on.
For accomplishment, the form of the deity
Should be meditated on by the yogin who strives.
The very form of the Lord of Buddhas
Is to be visualized in great accomplishment.

Here, in other words of intentional speech, the form of the Great Uṣṇīṣa is the body of the buddha of the three realms without exception. With compassion firmly established in space, the source of phenomena, at the first time, the time of approach, one meditates on it through withdrawal, and also makes it stable through the branch of meditative concentration.

On this the Blessed One made a promise:

Having entirely abandoned all conceptual thought,
Examine it for a single day and night.
If the signs do not arise,
Then my words are false.

There, the signs are the marks such as smoke. They are not otherwise, through mantras and the like.

This is the practice of a single day through which the signs arise for the mantrin. Therefore the mantrin abandons the notions of existence and nonexistence and makes the mind free of dependence, and from profound, nonreferential emptiness the signs such as smoke arise. That one is to meditate on them is the Tathāgata's definitive word.

Through the practice of operation and binding,
The three levels are to be accomplished.

According to these lines, the body of the buddha of the three levels is to be accomplished through the operations of the eye and the other faculties. The oral instruction on this is to be understood from the guru's own mouth.

There, through the guru's oral instruction, the yogin first sees something like smoke in the sky, and should know from his own experience, "This is not a mirage." After that it is like a mirage: that very smoke and the rest appear without conceptual thought, like a reflected image. The first is like smoke, the second like a mirage, the third like the sky, the fourth like a lamp, and the fifth like a cloudless sky. These signs are spoken of in the Guhyasamāja and in the Ḍākinījāla.

Furthermore, in the Ḍākinīvajrajāla the Blessed One said:

The aspect that is like an illusion,
And after it, the instant that is like a dream:
That is the cause of omniscience,
And it bestows accomplishment.

Therefore, according to the Blessed One's word, the marks beginning with the first, which is like smoke, become the signs of meditation. Some say that they come at the time of accomplishment. Yet these signs are what keep the Tathāgata's promise from failing, and that claim would refute the Blessed One's word:

Having completely abandoned all conceptual thought,
One should examine it for a single day and night.

Whatever mundane smoke and the like, or illusions and the like, there may be at the time of accomplishment, these do not become signs, because they are actually capable of the functions of smoke, of blazing, and of burning; and likewise, for example, with rains of saffron, flowers, jewels, gold, and so forth. Therefore it is the Blessed One's definitive word that smoke and the rest become the signs of the yoga of six branches.

So too it is said in the Ḍākinīvajrajāla Tantra:

Having meditated on the six branches,
One should then bless oneself again.
After that, in their proper order,
The whole set of signs is to be realized.

There, blessing oneself is seeing conventional emptiness. The sign of withdrawal is the appearance as clouds, smoke, and so forth; this ends with the lamp, and then continues up to the stainless, cloudless sky. In other tantras it runs from the first, the blazing, up to the last, the drop.

And in the Samādhi chapter of the Māyājāla, the Blessed One said this:

Arisen from space, self-arisen,
Great fire of wisdom and gnosis,
Great light that makes all things illuminated,
Blazing light of gnosis,
Lamp of beings, torch of gnosis,
Great splendor, clear light,
Lord of fierce mantras, king of vidyā-mantras,
King of secret mantras, accomplisher of the great aim.

With these two verses, in the Māyājāla, the Blessed One spoke of further signs in words of hidden intent.

What arises from the cloudless sky is "arisen from space, self-arisen," because all conceptual thought has been abandoned. "Great fire of wisdom and gnosis" is the appearance like blazing. "Great light that makes all things illuminated" is the appearance like the moon, and that alone is "blazing light of gnosis." "Lamp of beings" is the appearance of the sun. "Torch of gnosis" is the appearance like Rāhu. "Great splendor, clear light" is the appearance like lightning. "Lord of fierce mantras, king of vidyā-mantras" is the appearance like a drop, in the aspect of a moon disk blue in color. "King of secret mantras, accomplisher of the great aim" is seeing, through the practice of withdrawal, the appearance of the things of the three realms in all their aspects as like an illusion and a dream.

The operation of the eye faculty, and then the binding of breath control: through the practice of these operations and of binding, the three levels are to be accomplished. The meaning is that they are characterized as desire, form, and formlessness, and have the nature of the stable and the moving.

Likewise, it is said in the Vajraḍākinījāla too:

The moving and the unmoving, the three realms,
As far as the world systems extend,
And as far as there are those with vajra bodies,
All are accomplished, without anything left over.

That this comes about through the six branches is the Blessed One's definitive word.

Likewise, it is said in the Guhyasamāja too:

There is no thing and there is no meditation;
What is to be meditated on is not meditation.
Thus, since a thing is no thing,
Meditation is not to be apprehended.

Here, "no thing" is the cloudless sky, and "meditation" is withdrawal. That is what is meant by "meditation on no thing is not meditation." Here, since the object of the meditation of withdrawal is the cloudless sky, there is no meditation either, because it is free of conceptual meditation. Thus the thing that is seen through withdrawal is not a thing either: without imagining, one sees past, future, and present things as being without thingness. Then the Blessed One said that, because one meditates on withdrawal, conceptual meditation is not apprehended.

The Blessed One also taught this meditation in the Perfection of Wisdom, as follows: "Then Śakra (Indra), lord of the gods, said this to the venerable Subhūti: 'Noble Subhūti, how is the practice of the Perfection of Wisdom to be found?' Subhūti said, 'Kauśika, one who wishes to practice space should train in the Perfection of Wisdom.'"

Likewise, the Blessed One taught the meditation of the Great Seal as similar to a reflected image and an illusion, and as like the cloudless sky. Thus the branch of approach has been taught through withdrawal and meditative concentration.

Next, the form of Amṛtakuṇḍalin is explained, with hidden intent, as the wind.

This too is of two kinds. In the Later Tantra of Guhyasamāja the Blessed One said:

The breath, whose nature is the five jewels,
Blessed by the five buddhas,
Having drawn it out in the form of a ball,
Meditate on it at the tip of the nose.

Here, the words "five jewels" mean the five elements, earth and the rest, in the rasanā, which bear the qualities of the five maṇḍalas. The breath that has these as its nature, the breath whose nature is the five jewels, is in the right nostril. Likewise, the five buddhas are the five aggregates, consciousness and the rest, in the lalanā, which bear the qualities of the five maṇḍalas; what is blessed by them is in the left nostril.

"Drawn out in the form of a ball" means this: here the life wind of the right and left maṇḍalas, in the form of a ball, becomes one in the middle channel, the avadhūtī. That life wind, drawn out in the form of a ball, is meditated on at the tip of the nose. There, the words "tip of the nose" teach the centers of the lotuses of the navel, the heart, the throat, the forehead, and the crown; one should meditate at their tip. To meditate at the tip of the nose is to meditate at the center of the lotus in the middle, between the right and the left. Since in this way one holds the life wind, in the manner of a ball, at the place of the drop, this itself is taught as retention.

Likewise, the near accomplishment of the two branches is the form of Amṛtakuṇḍalin. That same near accomplishment is also taught as vajra recitation; the recitation in which the branches are unimpaired is the wind's not moving in the right and left channels.

"Having seen the form of the Uṣṇīṣa, afterward the mantrin applies himself to breath control; this is to be understood from the guru's oral instruction and from the words of hidden intent." This means that breath control and retention are near accomplishment.

Then, on the line, "For accomplishment, the form of the deity," the meaning here is this. Through the power of retention, the yogin sees caṇḍālī, who abides at the navel, blazing. Because it is free of all obscurations, the Great Seal, like a reflected image, blazes, and is beautified by a maṇḍala of light from which radiate clouds of light rays, infinite clouds of buddhas. So, through recollection, accomplishment is taught. This meditation on the practice of caṇḍālī at the end of retention is definitive.

Then, by the light of her gnosis, the aggregates, the elements, and the sense bases are burned and made of one taste. Consciousness and the rest, which abide in the right and left channels, and the nature of the maṇḍalas of earth and the rest, enter the moon maṇḍala at the forehead. Then, by the light of the fire of caṇḍālī's gnosis, the moon melts, and the awakening mind descends below in the form of drops: at the throat, the heart, the navel, and the lotus of the secret place, with the nature of joy, supreme joy, and the joy of variety; and then, at the tip of the vajra jewel, with the essential nature of innate joy. Or else it is with the nature of variety, maturation, destruction, and freedom from characteristics.

Thus, when the sixteen digits are completely full, and by the power of meditation the bliss that has entered the jewel arises, then whatever is like a bliss that words can speak of is a mere illustration. As for its essential nature, the mundane bliss that arises from the two organs does not reach even one part in a thousand koṭis of that supreme, imperishable bliss.

Here, the state of imperishable bliss whose nature is innate joy, which is not an object of words and which childish yogins do not recognize, is taught as the bodhisattvas' samādhi of emptiness. The intent is that it does not make one fall into the sense of sheer nonexistence that is known in the world.

Thus, the yoga of six branches: through mantra recitation, meditative concentration, and bliss, the yogin himself is buddhahood. "The purifier, the supreme destroyer of evil, is to be accomplished by the yogin in this very life": in these words the Blessed One taught what is to be accomplished and the certainty of its accomplishment.

The Six-Branched Yoga, composed by the Lord of Secrets, the lord of the ten grounds, the bodhisattva Vajrapāṇi, is complete.

It was translated, edited, and finalized by the translator, the monk Chokro Tingdzin Zangpo, from the mouth of the great Indian preceptor Śrī Subhūtiśānti.

Translator's notes

"Examination" and "analysis" render rtog pa and dpyod pa, the vitarka and vicāra among the limbs of dhyāna. "Unmoving perfection" reads phud sum tshogs pa as phun sum tshogs pa, an evident scribal slip. The Hundred-Thousand Collection (Tibetan 'bum sde) is taken as a reference to an extensive root tantra; the exact work is not certain. "Neuter recitation" renders ma ning gi bzlas pa, the vajra recitation in which the breath is the neuter element. The sentence on those who place the signs "at the time of accomplishment" is elliptical; it is read as the author rejecting that view because it would contradict the promise that signs arise within a day and night. In the verse from the Ḍākinīvajrajāla, chogs is read as tshogs ("the whole set"), and in "up to the stainless, cloudless sky" par du is read as bar du; both are evident scribal slips, as is sga gcan for sgra gcan (Rāhu). The eight-line passage cited from the Samādhi chapter of the Māyājāla corresponds to verses of the Mañjuśrīnāmasaṃgīti, given here as the Tibetan reads it. The Tibetan here writes la nā for the left channel; it is read as lalanā, the form used earlier in the text, assuming a dropped syllable. The four moments listed at the end of the descent of the drops (variety, maturation, destruction, freedom from characteristics) are the standard vicitra, vipāka, vimarda, and vilakṣaṇa.
