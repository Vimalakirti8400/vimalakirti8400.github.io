About this text

The Noble Crown of the Buddhas, known in Sanskrit as the Ārya Buddhamakuṭa Nāma Mahāyāna Sūtra and in Tibetan as Pakpa Sangyé Tsukgyen Zhéjawa Tekpa Chenpo'i Do, is a Mahāyāna sūtra belonging to the great class of Dharma teachings. The text opens in the classic sūtra style, with the Buddha residing on Vulture Peak Mountain at Rājagṛha in the company of a vast assembly of monks, bodhisattvas such as Maitreya and Mañjuśrī, and multitudes of gods, nāgas, yakṣas, and other beings. It is catalogued as Toh 274 in the Kangyur, the collection of canonical texts attributed to the Buddha himself. The translation was carried out by the Indian preceptors Jinamitra and Dānaśīla together with the great Tibetan editor-translator, the monk Ye shes sde, who also revised and finalized the text.

Translator's notes

The colophon names three figures: the Indian paṇḍitas Jinamitra and Dānaśīla, and the Tibetan chief editor-translator Ye shes sde, who was among the most prominent lotsāwas of the imperial period. The phrase "great editor-translator" (zhu chen gyi lo tsā ba) designates a senior reviser of canonical translations.

The term rendered "smell-eaters" (gandharvas) refers to a class of semi-divine beings who subsist on fragrances; the Tibetan "dri za" is a calque of the Sanskrit. The buddha-name rendered "Possessed of the Noble Body Complete with All Excellent Marks of Glory" translates a compound that could alternatively be parsed as "Having a Body Adorned with All Noble Excellent Marks of Glory." The rendering chosen reflects the most natural reading of the Tibetan syntax. The passage ends mid-sentence, as the source text itself breaks off at that point. The phrase rendered "reluctant regarding their marks and excellent features" translates a somewhat compressed Tibetan construction. The sense is that such practitioners will not be diffident or hesitant when it comes to explaining the major and minor marks of a great being — their eloquence will be unfailing on this topic. The same word for reluctance or hesitation governs both the clause about marks and the later clause about unheard teachings, and this has been rendered accordingly. The seven jewels of the limbs of awakening are the seven factors of awakening (bodhyaṅgas): mindfulness, discernment of phenomena, diligence, joy, pliancy, meditative absorption, and equanimity. The text's phrasing "jewels of the limbs of awakening" is an unusual formulation likely treating each factor metaphorically as a precious jewel; the translation follows the Tibetan literally. The aspiration of King of Limitless Supreme Fragrance specifies thirty-five thousand aeons in the framing passage above but only thirty thousand aeons within the body of the vow itself. This discrepancy is present in the Tibetan and likely reflects a textual variation within the source manuscript rather than a translation error; both figures have been rendered exactly as they appear. The verse fragment at the very end is incomplete in the passage supplied; the translation renders exactly what is present in the Tibetan without adding material from beyond the supplied text.

The epithet rendered "the Invincible One" translates mi pham pa, which is both a common bodhisattva epithet meaning "unconquerable" or "invincible" and the name Ajita (an epithet of Maitreya). Here it functions as a general epithet for a class of bodhisattvas ("those resembling the Invincible One"), so it has been rendered descriptively rather than as a proper name. The closing phrase "they will not take birth merely as a human" in the verse about liberating tens of millions is ambiguous: it may mean the practitioner transcends ordinary human rebirth, attaining something higher, or that they will not be confined to human rebirth alone. The Tibetan is open to either reading; the higher-birth interpretation is the more standard doctrinal one in this genre of buddha-name sūtras.

The verse "not by one birth, not by ten, not by twenty" indicates that the merit of the one who holds these names and makes offerings to the Victors is inexhaustible — the negatives are building toward the positive point that such merit vastly surpasses any finite reckoning.
