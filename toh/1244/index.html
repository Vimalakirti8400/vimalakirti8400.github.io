About this text

The Means of Accomplishing Hevajra called The Treasury of Purity, in Sanskrit Viśuddhanidhi nāma Hevajrasādhana and in Tibetan Kyé Dorjé Drubpé Tap Nampar Dakpé Ter, is Toh 1244 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the section of tantra commentaries. It is a sādhana, a ritual manual for meditative practice on Hevajra, the blue, eight-faced, sixteen-armed deity embraced by his consort Nairātmyā and surrounded by Gaurī and the other goddesses of his maṇḍala. Beginning with the yogin's daily washing and protective mantra, it guides the practitioner through the visualization of the deity and his retinue, the blessing and offering of torma to the gods, planets and nāgas of the world, and the inner fire offering in which ordinary eating becomes an offering to the deity. Its colophon attributes it to the great scholar Advayavajra Avadhūtīpa and records that it was translated into Tibetan by the eastern Indian paṇḍita Vanaratna together with Shönu Pal at the palace of Nedong.

Translation

In the language of India: biśuddhanidhi nāma hebajrasādhanaṃ. In the language of Tibet: The Means of Accomplishing Hevajra called The Treasury of Purity.

I pay homage at the feet of the glorious excellent guru.

Born from hūṃ, blue, rich with twenty-four eyes, eight-faced,
sixteen-armed, his feet pressing down the four māras, wearing a garland of fifty heads,
bearing the five mudrās, his neck embraced by Nairātmyā, encircled by Gaurī and the rest:
having paid homage to him, I shall set forth the means of accomplishing him.

First of all, the yogin washes his face and so on, and with oṃ rakṣa rakṣa hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā he protects himself, the place, and the yoga.

With a mind intent on the welfare of all sentient beings, he has entered the maṇḍala taught in the glorious Hevajra, has received the empowerments in their sequence, and has been properly authorized; he has obtained the sign of having found the wisdom of suchness in which emptiness and compassion are inseparable, and possesses wisdom that is unobstructed and free of doubt. In a place agreeable to the mind, such as a charnel ground, he sits on a comfortable seat marked with a crossed vajra, and partakes of the samayas such as the five nectars. Then, with the pride of the glorious Vajra, he should visualize at his heart, on a lotus, a sun transformed from the letter ra, and on it a blue syllable hūṃ.

From that hūṃ, rays of light of five kinds radiate upward and illuminate the three realms, and in the sky before him he should imagine the Blessed One: sixteen-armed, eight-faced, with three eyes on each face, his tawny hair bristling upward, adorned with the five mudrās, embraced by Nairātmyā, and surrounded by eight goddesses, with the body colors and hand implements that will be explained, his body emanating and gathering back.

In front of him is Gaurī, black, holding in her hand the moon, the awakening mind. To the right is Caurī, the color of madder, holding in her hand the sun, which is called the self-arisen flower. Behind is Vetālī, yellow, holding in her hand a vajra water vessel. To the left is Ghasmarī, blue-green, holding the secret medicine. In the front left corner is Pukkasī, blue, holding offerings of many kinds. In the front right corner is Śabarī, white, holding in her hand a stream of essence. In the back right corner is Caṇḍālī, entirely red, sounding a ḍamaru. In the back left corner is Ḍombī, smoke-colored, embracing her own body.

Having summoned the Blessed One surrounded by these yoginīs and made him steady, he should then draw those rays of light back into the seed at his heart.

Again, from that seed come forth the special outer, secret, and suchness offerings, and with them he should make offerings to the Blessed One. Presenting flowers, incense, lamps, food, the seven precious things and so on is the outer offering. The eight goddesses presenting the five nectars and the five lamps is called the secret offering. Following the guru's instruction, experiencing in union the joy of one taste with Nairātmyā is the suchness offering.

Having made offerings perfectly and bowed directly with body, speech, and mind, he should confess his wrongdoing in that presence, and so on:

"Whatever evil deeds I have done, caused to be done, or rejoiced in, through the ignoble conceptions of beginningless saṃsāra, all of them I confess, and I will not do them again. I rejoice in the roots of virtue of ordinary beings, of the gods, of the śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas, of the perfectly complete buddhas, and of all bodhisattvas. I dedicate all roots of virtue to unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening.

"Then, until I reach the heart of awakening, I go for refuge to the Buddha, Heruka Vajra, the very nature of all the three realms, in the form of great bliss; to the Dharma he taught, whose nature is mantra, mudrā, maṇḍala and the rest; and to the Saṅgha of yoginīs who dwell in his circle.

"I generate the mind set on awakening: the perfectly complete awakening that is the cause of accomplishing the aims of all sentient beings, characterized by emptiness endowed with the supreme of all aspects, whose nature is great bliss. I shall rely on the Vajra Vehicle, which is the path to unsurpassed awakening for these beings."

After that, in order to complete the perfection of generosity, he should offer his body. He requests the guru, the buddhas, and the bodhisattvas, the protectors, to turn the wheel of Dharma. Having requested them to remain for as long as saṃsāra lasts, he should then cultivate great loving-kindness, which embraces all sentient beings, as if each were an only child, with the bliss of the deity; great compassion, whose nature is the wish that all sentient beings, sunk in the ocean of all conceptual thought and without refuge, be free from suffering and the causes of suffering; joy, characterized by complete contentment from never being separated from great bliss; and equanimity, which embraces them with the path that counters the eight worldly concerns. These are unsurpassed.

Through wisdom, characterized as thoroughly discerning phenomena, he becomes convinced that all phenomena, being mere dependent origination, are unborn by their intrinsic nature. Because this mantra sees and illuminates that meaning, and because it is the essence that gathers up the suchness of all entities without exception, he recites mentally this mantra: oṃ śūnyatā jñāna bajra svabhāvātmako'haṃ. Then, following the instruction of the excellent guru, he rests for a moment in the unborn and unceasing state in the manner of not abiding. This is to be declared the suchness of phenomena, luminous and joyous; it is also the Perfection of Wisdom, which completely pacifies all māras.

After that, through the force of the power of aspiration, he then arises, as it were, from the samādhi of luminosity. Upon a sun maṇḍala arisen from the letter ra, a blue hūṃ transforms, and from it he should visualize a crossed vajra endowed with five rays of light. The rays of that crossed vajra, hard to bear like the fire at the time of the world's destruction, spread out in every direction and become a dense vajra fence, square, blazing, and as wide as he wishes. Above it is a vajra tent, and below, from that, he should see a vajra nature, brilliantly varied, reaching down to beneath the earth. Then rays of light come from the sun and the crossed vajra and spread out in all directions, and he should make all of that firm.

In its center, a yellow syllable bhrūṃ transforms, and from it comes a ten-spoked wheel that revolves and yet is like something unmoving. In the center of the ten-spoked wheel, on a crossed vajra, on a sun resting on a lotus, he sees the syllable hūṃ abiding between a moon and a sun joined face to face. Then, through the sequence of the five wisdoms, the mirror-like wisdom and the rest, he should visualize the Blessed One Hūṃkāra arisen from the hūṃ: like a sapphire, adorned with varied garments and ornaments, wearing a jeweled crown, his hands holding a vajra and a vajra bell as he embraces a consort like himself, seated with his left leg extended, pressing down Bhairava and Kālarātri.

Then, following the instruction of the excellent guru, through the union of vajra and skull cup, he should imagine the ten wrathful ones. From that, in the east, south, west, and north are Yamāntaka, Prajñāntaka, Padmāntaka, and Vighnāntaka, in that order black, white, red, and blue, holding a vajra hammer, a white staff, a red lotus, and an open-pronged vajra. They are short, with large bellies, adorned with eight venomous snakes, their tawny hair blazing upward, their three eyes red and round, with crowns of Vairocana, Akṣobhya, Amitābha, and Amoghasiddhi, and wearing skirts of tiger skin.

In the corners of Agni, Nairṛti, Vāyu, and Īśāna are Ṭakkirāja, Nīladaṇḍa, Mahābala, and Acala, deep black, holding a hook, a staff, a trident, and a sword. Ṭakkirāja and Acala are in a playful posture, adorned with divine ornaments such as fine garments and fine jewels, and wear jeweled crowns. Nīladaṇḍa and Mahābala take a form like Yamāntaka.

On the lower spoke is Sumbharāja, black, holding a pestle and wearing a jeweled crown. On the upper spoke is Uṣṇīṣa, yellow, holding a yellow wheel, with a jeweled crown marked by Vairocana. The five beginning with Ṭakkirāja have crowns of Akṣobhya. These ten wrathful ones stand on variegated lotuses and suns in the posture with the left leg extended. He should see them with their left hands holding toward the heart a fist with a snake noose and a threatening forefinger. In this way he should meditate on the protection wheel.

Then, in its center, Vajra Hūṃkāra transforms, and from him comes what is called the source of phenomena: the great element of space in the form of the letter ai, triangular, the nature of the great Vajradhara, white like the autumn moon, narrow at the base and wide above. He should imagine it hollow inside. Within it is a lotus of various colors, and on its anthers a very broad crossed vajra with twelve points. He should imagine its three eastern points white, the southern yellow, the western red, the northern green, and the center blue.

There, from yaṃ, arises a blue wind maṇḍala in the shape of a bow, marked with banners fluttering at its two tips. Above it, from raṃ, arises a red fire maṇḍala, triangular, marked at its three corners with the syllable raṃ. Above it, from vaṃ, arises a white water maṇḍala, round, marked with a vase. Above it, from laṃ, arises a yellow earth maṇḍala, square, marked at its corners with three-pronged vajras.

Then, in the center of the platform of the crossed vajra, he should imagine the great city of liberation, born from the transformation of the four great elements, which are dependent origination and mind alone, its nature gathering up the pure buddha fields. It is the nature of Vairocana, made of various jewels, square, with four doors, adorned with eight pillars, encircled by four ledges, and adorned with four archways. The vīṇā goddess is yellow, the flute goddess red, the mṛdaṅga goddess smoke-colored, the muraja goddess white, the garland goddess yellow, the goddess of grace red, the goddess of song red, the goddess of dance multicolored, the flower goddess white, the incense goddess black, the lamp goddess like gold, the perfume goddess yellow, the mirror goddess white, the goddess of taste red, the goddess of touch green, and the Dharma goddess white. These sixteen are perfected from the first letter of each one's own name, together with a half moon and a bindu, and abide in a manner that accords with their names. The palace is beautified by these offering yoginīs, arranged two by two in sequence on either side of the main doors.

Nets and half-nets, garlands of flowers,
canopies, mirrors, and yak-tail whisks,
beautiful vajra lines,
hosts of buddha light radiating,
the supreme banners of the fence fluttering,
bells ringing all around,
delighting with offerings
of the five supreme objects of desire:

such is the palace, and he should imagine it encircled by eight charnel grounds.

Of these charnel grounds: in the east, in the charnel ground of Caṇḍogra, are a śirīṣa tree; a great wonder-worker with the face of an elephant; Indra; the nāga Vāsuki; and the cloud called Thundering. In the southeast, in the charnel ground called Aṭṭahāsa, are a karañja tree; a great wonder-worker with the face of a goat; Agni, the god of fire; the nāga Mahāpadma; and the cloud called Dense. In the south, in the charnel ground called the Remainder, are a mango tree; a great wonder-worker with the face of a buffalo; Yama; the nāga Padma; and the cloud called Circling. In the southwest, in the charnel ground resounding with kili kili, are a latāparkaṭī tree; a great wonder-worker with the face of a corpse; a rākṣasa; the nāga Ananta; and the cloud called Brimming. In the west, in the charnel ground Blazing and Turbulent, are an aśoka tree; a great wonder-worker with the face of a makara; Varuṇa; the nāga Karkoṭa; and the cloud called Terrifying. In the northwest, in the charnel ground of Fearsome Darkness, are an arjuna tree; a great wonder-worker with the face of a deer; Vāyu; the nāga Kulika; and the cloud called Raining. In the north, in the charnel ground of the Long Cave, are an aśvattha tree; a great wonder-worker with a human face; Kubera; the nāga Takṣaka; and the cloud called Wandering. In the northeast, in the charnel ground of Lakṣmī's Forest, are a vaṭa tree; a great wonder-worker with the face of an ox; Īśāna; the nāga Śaṅkhapāla; and the cloud called Fierce.

In all of them he should see stupas: yellow, red, black, smoke-colored, green, yellow, and white. Indra and the others are yellow, blue-green, white, gray, red, variegated, black, and yellow; Vāsuki and the others are to be visualized in their order. The clouds are of various colors. The eight great wonder-workers show the upper half of the body, the left hand holding a skull cup and the right hand in the gesture of receiving torma and offerings at the time they are given.

Further, the charnel grounds have dense trees of many kinds; they are terrifying with all manner of crows, owls, herons, vultures, dogs, jackals and she-jackals, hawks, kites, lions, boars, tigers and the like; terrifying with snakes, ox-slayers, two-headed snakes, wolves and the like, running here and there; dreadful with skeletons impaled on stakes, hanging, and half-burnt, with heads, skulls, and skeletons still intact, knees, broken heads, hides, and skull tops. Many siddhas and vidyādharas, yogins who keep the conduct of samaya, hosts of yoginīs, yakṣas, vetālas, rākṣasas and the like run, move, sit, and go here and there, raising a great tumult. Such are the charnel grounds, resounding with a great cry of kili kili.

In its innermost level, upon a red eight-petaled lotus, he should see the four māras fallen down in terror. Of these, the māra of the aggregates is Brahmā, the māra of the afflictions is Viṣṇu, the māra of the divine son is the lord of the gods, Indra, and the māra of the lord of death is Maheśvara (Śiva). He should see these four māras pressed down at their hearts by suns. On the petals of the directions beginning with the east are Brahmā, Upendra, and Rudra. On the petals of the intermediate directions beginning with Īśāna's are Yama, Kubera, Nairṛti, and Vemacitrin; these eight too are pressed down at their hearts by moons. All of this is to be understood at the very moment the palace is completed.

The sixteen vowels beginning with a, doubled, become the moon maṇḍala: the aspect of wisdom, the nature of mirror-like wisdom. On it, the thirty-four consonants beginning with ka, with the six letters ḍa ḍha da dha ya la added and then doubled, the eighty letters, become the sun maṇḍala: the essence of skillful means, the nature of the wisdom of equality. In the center of their union, from aṃ and hūṃ transformed, arise a curved knife and a skull cup perfectly joined, each with its own seed; he should imagine these as the nature of discriminating wisdom. Then, with the nets of light rays coming from the two seeds, all the tathāgatas are illuminated and summoned; they fully enter into it, and all become one. This is the wisdom of accomplishing activities.

Then, through the complete transformation of the moon, the sun, the seeds and all the emblems, one should view oneself as the Blessed One Vajrasattva, with the body color, emblems and so forth that will be explained: the manifestation of glorious Heruka, the very nature of the vajra holder of the Hevajra Tantra, embraced at the shoulders by Nairātmyā, who is of just the same kind, as the very nature of the utterly pure sphere of reality (dharmadhātu). This is the full and perfect awakening through five aspects.

After that, in order to make her partake of the five families, one should meditate on Vairocana, Akṣobhya, Ratnasambhava, Amitābha and Amoghasiddhi, transformed from oṃ āḥ sva āḥ hā, on the head, heart, navel, secret place and pair of thighs of the Blessed Lady, in that order. From the transformation of a and hūṃ come the lotus and the vajra, and from the transformation of the syllable āḥ and the syllable hūṃ come the pistil and the vajra jewel. Then, in order to attain the wisdom that does not emit, within the lotus and the vajra, they are endowed with a yellow hūṃ and phaṭ. Then one should bless the lotus and the vajra with oṃ padma sukhādhara mahārāga sukhaṃ dad caturānanda bhaga viśva hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ kāryaṃ kuruṣva me and oṃ vajra mahādveṣa caturānandaya kakhagamukhairaso nātha hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ karyaṃ kuruṣva me. With oṃ sarvva tathāgatānurāgaṇa vajra svabhāvātmako'haṃ one should engage in passionate union. This much is the clarification of the first union, and it is also the brief teaching of the action seal (karmamudrā).

Then, looking at the syllable hūṃ in the heart of the Blessed One and the syllable oṃ in the heart of the Blessed Lady, one summons all the tathāgatas with the light rays that come from the seeds in their hearts; they fully enter the heart seeds and are made to melt. When the transformed drops of awakening mind are then emitted onto the joined moons and suns that rest at the heart of the seats of Brahmā and the others, in the cardinal and intermediate directions beginning with the east, one should view them, in order, as the eight seeds gaṃ caṃ baṃ ghaṃ paṃ śaṃ caṃ ḍaṃ. Then, from the transformation of the eight seeds, one should think of them as a curved knife, a ḍamaru drum, a tortoise, a snake, a lion, a monk, a wheel and a vajra; in these, the letters alone are the seeds.

Then, from the transformation of the moon, the sun, the emblem and the seed, one should meditate, as before, on Gaurī and the others, fully and perfectly awakened through five aspects. In the eastern gate, one should view Gaurī, arisen from the syllable gaṃ, black, holding a curved knife in her right hand and a rohita fish in her left, pressing down Brahmā, as the nature of the aggregate of form. In the southern gate, one should understand Caurī, born from the syllable caṃ, the color of madder, holding a ḍamaru drum in her right hand and a pig in her left, riding on Indra, as the nature of the aggregate of feeling. In the western gate, one should think of Vetālī, born from the syllable baṃ, golden in color, holding a tortoise in her right hand and a lotus vessel in her left, pressing down Upendra, as the nature of the aggregate of perception. In the northern gate, one should think of Ghasmarī, born from the syllable ghaṃ, deep blue, holding a snake in her right hand and a yogin's alms bowl in her left, riding on Rudra, as the nature of the aggregate of perception. In the corner of Īśāna, one should emanate Pukkasī, arisen from the syllable baṃ, blue, holding a lion in her right hand and an axe in her left, pressing down Yama, as the nature of the earth element. In the corner of Agni, one should call to mind Śabarī, born from the syllable śaṃ, white, holding a monk in her right hand and a ringing staff in the other, riding on Kubera, as the nature of the water element. In the corner of Nairṛti, one should meditate on Caṇḍālī, born from the syllable caṃ, deep blue like the sky, holding a wheel in her right hand and a plow in her left, pressing down a rākṣasa, as the nature of the fire element. In the corner of Vāyu, one should think of Ḍombī, born from the syllable ḍaṃ, of variegated color, holding a vajra in her right hand and making the threatening gesture with her left, riding on Vemacitrin, as the nature of the wind element.

All of these have three red, round eyes; they stand in the dance posture with legs half crossed; their hair is reddish yellow and stands on end; they have two arms; they are adorned with the five seals; they have moon seats; they possess the nine moods of dance; they are naked, with faces baring their fangs. One should meditate on them as having arisen from the fallen drops of awakening mind.

Then one should view the three syllables oṃ āḥ hūṃ, the nature of body, speech and mind, interspersed with two red syllables hoḥ, as what is called the gandharva being. Together with complete delight, it enters the Blessed One by way of his mouth, passes along the vajra path into the deity's interior, and then one should view it as melting. Immediately the Blessed One, being of one taste in supreme great bliss, melts together with his own consort, and one should think of him as abiding in the form of a seed.

Then the four goddesses, Pukkasī and the rest, saying, "We are without a protector," fall into great sorrow and are deeply distressed. They urge him on with vajra songs that fully awaken his mind, and so they rouse the Blessed One:

"Arise, Lord, whose mind is compassion!
Protect me, Pukkasī.
Cast off your nature of emptiness,
and take me in the union of great bliss.

"Without you I shall die;
O Hevajra, arise!
Casting off the very nature of emptiness,
accomplish what Śabarī must do.

"Lord of joy, who has hosted the world,
do you dwell in emptiness?
Without you I know no direction;
I, Caṇḍālī, make this plea.

"I know your mind to be illusion;
alas, master of illusion, arise!
I, Ḍombī, am a woman of the town;
do not cut off your compassion."

Urged on in this way with songs of reality, the Blessed One rises like one waking from a dream. From that melted nectar comes the form of the moon, and upon it the two emblems arisen from the two seeds aṃ and hūṃ, marked with the two seeds; then comes the sun maṇḍala.

From the complete transformation of all that, oneself becomes the Blessed One arisen from hūṃ, glorious Hevajra of the fruition, in union with naked Nairātmyā arisen from aṃ. With the threatening gesture of his outstretched leg he rages at the gods and the asuras. His brows are knitted and his wrathful frown is twisted. His forehead is adorned with a garland of five skulls. He is in the full bloom of youth. With his four feet he presses down the four māras. He wears a garland of fifty freshly severed heads. On his crown he bears a crossed vajra, and his body is black. He has the light of a blazing sun, emanates the syllable hūṃ from his mouth, and his body is streaked with lines of ash. He has eight faces: the root face is deep black; the one on the right is like kunda jasmine; the next two faces on the right are black; the face on the left is red; the next two faces on the left are black; and the upper face is smoke colored, baring its fangs. Each face has three red, round eyes, and the mouths slightly bare their fangs.

Erotic, heroic, repulsive,
fierce, mirthful and terrifying,
compassionate, wondrous and peaceful:
he possesses these nine moods of dance.

His hair is tawny, standing on end and bound up. He is adorned with the five seals. He stands on the sun in the dance posture with legs half crossed. He has sixteen arms, and in his eight left and eight right hands he holds eight skull cups each.

Elephant, horse, donkey, ox, camel,
human, śarabha and cat:
these are in the eight skull cups on the right,
in order, beginning with the elephant.
Earth, water, wind and fire,
the moon and the sun,
Antaka and Dhanada:
these are in the eight skull cups on the left.

One should meditate on the Blessed One become such as this. The Blessed Lady, too, has one face, two arms and two legs, and is black in color. She has three red, round eyes, is naked and bears the five seals. Her tawny hair stands on end, and her head is adorned with a garland of five human heads. With her right hand holding a curved knife and her other hand holding a skull cup, she embraces the Blessed One. One should think of her as fully savoring the honey below from the Blessed One.

Then, in the heart of the Blessed One, on a lotus and a sun, is a skull cup arisen from hūṃ, and on a sun resting in the skull cup is a blue syllable hūṃ. In the heart of the Blessed Lady, too, on a lotus and a moon, is a curved knife born from the syllable oṃ, and on a moon resting on the curved knife one should meditate on the syllable aṃ. Likewise in the hearts of the eight yoginīs, on lotuses and moons, one should view the emblems born from their own seeds, blessed in turn by the seeds resting upon them. This much is the samādhi called the Supreme King of Maṇḍalas.

Then, seeing the whole expanse of space pervaded by the masses of light rays from the completed maṇḍala, one summons the wisdom circle with the hook of light rays from the seed in one's own heart and places it in the sky directly facing the eastern gate. With the eight-faced one and the hūṃ syllables one drives away the obstructors; one offers welcoming water and water for the feet, worships and praises. Then, by Gaurī and the others, blessed with the letters jaḥ hūṃ baṃ hoḥ, it is in sequence summoned, drawn in, bound and pleased, and one should think that the samaya circle and the wisdom circle have mingled into a single taste, like water poured into water.

In the eyes, she becomes Mohavajrā;
in the ears, Dveṣavajrā;
in the nose, the one called Mātsaryā;
in the mouth, Rāgavajrā;
in the body, Īrṣyāvajrā;
in the mind, the yoginī Nairātmyā.

Vajrā, white; Nairātmyā, black; Guhyagaurī, yellow; Vārī, red; Vajraḍākinī, deep blue; and, once again, Nairātmyā: these, Vajrā and the rest, are by other names Mohavajrā and the rest, each arisen from her own seed. This is for the sake of the armor and the purification of the senses.

After that, on the paths of body, speech and mind, one should meditate with the letters oṃ āḥ hūṃ on the three families, Bhūcarī and Nairātmyā. One should meditate on all of these as holding a curved knife and a skull cup in their two hands, with one face and three eyes, seated on a moon resting on a corpse.

With the light rays emerging from one's own heart one summons all the buddhas, and when they are seated in the sky and the eight mothers have duly worshipped them, one should make the request with oṃ abhiṣiñcantu māṃ sarvva tathāgata. Then those buddhas, in the form of Heruka, should confer empowerment with vases filled with the five nectars, which are the nature of the five tathāgatas:

"Just as, the moment they were born,
all the tathāgatas were bathed,
so I perform this bathing
with pure divine water."

Reciting oṃ sarba tathāgata abhiṣeka samaya śriye hūṃ, one should confer empowerment. As the empowerment is conferred, a rain of flowers and a rain of saffron falls, and the sound of great drums arises. When empowerment has been conferred, the lord of the family appears on the crown: on the crown of the Blessed One, Akṣobhya appears. Likewise, one should seal the four, Gaurī and the rest, with Akṣobhya, Vairocana, Ratneśa and Amitābha, and Pukkasī and the rest with aversion, delusion, avarice and passion, and one should recite the mantra oṃ sarvva tathāgata abhiṣeka vajra svabhāvātmako'haṃ.

Then there is a black wind maṇḍala born from the syllable yaṃ. Above it is a red fire maṇḍala, the transformation of the letter ra. Above it, resting on three heads, is a skull cup born from the syllable a, white outside and red inside. In it are bhūṃ āṃ jṝṃ khaṃ hūṃ; by their transformation they become the five nectars, which are the nature of the five tathāgatas, and the five lamps, blessed by their own seeds. Above that, from the syllable oṃ, there is something in the form of a lid, and from the transformation of the syllable āḥ, a moon maṇḍala; above it is a vajra arisen from hūṃ, marked with its seed.

Then, as the wind stirs, the fire melts everything except the vajra, and what rests inside the skull cup boils up four or five times. That boiled-up nectar fully gratifies the vajra, which melts and falls into that very nectar. After that, with the three letters oṃ āḥ hūṃ, the nectars dwelling in the three realms are summoned and come to rest in it, and it is blessed by those very syllables.

Then, together with the circle, one meditates on a white vajra the size of a barley grain, arisen from a white syllable hūṃ at the tip of the Blessed One's tongue; the Blessed One's body tastes the nectar with that very vajra tongue, and one should give it from mouth to mouth, as is pleasant, to the yogins and to the ḍākinī goddesses. Reciting oṃ sarba tathāgatāmṛta sada vajra svabhāva ātmako'haṃ, by tasting it the whole circle without exception becomes utterly joyful.

With the various offerings of Rūpavajrā, Śabdavajrā, Rasavajrā, Sparśavajrā and Dharmadhātuvajrā, who emerge from the seed in one's own heart holding in their hands a mirror, a vīṇā, a conch of perfume, a vessel of flavors, a heap of garments and a source of phenomena, one should make offering to oneself together with the circle, and recite oṃ sarba tathāgata pūja vajra svabhāvātmako'haṃ.

Locanā and the others offer praise:

"You, surrounded by your companions, the hosts of yoginīs;
you, who have attained the supreme by your own mind;
by this all beings, moving and unmoving, are made beautiful;
with a mind of compassion you awaken sentient beings.
O Vajra, who brings down the elaborations of illusion,
delighter, glorious Heruka Vajra!"

One should praise with this vajra song, and recite oṃ sarba tathāgata stutira vajra svabhāvātmako'haṃ.

The palace, arising from the purification of Vairocana, is the first limb. Passionate union, the nature of Vajrasattva, is the second. Empowerment, the nature of Akṣobhya, is the third. Tasting the nectar, the nature of Amitābha, is the fourth. Offering, the nature of Amoghasiddhi, is the fifth. Praise, the nature of Ratnasambhava, is the sixth. Thus one should meditate on the six limbs. This will swiftly bring the circle to full accomplishment.

Then one should look for the signs of accomplishment that arise from the power of meditation. First the maṇḍala appears like a full moon veiled by clouds. Beyond that it becomes like an illusion. Beyond that it becomes like a dream. Beyond that, when, through ripening, one attains something like the inseparability of the states of sleep and waking, the yogin of the seal becomes accomplished through the great seal (mahāmudrā).

Then, emanating the eight yoginīs, Gaurī and the rest, and the lord of the maṇḍala too, without interruption into the ten directions, one transforms them into the various forms suited to the various sentient beings to be trained. Having accomplished their aims just as they wish, one gathers them back, places them in that maṇḍala, and should view it as the nature of great bliss. This is the teaching of the Dharma seal.

In this way one should regard the three realms entirely as the circle of the maṇḍala, and, understanding that these too arise from the awakening mind, the source of great bliss in which wisdom and means are of one taste, one should gather the eight goddesses, Gaurī and the rest, into the eight faces of one's own body. After that, one gathers the seven faces into the principal face. Likewise one should meditate on the fourteen arms, with their emblems, as within the two principal arms. Then one gathers the two legs into the two principal legs.

Thus, as the nature of wisdom and means, having clearly seen the whole of the three realms as a single body, one seals the four wheels as they abide. In the wheel located below the navel is a multicolored lotus of sixty-four petals, and at its center the seed, a black syllable oṃ with five light rays, facing upward. At the heart, the wheel of Dharma, is a lotus of eight white petals with a blue syllable hūṃ at its center; on its eight petals are bhrūṃ āṃ jṛṃ khaṃ in the cardinal directions and e vaṃ ma ya in the intermediate directions. At the throat, the wheel of enjoyment, is a red lotus of sixteen petals, at whose center is a red syllable aṃ encircled by the vowels and consonants. At the crown, the wheel of great bliss, is a white lotus of thirty-two petals, and at its center one views the seed, a white syllable haṃ facing downward, from which a stream of nectar blazes forth; and one should properly meditate on these wheels. One radiates the mass of its light rays throughout all of space, gathers them back, and dissolves them once more into it.

Likewise the yogin endowed with the samaya should perform the mantra recitation by this rite. The letters that one recites emerge with light rays from the mouth of the goddess, enter one's own mouth, emerge by way of the vajra path, and are emitted into the lotus of one's own goddess; then again, in sequence from the goddess's mouth to one's own mouth, one should make the mantra circle without interruption. According to the different activities, pacifying and the rest, one should vary the colors, white and the rest.

Here the mantra is: oṃ aṣṭānanāya pidgordhakeśavartmane caturvidśatinetrāya ṣoḍaśabhujāya kṛṣṇajīmūtavapuṣe kapālamālānekādhāriṇe ādhmatakrūracittāya ardhendudaṃṣṭiṇe māraya māraya kāraya kāraya garjjaya garjjaya tarjjaya tarjjaya śoṣaya śoṣaya saptasāgarān bandha bandha nāgāṣṭakāna gṛhṇa gṛhṇa sarba śatrūṃ ha hā hi hī hu hū he hai ho hau haṃ haḥ phaṭ svāhā This is the root mantra of the Blessed One. oṃ deva picu vajra hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā This is the heart mantra. oṃ vajra kartari hevajrāya hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā This is the near-heart mantra.

Then one should view the seed syllable aṃ, together with its moon, melting and entering the seed in the heart of the Blessed One.

Then the seed of the emanation wheel, which is called Caṇḍālī, the fierce one, because of its very fierceness, blazes like the tip of a lamp flame and rises to the wheel of Dharma. It burns the five tathāgatas and Locanā and the others who dwell there, and it burns as well the wheel of enjoyment with its vowels and consonants, and the syllable haṃ, the nature of consciousness, that dwells at the center of the wheel of great bliss. Taking up the awakening mind, indestructible, the nature of great bliss, it becomes of equal taste with the Blessed One and comes back again; it also takes up the skull cup with the sun and places it in the seed at the heart.

That seed letter alone now abides as the nature of the five tathāgatas, together with its blazing nāda. As the scripture says:

"Nāda and bindu become the Vajra;
the half-moon is held to be Vairocana;
the rekhā is the Lord of Jewels himself;
the letter ha is Amitābha;
and the letter ū, Amoghasiddhi.
Knowing this, it is the nature of the five Victors."

Then again, drawing forth the tip of a lamp flame from the nāda, one gathers the letter ū, the nature of jealousy, into the letter ha, the nature of passion. After that, one gathers the letter ha too into the rekhā, the nature of avarice. Then the rekhā into the half-moon, the nature of delusion; the half-moon into the bindu, the nature of aversion; and the bindu one should gather completely into the nāda, the nature of the sphere of reality. Thus the suchness that is the single nature of all phenomena, free from permanence and annihilation and from every center and extreme, the great bliss that abides nowhere at all, is purified into mere nāda. This is the brief teaching of the great seal.

So it is said:

"A is the supreme of all syllables,
of great meaning, supreme, unchanging,
the great life breath, unborn,
free from verbal expression."

And in the Vajrahṛdayālaṃkāra too:

"In the center of the heart, the indestructible,
like a blazing lamp,
unchanging and supremely subtle,
is the letter a, the sublime chief."

And elsewhere too: "Because it is unborn from the very beginning, the letter a is the door to all phenomena." As it is said in the Māyājāla:

"For phenomena that are unborn
there are no letters; they are indestructible.
Whatever is expression and conception,
these words are called external."

Then, by the force of the power of one's aspirations, and in accordance with sentient beings to be trained who have various dispositions, one emanates as the dharmakāya itself and in the forms of the various saṃbhogakāyas and nirmāṇakāyas, and acts for the benefit of sentient beings. As the scripture says:

"In the empowerment of the great seal,
just as great bliss is known,
from that very thing this arises;
this maṇḍala does not arise from anything else.
Just like an illusion, like a dream,
like the way the intermediate state comes to be,
just so the maṇḍala appears,
arising from the yoga of constant familiarity."

Thus, having made the entire three realms without exception into mere dependent origination, the nature of the circle of the maṇḍala, like the moon in water, the yogin whose senses are unobstructed and unclouded, who abides in nothing at all, should dwell at ease, free of doubt, with the pride of his own deity. As Nāgārjunapāda said:

"These are not the three existences but the palace; these are not living creatures but Victors.
I am not a man but the lord of the wheel; there are no objects, no senses, no earth and the rest;
no form and the rest, nor anything whose self is the nature of reality; therefore they all belong to the maṇḍala.
Having made all these various things the circle of the maṇḍala, how could the mind fall into error?"

This is the brief teaching of the samaya seal.

Then one should recollect the purifications. From the purification of expelling all obstructors, there is the vajra fence and the rest. From the purification of all phenomena as space, there is the source of phenomena. Through the purification of the nature of reality, untainted, there is the lotus with variegated petals. From the purification of the five indivisible wisdoms, all phenomena being without intrinsic nature, there is the crossed vajra. From the purification of Vairocana with his consort, there is the palace. From the purification of the eight consciousnesses, because it is free from multiplicity and the rest, its various parts are mere shape. Because tathāgatas and ordinary beings are in reality of a nondual nature, there is no inequality, and so it is square. Through the purification of the four applications of mindfulness, there is the eastern gate. Through the purification of the four right abandonments, the southern gate. Through the purification of the four bases of miraculous power, the western gate. Through the purification of the five faculties, the northern gate. Through the purification of the four meditative concentrations, the four archways. Through the purification of the four samādhis, the four parapets. Through the purification of the qualities of patience and of the supreme mundane quality, the hosts of goddesses. Through the purification of the nine branches of scripture, the nine banners with bells. From the purification of the seven limbs of awakening, the nets and half-nets, the moons and suns, the mirrors, the flower garlands and the yak-tail whisks, all blazing. From the purification of being completely filled with compassion, arhatship, and the accumulations of merit and wisdom, the eight full vases. Through the purification of all the buddhas everywhere continuously turning the wheel of Dharma in the manner of the vajra, the black vajra garland. Through the purification of form and the rest, the five infinite and incomparable sense pleasures. Through the purification of the utterly pure sphere of reality, the five offerings to the maṇḍala. Through the purification of conscience and of shame before others, the curtains hanging down over the gates. Through the purification of the eightfold noble path, the eight pillars. Through the purification of the inconceivability of all phenomena, its adornment with the eight charnel grounds. Through the purification of the Teacher of great passion, the red lotus of eight petals. Through the purification of the reality that is without intrinsic nature and nondual, which tears out faults by the root, the seats on the pistil, Brahmā and the rest, the four māras.

In order to destroy the darkness of ignorance that constantly abides in all sentient beings, to dry up the mire of nonvirtue, and to bring the sprout of the awakening mind to full ripeness, there is the sun maṇḍala upon Brahmā and the rest. From the purification of pacifying the eight worldly concerns, there are the seats of the eight guardians of the directions on the eight petals. Through the purification of pacifying the fire of the afflictions of all sentient beings, there are the moon seats.

Through the purification of the aggregate of form, great delusion, mirror-like wisdom, visible form, the perfection of generosity, the view of the perishable collection, and the obscuration to knowledge, there is Gaurī. Through the purification of avarice, the rohita fish. Through the purification of the six faults, pride and the rest, the curved knife.

Through the purification of the aggregate of feeling, pride, avarice, the wisdom of equality, the view that holds to extremes, the afflictive obscuration, and moral discipline, there is Caurī. Through the purification of the nine branches of scripture, the ḍamaru drum. Through the purification of delusion, the pig.

Through the purification of the aggregate of perception, great passion, discriminating wisdom, wrong view, the obscuration of birth, patience, and smell, there is Vetālī. Through the purification of jealousy, the tortoise. Through the purification of emptiness, the skull cup.

Through the purification of the aggregate of formations, jealousy, the wisdom of accomplishing activities, holding views to be supreme, the obscuration of karma, meditative concentration, and taste, there is Ghasmarī. By aversion, the snake. By passion, the yogin's alms bowl.

Through the purification of the earth element, the door of liberation of emptiness, great love, and skillful means, there is Pukkasī. By pride, the lion. Through the purification of the faults of body, speech and mind, the axe.

Through the purification of the water element, the door of liberation of signlessness, great compassion, and aspiration, there is Śabarī. Through the purification of the awakening mind, the monk. Through the purification of undistracted mindfulness, the ringing staff.

Through the purification of the fire element, the door of liberation of wishlessness, great joy, and power, there is Caṇḍālī. Through the purification of uprooting every fault without exception, the plow. Through the purification of cutting off unknowing, the wheel.

Through the purification of the wind element, the door of liberation of the uncompounded, great equanimity, and the perfection of primordial wisdom, there is Ḍombī. Through pure wisdom, the vajra. Through the purification of the single nature of all phenomena, the threatening gesture.

Through the purification of the aggregate of consciousness, the utterly pure sphere of reality, holding moral discipline and ascetic vows to be supreme, touch, and the nature of the perfection of diligence, there is glorious Heruka. Through the purification of the loving mind, the black limbs. The three eyes are the nature of the vajra of body, speech and mind, and they are red all over because of compassion. Through the purification of the eight liberations, the eight faces. Among them, through pure subjugation, the left one is red; through pure pacifying, the right one is white. Through the purification of Akṣobhya, Amitābha, Ratneśa, Vairocana and Amoghasiddhi, there are the wheel, the earrings, the necklace, the bracelets and the girdle. Through the nature of Vajrasattva, the lines of ash. Because he appears as wrathful for the sake of those to be trained who are wrathful and savage, his hair is red and tawny and stands on end. In order that the lack of intrinsic nature may be realized, there is the crossed vajra on the crown. Through the purity of the five families, the five skulls on the forehead. Through the purification of the sixteen emptinesses, the arms. Through means and wisdom, the skull cups of the gods and the rest on the left and on the right. Through the purification of cough, difficulty in breathing, madness, wasting disease, leprosy, swelling of the legs, disease of the liver and disease of the spleen, he is endowed with the eight, the elephant and the rest. Having removed the cough and the rest of all sentient beings, from the purity of every mastery of accomplishment, he is endowed with the eight, earth and the rest. Through the purification of the vowels and consonants, the garland of fifty heads. Through the purity of the four means of attraction, the four legs. Through the purity of the seven limbs of awakening, the hide. Through the purification of the four truths, the bones. The flesh is Pukkasī, the blood is Śabarī, the semen is Caṇḍālī, and the marrow and fat are Ḍombī. Through the purity of form, feeling, perception and formations, the four, Gaurī and the rest. Through the purity of the three bodies, the emanation body, the Dharma body and the enjoyment body, the threefold bend. Through the purity of the body of great bliss, being joined in a single body. Through the purification of the entire three realms as without any object of focus, the half-crossed legs. Because nothing at all is superimposed, the toes of one foot are planted on the sun seat.

Through the purification of the selflessness of all phenomena, there is Nairātmyā, the essential nature of the perfection of wisdom, the nature of the sphere of reality. Through the purification of cutting off the faults of existence, nonexistence and the rest, the curved knife. Through the purity of the heads of conceptual thought about things and the absence of things, the lotus vessel. From the purity of the blood of the four māras, the skull cups filled with blood. Through the purity of the fifty letters, the garlands of heads. By the nature of existence and peace, the two arms. Through the purity of the two truths, the two legs. Through the purity of the final attainment, correctly knowing the flow of one's own taste, in which emptiness and compassion are inseparable, the union of wisdom and means is shown outwardly. Through the purification of night and day, the designations of wisdom and means. Through the purification of the sixty-four periods, the eight watches, the sixteen transitions and the thirty-two water hours, there are, in order, the petals in the four wheels, the emanation wheel and the rest.

Then the yogin endowed with the samaya of Hevajra, holding the vajra and bell, should bless the torma for the benefit of all sentient beings. For this, one views the inside of the source of phenomena as adorned in the center, above and below with three red lotuses of eight petals.

On the eastern petal of the eight petals of the central lotus is Indra, white and thousand-eyed, with a vajra in his hand and a white elephant for his seat. On the southern petal is Yama, black, with a staff in his hand, riding a buffalo. On the western petal is Varuṇa, white, with a noose in his hand, riding a makara. On the northern petal is a yakṣa, yellow, with a śrīphala fruit in his hand, riding a man. All of these are complete with every ornament and have jeweled crowns. Yama has a sun seat and the light of the sun; the rest have moon seats and the light of the moon.

On the petal of Īśāna is the Lord of Spirits (bhūtas), white and three-eyed, with a crown of matted locks. He wears a garment of tiger skin, is smeared with ash in stripes, is adorned with ornaments of bone, holds a trident in his hand and rides a bull, with a moon seat and the light of the moon. On the petal of Agni is Agni, very tawny, dwarfish, with a hanging belly and a crown of matted locks, holding a rosary and a water pot; wise, he rides a ram, with a sun seat and the light of the sun. On the petal of Nairṛti is a rākṣasa with tawny hair standing on end, wrathful and with knitted brows, holding a knife and a skull cup and riding a black man, with a sun seat and the light of the sun. On the petal of Vāyu is Vāyu, green, with a jeweled crown and a banner in his hand, riding a deer, with a moon seat and the light of the moon, adorned with every ornament. At the center is the earth goddess Pṛthivī, yellow, with divine garments and every ornament, a vase in her hand, on a moon seat with the light of the moon; Pṛthivī dwells surrounded on all sides by hungry ghosts.

On the eastern petal of the eight-petaled lotus that stands above the source of phenomena is the Moon, white, with a jeweled crown and a white water lily in his hand, with every ornament, riding a white horse, on a moon seat with the light of the moon. On the southern petal is the Sun, red, holding lotuses in his two hands, with a jeweled crown and every ornament, riding a green horse, on his own seat with his own light. Beginning with the Moon and the Sun, in the directions and intermediate directions in order are Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, Saturn and Rāhu, who are red, blue, yellow, white, black and smoke-colored, each with their own seats, emblems and every ornament. In the central hub is Brahmā, yellow and four-faced, with four arms, holding a triple staff, making the gesture of fearlessness, and holding a rosary and a water pot. He wears the sacred thread, is dwarfish with short limbs, and rides a goose, on a moon seat with the light of the moon, and he is surrounded on all sides by Brahmās. One should view all the gods, those of the Brahmakāyika and Brahmapurohita heavens, of Tuṣita, of the Yāma heaven, of Akaniṣṭha and the rest, in their own forms.

On the eight-petaled lotus that lies below the source of phenomena, in the directions and intermediate directions in order are Karkoṭa, green; Śaṅkhapāla, yellow; Ananta, blue; Vāsuki, red; Takṣaka and Kulika, yellow; and Padma and Mahāpadma, white. These eight have jeweled crowns and human faces with serpent hoods, and every ornament, and they are accompanied by asuras and hell-beings. In the center is Śeṣa, white, with a human face crowned with a serpent hood and a jeweled crown. All of these are seated on lotuses.

Gaurī, emerging from the seed syllable at one's own heart, summons them; Caurī draws them inside the lotus; Vetālī binds them with the bonds of iron chains; and through the power of Ghasmarī's bell they are brought under control. Those dwelling on the central lotus, their own colors, shapes and emblems completely transformed, become dark figures born from the syllable hūṃ, crowned with Akṣobhya. Those dwelling above give up their own forms and become white figures born from the syllable oṃ, crowned with Vairocana. Those dwelling below give up their own forms and become red figures born from the syllable āḥ, crowned with Amitābha. All of these dwelling on the three lotuses, moreover, should be viewed as one-faced and two-armed, holding a vajra and a vajra bell, embraced by consorts of their own family who are like them, and seated in the posture with the right leg extended.

Having thus made them steady, with the mantra oṃ pravarasatkāra arghaṃ pratīccha svāhā one offers the welcoming water in front with the conch mudrā. Taking it up with the tongs mudrā, one offers water for rinsing the mouth. As before, one makes offering with the gaṇa torma, savoring the nectar, and having made them replete with that very nectar, one should praise them with this: oṃ inda jama jāla jakkha bhūta bahni bāu rakkha canda sujja māda bappa tala pātāle aṭṭa sappa svāhā.

Ringing the bell, one offers the torma. Drawing it up by streams of light from vajra tongues the size of a barley grain born from hūṃ, just as the yogin himself eats, so one should have these eat. One should think: "They too, by the streams of the vajra tongues arisen from hūṃ, enjoy it in bliss, just as I do."

With idaṃ baliṃ bhūṃja jiṃgha phulla dhūppa māṃsa piṃgha ambha kajja sarba sādha khanti khuṇi pheḍa gāda one entrusts them with the activity, and one should ring the bell at the three places. With the mantra oṃ akāro mukhaṃ sarvva dharmmāṇāṃ ādyanutpannatvāt oṃ āḥ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā one should satisfy them.

With the vajra song kaulla ireṭṭhi abolā mummuṇire kakkolā ghaṇe kṛpiṭa ho bājjai kāruṇe kiai narolā tahiṃ bala khājjai gāḍheṃ maaṇā bajjii hale kālañjira pāṇiai dundura bājjiai causama kācchurī sihla kappura lāiai māla indhaṇa sāliṃja tahiṃ bharu khāiaī phreṃkhaṇa kheṭa karante suddhāsuddha na muṇiai niraṅśu aṃge caḍābi ai tahiṃ jasarāpa pāṇiai malaaja kunduru pāṭtai ṭiṇḍima tahiṃ bājjiai one fills all the limbs and makes them perfectly full. Afterwards one offers water for the feet and recites oṃ vajrasatva samaya manupālaya vajrasatva tvenobatiṣṭha dṛḍho me bhava sutoṣyo me bhava supoṣyo me bhava anurakto me bhava sarva siṃddha me prayaccha sarvva karma su ca me cittaṃ śreyaḥ kuru hūṃ ha ha ha ha hoḥ bhagavān sarva tathāgata vajra mā me muñca vajri bhava mahāsamaya satva āḥ. Then with this, oṃ vajra muḥ, one should request them to depart.

Then further, when partaking of food and the like, one should eat by this rite. On the hearth of hunger, into the fire of the sun at the heart, one pours the substances of food and the like with the hand, and should make a fire offering. With vajra tongues marked with vajras the size of a barley grain, arisen from a white hūṃ, the full fire offering that the yogin has offered to those who have the nature of Heruka together with his circle, the embodiment of the three bodies, all enters through the opening of the throat. Ripened by the fire of hunger, it becomes nectar, and by that nectar the Blessed One together with his circle is satisfied. This is the rite of the inner fire offering.

The conceptions that divide self and other in past births without beginning, endless and base;
whatever here, where false bliss vividly appears, is always various and beautiful, is wholly transformed;
from purity upon purity comes this nature of the Victors, the nondual sameness of existence and peace.
Relying on the qualities of the king, lord of the wheel within the wheel, Vajraḍāka, lord of sages,
this very clear sādhana of glorious Heruka, made by the glorious Jewel Queen,
with its limbs and subsidiary limbs, is rightly given the name Treasury of Purity.
Whatever merit I have surely gathered by making this, through it, free from suffering,
may the beings of the three worlds swiftly attain the state of the Great Seal (mahāmudrā).

The Hevajra sādhana called Treasury of Purity, composed by the great scholar Advayavajra Avadhūtīpa, is complete.

In the presence of the great scholar of eastern India, the glorious Vanaratna, at the request of the lord of men Drakpa Jungné, who has climbed from the steps of the path of glorious Hevajra up to the mansion of great bliss, it was translated at the palace of Nedong by that same great paṇḍita and Shönu Pal. The length of this text in the Indian manuscript is said to be three hundred and seventy-odd verses. By this too, may many beings be benefited.

Translator's notes

Pukkasī's attribute, written stobs ("strength"), is read as stob, "offering", as in the sentence on the outer offerings just after. "The suchness offering" reads mchod pa for the written chod pa. The phrase rendered "experiencing in union" is literally "experiencing as two," describing the union with Nairātmyā in one taste. The source of the offerings reads 'thon pa, "come forth", for the written mthon pa. "Hard to bear" reads bzod par dka' ba for the written bzod par dga' ba. "Jeweled" reads rin po che for the written ring po che. The wrathful one of the southeast, written simply 'dod pa, is taken as Ṭakkirāja ('dod pa'i rgyal po), as in the standard set of ten wrathful ones. The "great wonder-workers" (rdzu 'phrul chen po pa), the animal-faced figures in each charnel ground, are elsewhere known as field protectors (kṣetrapāla). The names of the charnel grounds and clouds that could not be securely identified are translated literally, among them the southern "Remainder." The petals of the four directions name only three deities (Brahmā, Upendra, Rudra), and the stupas only seven colors, as the text stands; "on the petals" reads la for the written pa. The goddess of the northern gate is assigned the aggregate of perception, the same as Vetālī in the west; in the usual Hevajra scheme she represents formations. The text is translated as it stands. Likewise the northeastern seed is written baṃ here, though the list of eight seeds gives paṃ. The northeastern goddess is called sbos mo here; from her position, attributes and the later reference to Pukkasī as one of the four, she is identified as Pukkasī. "Fallen drops of awakening mind" reads phung ba as lhung ba, "fallen," which the context requires. The text names "the three families" but lists only Bhūcarī and Nairātmyā; it is translated as it stands. Ratneśa renders rin chen dbang po, "Jewel Lord," here standing for Ratnasambhava, the buddha of the jewel family. "The honey below" is the tantric idiom for the nectar of sexual union. The offering goddesses are five in this text (form, sound, taste, touch and the sphere of reality), while six objects are listed; a goddess of scent seems to be implied by the conch of perfume but is not named. Translated as it stands. The term rendered "parapets" (kha khyer) for the four samādhis is uncertain; it names some architectural feature of the palace paired with the archways. De zhin nyid in the summary of the great seal is read as de bzhin nyid, "suchness." Nāda, bindu, half-moon and rekhā (the stroke) are the parts of the seed syllable dissolved in sequence. Indra's seat is literally "the white earth-protector," taken here as the elephant (his mount Airāvata is called "son of the earth-protector"). The color of Vāsuki is read as red, taking the extra "and" between his name and the color as connective. "Dark figures" renders "not white" for those born from hūṃ. "Tongs mudrā" renders "the dry [mudrā]," uncertain. The Lord of Spirits on Īśāna's petal is the Great God (Śiva), and the central white nāga "Remainder" is taken as Śeṣa. "Made by the glorious Jewel Queen" renders dpal ldan rin chen btsun mo literally; it is unclear whether this names a person connected with the work or is an epithet, and it sits oddly beside the colophon's attribution to Advayavajra. The fourth verse line is compressed, and "Vajraḍāka, lord of sages" is taken as an epithet of the deity on whose qualities the work relies. "Three hundred and seventy-odd" reads "three hundred exceeded by seventy."
