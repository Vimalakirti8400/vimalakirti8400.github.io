About this text

The Tilaka of the Clear Realization of Glorious Hevajra, in Sanskrit Śrī Hevajrābhisamayatilaka and in Tibetan Dpal kye rdo rje'i mngon par rtogs pa'i thig le, is Toh 1277 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the section of tantra commentaries associated with the Hevajra cycle. Composed by the renowned scholar and elder Śākyarakṣita, it is a practice manual, or sādhana, setting out the clear realization of the deity Hevajra, opening with a verse of homage to Hevajra as the blue, wrathful lord of adepts dancing with his consort. It guides the initiated yogin, devoted to the guru and of pure livelihood, through the visualization and worship of the Hevajra maṇḍala in the generation stage, and includes the torma offering rites, instructions on making, consecrating and using a rosary for mantra recitation in the four kinds of ritual activity, and a summary of the tantric commitments, or samayas, that the practitioner must keep. Its colophon records that it was translated into Tibetan by the monk Kyopa Palzangpo, a student of Jangchup Tsemo, who notes that he worked without a paṇḍita and without a second Indian manuscript.

Translation

In the language of India: śrī hevajra abhisamaya tilaka nāma

In the language of Tibet: The Ornament of the Clear Realization of Glorious Hevajra.

Homage to the Blessed One, glorious Hevajra.

Lord of adepts, blue in color, who utterly steals away darkness, your crown adorned with a crossed vajra; dancing in the center of the circle, upon the breasts of the breathless fallen ones, O devourer; with the vessels born from the crown of the head, and with your consort, your hands bestowing, endowed with the six seals; wearing a garland of heads, terrifying in your aspect, with reddish-yellow hair, Hero who shakes the three worlds, to you I bow.

Here, the yogin who has first received empowerment in accordance with the rite, who is devoted to the guru, possesses a pure livelihood, and has no doubt about the words of truth, having purified the mouth with the five nectars and so on, in some place agreeable to the mind such as a charnel ground, seated on a comfortable seat marked with a crossed vajra, should protect the place with the mantra oṃ rakṣa rakṣa hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā.

Then, within his own heart, he should clearly visualize a blue syllable hūṃ resting on a sun disc arisen from a red raṃ. With its five-colored light rays he illuminates the interior, and the rays, issuing forth from each of the pores of the body, invite the Blessed One, the glorious Hero who shakes all three worlds, in the form that will be described below, together with the venerable guru. Having prostrated to them as they sit in the sky in front, he should worship them with the outer, the secret, and the suchness offerings that issue forth from the seed at the heart.

Of these, worshiping with the five objects of enjoyment, such as divine flowers and incense, is the outer offering. Worship performed by the goddesses Gaurī and the rest, emanated from that same seed, with their own samaya substances, is the secret offering. Here, in the east, south, west, and north of the Blessed One are Gaurī, Caurī, Vetālī, and Ghasmarī, black, red, yellow, and green in color, holding respectively the awakening mind in a skull cup, the self-arisen flower, vajra water, and medicine. In the northeast and southeast are Pukkasī and Śavarī, blue and white in color, holding mercury in skull cups. As for what is stated in the Two Examinations, "Pukkasī, the vajra in her hand," that vajra too is explained to be the great flesh. In the southwest is Caṇḍālī, sky-blue, with a sounding hand drum. By these, that is, by Gaurī and the rest, is the secret offering; but some explain that Caṇḍālī's hand drum is an outer offering. In the northwest is Ḍombī, of variegated color, who embraces with her own body. She, becoming one with Nairātmyā and embracing the Lord from the neck, causes him to be endowed with the nature of great bliss: this is the suchness offering.

Moreover, this is stated here:

She who worships the Lord with great bliss
is one with the goddess born within; she is no other goddess.

Thus all of them are emanations from the yogin's own mind stream. And because they are separated out from one's own stream, having bodies whose nature is mental and being unobstructed, they abide in a single body, just like the being of the intermediate state. This is the oral instruction.

One also finds the view that, after Nairātmyā of the heart has been set aside, Ḍombī embraces the Principal from the neck and worships him with the suchness offering of the nature of great bliss. But the claim that the eight goddesses worship with the secret offering and then once more become of one taste with Nairātmyā, becoming nondual with her, and that this is the suchness offering, is not good. For the suchness offering is set apart by a form that accords with the action, since the tantra says:

Ardent with great passion,
Ḍombī embraces his body.

After that, he performs the unsurpassed rite of the seven limbs, with its variations: confessing his own misdeeds, rejoicing in the merit of others, urging the perfect buddhas to turn the wheel of Dharma, requesting the Tathāgatas who intend to pass into nirvāṇa to remain in saṃsāra, rising in his mind and prostrating, and so on. Then:

"Until the heart of awakening, I go for refuge to the Buddha, glorious Heruka Vajra, the source of great bliss, whose nature is the whole of the three realms; to the Dharma, the mantras, mudrās, maṇḍalas, and so on that he taught, which are forms of his intrinsic nature; and to the Saṅgha, the assembly of yogins who abide in his circle. I shall generate the mind whose nature is great bliss, the perfect and complete realization of emptiness endowed with the supreme of all aspects and of signlessness, which is the cause of accomplishing the welfare of all sentient beings. I shall always abide in the Vajrayāna, the path of unsurpassed awakening. I shall offer myself to glorious Heruka and the rest and to the assembly of vajra queens."

Having thus gone for refuge to the Three, and so on, up to the culmination, the offering of his own being, he cultivates the following. Loving-kindness, wishing to join sentient beings, as if each were his only child, to the great bliss of one taste in suchness. Compassion, whose aspect is the wish to liberate all the protectorless beings sunk in the ocean of conceptual thought. Joy, whose nature is the wish that they never be parted from the enjoyments born of virtue and the like. And equanimity, which, having abandoned anger toward all along with what accompanies it, is characterized by entering into the equal taste of self and other, even with regard to the very nature of wishing to benefit others. Having cultivated these, in order to produce the yogin's accumulation of merit and to increase his accumulation of wisdom, he makes the meaning of oṃ svabhāva śuddhaḥ sarba dharmāḥ svabhāva śuddho'haṃ directly evident, first reciting it, and then, according to the guru's oral instruction, he meditates on all phenomena as emptiness.

The manner of dissolution is of two kinds. Of these, the first is this: causing the entire retinue and the Blessed One to enter luminosity in an instant is the dissolution.

The second is this: the Blessed One, together with all the offering goddesses, is dissolved into the seed at one's own heart; that into the sun; the sun into the ū of the syllable hūṃ; the ū into the syllable ha; the ha into the head; the head into the crescent; the crescent into the drop; and the drop into the nāda. At the end, the meditator too, having meditated on himself as a mere hundred-thousandth part of the tip of a hair, makes the meaning of oṃ śūnyatā jñāna vajra svabhāva ātmako'haṃ directly evident, and then even that pervasive conceptual thought just mentioned is to be abandoned. This is the Blessed Mother, the Perfection of Wisdom, whose nature is that no phenomenon is apprehended.

Then, by the force of his former aspiration, he rises from natural luminosity, from the samādhi of emptiness, which is conceived as space. Here the sequence of arising is as follows. In space, on a sun disc arisen from ra, he meditates on a crossed vajra with three-pronged tips, transformed from hūṃ and radiating five-colored light. Light rays like the fire at the destruction of the eon radiate from it in every direction and burn all evil deeds to ash. Then he generates a blazing vajra fence, as wide as he wishes; above it a vajra tent; beneath that a vajra canopy; and below, a ground of vajra nature reaching all the way to the bottom of the earth. He should contemplate all this. These, the vajra fence and the rest, serve to destroy obstructors, and they are to be understood as having the nature of purified worldly consciousness.

Then, within the vajra tent, is the great source of phenomena: triangular through the purity of body, speech, and mind; white like the autumn moon because all obscurations have been abandoned; embodying the three bodies; of the nature of Vajradhara. That source of phenomena is narrow below and wide above, and white in color. Within it, in the center of the eight charnel grounds, which are the purity of the eight consciousnesses, is a lotus with multicolored petals, arisen from the syllable baṃ, which is the purity of being untainted by the eight worldly concerns. On its anthers lies a corpse, which is the purity of the expanse of phenomena, since it is Nairātmyā herself. On the corpse's heart is a red-yellow sun disc, because its garland of light outshines and benefits the splendor of the three worlds. On it, from the transformation of the two sets of vowels, is a moon, whose nature is mirror-like wisdom and whose essence is method. Upon that, from the transformation of the two sets of consonants together with ḍa ḍha da dha ya ra la, is a sun, whose nature is the wisdom of equality and whose essence is wisdom. Just as splendor shines over the three classes of beings, so also the person previously visualized abides, arising from wisdom; this is to be contemplated as the great bliss of union.

Then, in the center of the mother and father, the sun and moon joined face to face, he should visualize the two blue seeds aṃ and hūṃ, of the nature of wisdom and method, which have entered there. From their transformation arise a curved knife and a vajra, and at their hubs, resting on moon and sun, are those same two seeds; these he should meditate on as the nature of discriminating wisdom. With the net of their light rays the welfare of beings is accomplished, and all the Tathāgatas, vanishing from sight, enter into that very thing. He should contemplate all of them, become one, as the wisdom that accomplishes all actions.

From the complete transformation of moon, sun, seed, and emblem arises oneself, utterly pure, whose nature is the wisdom of the expanse of phenomena: the glorious Protector who shakes all three worlds. He is blue in body color through the purity of great loving-kindness; some say his body is black because the conceptual thoughts of the affliction of anger are destroyed. He is wrathful because he tames the vicious. He has one face through the purity of nondual wisdom. His face is fierce because he has utterly destroyed wrong views. He bares his fangs and is terrifying because he crushes the four māras; he binds the māras, and because he crushes them he laughs fiercely. He has three eyes through the purity of body, speech, and mind, or else through the purity of seeing the three paths in every aspect. They are red because he is endowed with tender love. His reddish-yellow hair stands upright through the purity of taming the vicious. He has a crown ornament marked with a crossed vajra because he cares for all the various beings; some say it signifies discerning the absence of intrinsic nature. Through the purity of the families, five heroic skulls rest at his forehead. He has two arms through the purity of the two accumulations. With the right hand he holds a blue five-pronged vajra, because he is skilled at splitting the mountain of ignorance, and through the purity of meditating on the five wisdoms as of equal taste. With the left, from the left side itself, he embraces Wisdom, his consort in equal union, and holds a skull cup marked with a vajra and filled with blood, through the purity of the blood of the four māras and of the taste of great bliss and compassion. He has two legs through the purity of the two truths. He stands in the dancing posture of half-crossed legs, with the tip of the right foot placed on the left thigh, upon the sun on the heart of the corpse lying on the multicolored lotus; this is through the purity of the three realms being unapprehended and clear, and of the awakening mind, which has become method, moving upward. With the tip of the big toe of his left foot he rests on the delightful seat, through the purity of having abandoned all anger toward everyone and of the accumulation of great compassion. He has three wrathful frowns through the purity of the three bodies: the Dharma body, the body of perfect enjoyment, and the emanation body. His body is adorned with a garland of fifty fresh heads strung on moist entrails, through the purity of the fifty vowels and consonants. Through the purity of Akṣobhya, Amitābha, Ratnasambhava, Vairocana, and Amoghasiddhi, he wears the five seals made of human bone, known as the wheel, the earrings, the necklace, the bracelets, and the girdle. Through the purity of Vajrasattva, he bears the sixth seal, his whole body smeared with ash produced from bone, signifying that the awakening mind pervades all the limbs; others say his body is smeared with ash because the impermanence of beings has been burned. Through the purity of nonconceptual Brahmā, he wears a sacred thread of human hair. Through the purity of attachment and the rest, he wears a lower garment of tiger skin.

Born from the syllable oṃ is Nairātmyā, blue. With her right hand she holds a curved knife, because she cuts off conceptual thoughts of entity and non-entity; with the other she holds a skull cup filled with blood whose nature is great bliss and great compassion: this is her magical display. She has one face and wears the five seals. Through the purity of emptiness she wears a garland of fifty dry heads, embodying the fifty vowels, and her forehead is adorned with five skulls. Her reddish-yellow hair is coiled upward in a mass. She has three round red eyes, and her face bares its fangs.

Through the yoga of perfect union with her who wears the girdle of tinkling bells, by the power of nondual great bliss coursing through the whole body, the body bristles with hair standing on end. Some, however, proclaim, "Ḍombī embraces his body."

Because his nature beautifully overpowers the three worlds, the enjoyment of his body is abundant, and because he has the mood of sublime playful grace, he is graceful. Because he subdues the three worlds, he is heroic. Because he wears ornaments such as a garland of human heads, and because he subdues, he is repulsive. The radiating play that follows on grace is his laughter. Because he sports and plays with the consort on the utterly fierce sun disc that rises when the destruction of the eon begins, he is fierce. With a body that frightens all the gods and asuras, he is terrifying. Though free of the signs of all conceptual thought and of the very nature of great bliss, by the force of his former aspiration he sends forth every one of the excellent emanations he has accomplished, and the welfare of beings comes about perfectly, just as it should: this is his great compassion. His body, which surpasses beings and is unsurpassed, is majestic, that is, wondrous. Because the heaps of afflictions such as attachment are pacified, and because he holds a treasure without beginning, he is peaceful.

The inner purity of the Blessed One is this: his flesh is Pukkasī, his blood Śavarī, his semen Caṇḍālī, his marrow and fat Ḍombī; the seven layers of his skin are the seven limbs of awakening, and his bones the Four Truths.

Likewise, the yogin endowed with the pride of the commitment being visualizes the following. Within the heart of the Blessed One, on a sun arisen from hūṃ, is a vajra; within its hub, upon a sun, is the syllable hūṃ. At the heart of the Blessed Mother, on a moon, is a curved knife born from the seed syllable aṃ; on a moon resting on its handle is the syllable aṃ. By sending out and gathering back their light rays, the wisdom being is invited with effort along the path in front, and sits before him. The goddesses Gaurī and the rest, radiating from the light rays of the seed at the heart, first present the guest water and then worship it perfectly and praise it perfectly. With jaḥ it is invited; with hūṃ it is made to enter the blessed commitment being, like water into water; with baṃ it is bound; and with hoḥ it is brought under control.

Then, without interruption, light rays from the seed at the heart, in the form of hooks, invite all the buddhas. As before, the eight mothers make offerings to them and pay them honor, saying:

Just as the great worship was bestowed
upon the Buddha by Bodhivajra,
so, for the sake of protecting beings,
O Space Vajra, grant it to me today.

abhiṣiñcantu māṃ sarba tathāgata

Then those buddhas, in the form of Heruka, declare:

The great vajra empowerment,
arisen from the abode of the three secrets
of all the buddhas,
honored by the three realms, we bestow.

oṃ sarba tathāgata abhiṣeka samaya śriye hūṃ

And with vases that embody the five Tathāgatas and hold the five nectars, they exhaust all the stains and obscurations residing in him, and they empower him with the waters of wisdom, which admit no return and no rebirth-linking. At the very moment of the empowerment, a rain of saffron, flowers, and the like falls, and the sound of great drums also arises. Rūpavajrā and the others make offerings, and Locanā and the others praise with the vajra songs that will be explained below. The remaining water of the empowerment is meditated on as the lord of one's own family, and from it, through the purity of a thoroughly cleansed livelihood, Akṣobhya is to be generated on the crowns of the heads of the Blessed One and the Blessed Mother. Then, with oṃ sarba tathāgata abhiṣeka vajra svabhāva ātmako'haṃ, the empowerment is to be made especially firm.

Then, without interruption, armor is placed on the senses. In the eyes is a white goddess, like the one named Mohavajrī. In the ears is a black one, like Dveṣavajrī, who bears the name Nairātmyā. In the nostrils is a yellow one, like Mātsaryavajrī, who bears the name Inner Secret Gaurī. In the mouth is a red one, resembling Rāgavajrī, the inner Water Yoginī. In the body is a green one, like Īrṣyāvajrī, who is also known as Vajraḍākinī. In the mind is a black one, like Nairātmyayoginī, who bears the name Nairātmyā. These too, in their respective order, are born from the seeds āṃ aṃ īṃ īṃ uṃ. They should be visualized with one face and three eyes, holding a curved knife and a skull cup in the right and the other hand, and seated on a moon resting on a corpse.

At the crown, throat, and heart, one should also visualize goddesses born from the syllables oṃ āḥ hūṃ, white, red, and black in color, arisen from the three worlds: the Goer, the Sky-Goer, and Nairātmyā. Some say they are born from the seeds oṃ aṃ aṃ. Their form is like that of Mohavajrī and the others.

Likewise, according to the sequence of Saroruhavajra, the order is clearly seen to be: first the wisdom being is made to enter, then the empowerment, then the armor. In the Clear Realization of Lūyipa, however, the order is that immediately after the armor the wisdom being is made to enter, and then the empowerment is conferred. It is written the same way in the sādhana of two-armed Hevajra that follows the intent of Śāntipa. The statement in the Treasury of Purity that the armor is recited immediately after the wisdom maṇḍala has been made to enter, and the empowerment is then conferred, does not accord with the view of the two adepts.

Immediately after that, in front, from a blue syllable jaṃ arises a bow-shaped wind maṇḍala, marked by banners fluttering at its two tips. Above it, from a red raṃ, arises a triangular fire maṇḍala, marked with flame strokes at its three corners. Above that, resting on a hearth of three heads, is a lotus vessel arisen from a red a, that is, a skull cup. In its center, from the transformation of bhūṃ āṃ khaṃ hūṃ, arise the five lamps, whose own nature is go ku da han, and the five nectars, bi ra śu ma mu, each blessed by its own seed. Above them one visualizes a white oṃ; above that, a moon disc arisen from the syllable aṃ that pervades its form; and above that again, one should behold a vajra arisen from a white hūṃ, marked with its seed.

Then the fire, fanned by the wind, melts the whole circle of the vajra and the vajra as well. The nectar that comes from the melting of the four groups of five is produced within that very vessel, and one should then contemplate it as filling the vessel to the brim. Above it one visualizes the three syllables oṃ āḥ hūṃ. Their light rays, in the form of firebrands, invite the nectar that abides in the three worlds and the wisdom nectar of all the Tathāgatas; these are made to enter into it and become of one equal taste. One contemplates it as stainless like the light of the autumn moon, possessing divine fragrance and taste. On the tip of one's own tongue one visualizes a vajra the size of a barley grain, arisen from a white hūṃ; through its firebrand-like light rays the Blessed One tastes that nectar with his mouth and gives it into the mouth of the Blessed Mother. By reciting oṃ sarba tathāgata svada vajra svabhāva ātmako'haṃ, the Blessed One is pleased.

Then Rūpavajrā and the others, emerging from the seed at the heart, should make uninterrupted offerings with hands holding a mirror, a vīṇā, a conch of perfume, a vessel of flavors, garments of many kinds, and a source of phenomena, while one recites oṃ sarba tathāgata pūjrā vajra svabhāva ātmako'haṃ. And Locanā and the others should offer praise with the vajra song:

You are encircled by the host of yoginīs;
you have attained the supreme of your own mind;
you adorn all beings, the moving and the unmoving;
with a compassionate mind you awaken beings;
mind that emanates illusion and accomplishes the vajra:
Heruka, glorious Vajra, delights in mind.

Then one recites oṃ sarba tathāgata stuti vajra svabhāva ātmako'haṃ.

Afterward one forms the seal whose nature is the four wheels. Below the navel is the wheel of emanation, red or multicolored, a lotus of sixty-four petals radiating light; at its center a black syllable aṃ is blessed, facing upward. At the heart is the wheel of Dharma, a white lotus of eight petals. In its center is the seed, a blue syllable hūṃ; on the petals of the east and the other directions are, in due order, the seeds of Vairocana and the others, bhrūṃ āṃ jṝṃ khaṃ. On the petals of the northeast and the other intermediate directions are the seeds of Locanā and the others, e vaṃ mā ya. These face downward. At the throat is the wheel of perfect enjoyment, a red lotus of sixteen petals; on its anthers is a red syllable aṃ, surrounded by the vowels and consonants. These face upward. At the crown is the wheel of great bliss, a lotus of thirty-two petals; in its center it is blessed by a white syllable haṃ, facing downward. The letters of all these rest on moons.

Then the mass of light rays from those four wheels radiates into space and accomplishes the welfare of beings, and it is then meditated on as drawn back within. After that, in order to make her possess the portions of the five families, at the crown, the heart, the navel, the secret place, and the two thighs of the Blessed Mother, in their order, one visualizes Vairocana, Akṣobhya, Ratnasambhava, Amitābha, and Amoghasiddhi, arisen from the transformation of oṃ hūṃ svā āḥ hā. From the transformation of hūṃ one visualizes a lotus and a vajra, and also the anthers and the jewel, completed by the syllables āḥ and oṃ. Then, with oṃ padma sukha dhāra mahā rāga sukhanda catur ānanda bhaga biśva hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ karyaṃ kuruṣva me and oṃ vajra mahā dveṣa catur ānanda dāyaka khaga mukha eka rāso nātha hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ karyaṃ kuruṣva me, the lotus and the vajra are blessed. In order to attain the wisdom of great bliss without emission, one visualizes the yellow letters hūṃ phaṭ in the channels of the lotus and the vajra. Then, uttering the three syllables sī, one should unite in passion with this: oṃ sarbba tathāgata anurāgaṇa vajra svabhāva ātmako'haṃ.

For the commitment as well, one should bring to mind everything: the union with the action seal, the accomplishment of the one possessing the portions of the five families, and so forth. Further, it is the yogin who is proud of impure bliss who should accomplish this.

Then, at the time of the empowerment of wisdom knowledge, through the distinctions of characteristics such as "the various," understood in particular from the instruction of the holy guru, the joys and the rest arise in succession in the union with the action seal as well, by the force of habituation in just that way. At the end comes the fourth, free of the net of all conceptual thought: the innate bliss, which at once pervades all the gates, an aspect unmarked by stain. To abide fully in luminosity: this is the sequence.

Or else, its sequence is this. The three worlds dissolve into the charnel ground; the charnel ground into the lotus seat; the lotus into the corpse; the corpse into the sun; the sun into the Blessed Mother; the Blessed Mother, melting through passion, into the Blessed One; the Blessed One into the sun at his heart; the sun into the vajra; the vajra into the sun that rests at its hub; the sun into the ū of the syllable hūṃ, the essence of Amoghasiddhi; the ū into the syllable ha, the nature of Amitābha; the ha into the head, known as Ratnasambhava; the head into the crescent, the nature of Vairocana; the moon into the drop, the nature of Vajrasattva; and what remains, the drop, is to be made to abide in the nāda, which embodies Akṣobhya. Some, however, say that the drop is of the nature of Akṣobhya and the nāda of the nature of Vajrasattva.

Those of superior intellect alone should then imagine even the nāda as no more than a hundred-thousandth part of the tip of a hair, and let it go. Then, in that very state, the supreme emptiness of entities, non-entities and the rest, clear like a cloudless sky, at the level of luminosity, since the power of one's former aspirations remains, one should, by the force of the practice, behold it exactly thus. In the same way, engaging again and again in the act of arising, the yogin should then enter again and again into the true limit, just as the breath resting on a mirror vanishes entirely. This stage makes directly manifest the inseparability of the two truths, the yoga whose nature is union and nonduality, whose nature is the result, Vajradhara. This is the method of fully entering luminosity through meditation on reality with a wisdom consort.

With regard to meditation without a wisdom consort, it is the khaṭvāṅga itself, purified wisdom; for it is said, "Wisdom has the form of the khaṭvāṅga." Therefore the dawning of the innate through the yoga of caṇḍālī and the like is the full entry into luminosity.

Here, first, the caṇḍālī yoga of this practice is as follows. With the deity's form clear, at the navel wheel there is the syllable aṃ itself; or else, as a cause for steadying the mind, one gathers in both flows of the vital wind and makes them enter the avadhūtī. Then the Blessed Lady Caṇḍālī, blazing, the fiercely blazing fire kindled by the wind below the navel, straight and in the form of an exceedingly fine line, she who bears the name of the supreme Nairātmyā, burns the five tathāgatas who reside at the heart wheel and the four goddesses Locanā and the rest, and rises until she touches the one, not a destroyer of the mind, who resides at the crown wheel. As she blazes thus, and the Blessed One, the hare-marked moon, comes dripping down, at that time too one makes caṇḍālī blaze swiftly. As she blazes intensely, the hare-marked one drips very swiftly and becomes one with caṇḍālī, who is being held in focus at the navel maṇḍala, and the mind becomes firmly fixed.

The awakening mind, dripping profusely, is gathered in the emanation wheel and the others, and by the sequence of supreme joy, without interruption from the crown down to the tip of the jewel of the lotus and vajra, the inseparability of emptiness and compassion gradually increases and reaches its full measure. Thoroughly purified by the light of caṇḍālī's fire, it pervades the thirty-two channels of the wheel of great bliss, and from there pervades the whole body, as far as the other seventy-two thousand channels. The inner sun and moon, wisdom and skillful means, travel in equal measure, and by the power of the great bliss of the joy of cessation, all of this, the aggregates, the elements and the rest, together with the māras, becomes manifest and is wholly transformed. This is abiding fully in luminosity through the yoga of caṇḍālī.

This itself is the Dharma seal. This itself is the union of the springtime drop. Thus, the spring is the Blessed One, that is, the moon; its drop is the Blessed Lady Caṇḍālī; and "their union" means union with this very one. The yogin who constantly cultivates caṇḍālī, guarding it steadily, should make clear the innate caṇḍālī, which has become the cause of supreme great bliss. Likewise, the yogin who abides in union and nonduality in the vajra-like meditative absorption realizes in particular the accomplishment of the great seal.

Some proclaim that "yoginī" means the realization of the great secret. Its path is to be made clear, with the wisdom seal or the Dharma seal, through the sequence of the ten grounds; afterwards one is shown to be Vajradhara.

In some sources it is said that at the sacred sites and elsewhere, by enjoying and uniting for a month or so with an action seal born of the vajra family or another, one who has the capacity to attain wisdom, great bliss and the nature of compassion expand without limit and do not cease. Having made directly manifest the meditative absorption of emptiness, the innate great bliss endowed with seven limbs, one comes to enter the supreme state; and within a single month it bestows the body of the great Vajradhara, who between sessions truly works for the good of beings with a host of emanated women who delight with tresses of manifold qualities and with ornaments.

Or else, having practiced the observance of the knowledge consort for six or seven months, and having made oneself into the form of the deity, when the consciousness of the eminent yogin becomes able, with blazing light, to face continuously toward the yoga of reality, as though day and night were joined together, then, endowed with the yoga of Hevajra, in order to practice the innate together with the seal, one should undertake the effort of union at dawn, in the intervals between sessions, and at other times.

The stage of yoga establishes all three realms of existence pre-eminently in a single essence, of one nature, wholly the same and not wholly the same; sealed with the vajra seal, by the power of the great bliss of equal taste, it abides constantly, day and night, like light and the spreading of light. Then, at the time of the breaking of dawn, which makes directly manifest the reality of the fourth joy, realized at the culmination of supreme joy, the practitioner will attain the supreme accomplishment.

In the Later Tantra too it is said:
Joining throughout what is to be accomplished and the means of accomplishment,
That which becomes clear from service;
Joining vajra and lotus as one
Is to be called the near accomplishment.
Fully endowed with the syllables hūṃ phaṭ
Is declared the accomplishment of movement.
One's own bliss, peaceful by nature,
Is to be called the great accomplishment.

Thus, through these four limbs of skillful means is the method for the state of the glorious great Vajradhara and the great Vajradhāriṇī, the Dharma of all the buddhas and the Dharma to be practiced; and by this passage it is held to be the stage that makes this very thing directly manifest.

If that same practitioner enters into union with an action seal, then, because it becomes one-pointed, as before, through the power of the innate, of the hare-marked moon melted by that fiercely blazing fire of caṇḍālī, this yoga is to be called the yoga of action caṇḍālī, and it is the practice with a wisdom consort.

That which is without a wisdom consort is spoken of in every case as meditation in the form of the deity, or as inner meditation: the dawning of the innate caused by caṇḍālī blazing constantly. At the time of empowerment, too, the blazing that comes from union with an outer knowledge consort is its caṇḍālī; and that which dawns, as before, from the dawning of the innate is held to be the yoga of action caṇḍālī. Yet it is said, "It is the innate; it does not follow her beautiful face." Therefore this yoga of action caṇḍālī is not a matter of the face at all.

Some, however, say this: for one who, though again conquering and growing accustomed to the winds as before, has not gained mastery of breath control, it is done at the navel maṇḍala, the boundless path of the wind; or else, from retention, the great bliss that arises from that caṇḍālī blazing intensely is what is called the yoga of action caṇḍālī.

If one wishes to make the mind abide through the yoga of the drop and to have the light of all accomplishments spread, then one views the four syllables, aṃ and the rest, in the holes at the knots of the wheels at the navel and elsewhere. The wind residing at the secret wheel fully stirs the fire, and the rays of the blazing syllable aṃ, which is of the nature of caṇḍālī, completely melt the three syllables, hūṃ and the rest. One meditates on them as inseparable together with the syllable oṃ, in the form of a drop whose nature is innate joy, and when the meditation is complete, one should emanate. Then one should see that very drop as subtle lines of light blazing, abiding as though blazing as one. By its light it becomes a drop filled with melted nectar and radiating, and that in turn comes to have lines of white light. Then one should gaze at that drop for as long as the mind can reach it, and again for as long as it takes to perceive the sign of having gained stability. Its perception and its stability are these: one sees an appearance of various lamps radiating from the lines of light, and one sees everything like an āmalaka fruit in the palm of the hand. This, called "the spreading of the light of all meditative absorptions," is the yoga of the drop.

Or else, the three worlds, to be summoned by the tathāgatas emanated from the mass of light rays of the mantra at the heart, are to be placed within that syllable hūṃ in the form of a drop, for as long as the mind wishes. After that, by the stages of the path of emanation, one goes as far as the vajras of body, speech and mind. The appearance of its rays is entirely gathered in the manner of an inhalation and swiftly placed into the drop of the seed syllable; and this is the maṇḍala of that Blessed One as described. For meditation on the wheel, one should place the wheel of the maṇḍala there. Again, by way of emanation, rays radiate and illuminate the world, which is merged into that very drop; and the tathāgatas, who are working for the good of beings, are likewise drawn into the heart in the manner of an inhalation, and one should meditate for as long as one wishes. This is the supreme yoga of the drop.

Or else, the rays of the samādhi being go into the avadhūtī and melt the awakening mind residing at the wheel of great bliss; and one should imagine that, coming from there into the drop of the syllable hūṃ at the heart, it abides there in the form of a most subtle particle of its own nature. This is the yoga of the drop according to others.

After that, meditation on the subtle yoga is the method for making the mind abide supremely. In just the same way, the syllables aṃ and the rest melt and become of one taste, and by their innate nature, within a drop of awakening mind no larger than a mustard seed, there is the body of the Blessed One, clear and complete. In relation to meditation on the wheel of the maṇḍala, the wheel of the maṇḍala is at the tip of the vajra jewel. One should meditate on it trickling from there and resting in the lotus, or else resting at the tip of the nose. Having seen the sign of stable abiding as described, one should meditate on the maṇḍala of that Blessed One, or emanate the maṇḍala. This is the subtle yoga.

Having meditated on that very thing in the form of the deity, one enters luminosity, arises and meditates, as far as one is able, by the yogas just described without interruption, or by the yoga of others. When one grows weary, one should recite the mantra syllables by this rite of mantra recitation or by another. Here, the rays emerging from the mouth of the wisdom consort enter one's own mouth, emerge by the vajra path and enter the goddess's lotus; again they pass from the goddess's mouth fully into one's own mouth, and one should keep the mantra flowing without interruption. This is described in the Sādhana of Saroruha as palanquin recitation.

Elsewhere it is said: with the pride of the sameness of all phenomena, the garland of mantra enters one's own mouth, goes by the vajra path into the lotus, comes up by the path of the avadhūtī, is emitted from the mouth of the Blessed Lady and fully enters the door of one's own mouth; meditating on it circling in just this way, one recites. This is called the palanquin recitation of gentle and peaceful syllables. This same practice, differing only in that the mantra enters the Blessed Lady's mouth and comes into one's own mouth, with hard and rough syllables, is the wrathful recitation; it is wrathful recitation because the heart mantra and the rest are uttered with the utmost fierceness.

Or else, one views the garland of mantra syllables standing in a circle around the syllable hūṃ at the heart, like a garland of lamps, visualizes it circling swiftly, and, abandoning improper thoughts, one should recite: oṃ trailokya ākṣepa hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā This is the root mantra of the Blessed One. The Two-Part Tantra and its commentary state that its three syllables hūṃ are short.

Then, having offered the torma that will be explained below and having also made aspirations, one should remain at ease. When the interval between sessions comes, one rises from the form of the Blessed One and performs the drawing in of the wisdom being and the rest. At the midnight interval, too, having done all of this, one should sleep with a strong conviction that sleep is of the nature of luminosity. At the dawn interval, awakened by the four goddesses Locanā and the rest with vajra songs, one should perform all the activities of rising and so on.

Next, the mantra for shaking a city: oṃ a ka ca ṭa ta pa ya śa svāhā Here the instruction for it is as follows. Quickly visualize yourself in the form of Hevajra with his wisdom consort, and imagine at his heart a red lotus with eight petals. On its petals of the east and the other directions, going clockwise in due order, are the syllables a ka ca ṭa, and on the intermediate petals, beginning with that of Īśāna in the northeast, ta pa ya śa. On the central anther are svā to the east, oṃ in the center and hā to the west. All of them rest on sun disks and are red in color. From these, rays that are hooks in purified form issue from the pores of the body. Thinking, "I summon the target, place them beneath the practitioner's feet, and bring these very ones under control," one should recite the mantra. By reciting it a hundred thousand times, the city will be shaken.

For the accomplishment of bringing under control, in the order described, one first performs a hundred thousand recitations for the approach. Then one holds a feast gathering in a charnel ground; and since the assembled beings, too, wish to kill the practitioner who longs for the result, one should therefore take hold of a pillar. Then, going to some place, one lies down with the head toward the east on a high pillow, places a skull cup filled with māmakī so that it touches the soles of the feet, and should recite one round. Later, on rising, one drinks all of it, goes into every place of the feast gathering, and should again recite ten thousand times. The yogin who has no doubt about Hevajra should purify everything.

Then, following the system of the Kālacakra, one should perform the recitation of the vowels and consonants. Since the vowels and consonants are implied by the sounds a ka ca ṭa ta pa ya śa, one should recite them: oṃ a ā i ī ṛ ṝ u ū ḷ ḹ a ā e ai ar ār o au al āl ha hā ya yā ra rā va vā la lā Thus one recites the vowels with the inhalation, in the sequence of emanation. ka kā kha khā ga gā gha ghā ṅa ṅā ca cā cha chā ja jā jha jhā ña ñā ṭa ṭā ṭha ṭhā ḍa ḍā ḍha ḍhā ṇa ṇā ta tā tha thā da dā dha dhā na nā pa pā pha phā ba bā bha bhā ma mā da dā dha dhā na nā sa sā ñpa ñpā ṣa ṣā ṭhka ṭhkā svāhā Thus one recites the consonants as the wind goes out. Having recited the vowels and consonants a hundred thousand times, the mantra practitioner who brings forth the three worlds should do it to the measure of one round and then again one; it grants summoning, protection and increase. This sequence, too, is found in the treatise of the glorious Vajragarbha.

Next, the instruction for bringing under control. Quickly, upon a beautiful heap of red lotuses, view yourself in the form of Heruka with his wisdom consort. At the heart, on the anther of a red lotus with eight petals, write vividly in the mind the syllables aṃ and hūṃ, whose essence perfectly gathers the vowels and consonants and which are wholly devoted to the qualities of the buddhas. From them masses of intensely red and lovely rays radiate forth and illuminate beings; with these one draws in the body, speech and mind of others and should place them into the drops of those very syllables aṃ and hūṃ. Then these transform, and there is a bee, Manmatha, with the likeness of a ruby, its mind intoxicated from drinking the honey of supreme compassion. By the sound of a very long hūṃ that it utters, one should imagine them in the form of Nairātmyā and Heruka, and at the heart of each, in a drop the size of a mustard seed, like something dyed with lac, one clearly generates fine syllables aṃ and hūṃ.

Then that Manmatha, riding the chariot of the out-going breath, emerges from the nostril, purifies and seizes the body, speech and mind of beings, and again riding the in-going breath returns by that same path; one should place it into the anther of the lotus at the heart. Then, by meditative absorption, one should dissolve the bodies, speech and minds thus summoned into one's own body, speech and mind. One should meditate in this way again and again, generating it until the image becomes clear.

Afterwards one should meditate on a red eight-petaled lotus arisen at the heart of the man or woman who is the target, and that bee Manmatha is drawn in by that very means; entering through the nostril, it should be placed in the lotus at the heart, which is of the nature of that person's body, speech and mind. Then that Manmatha makes itself reddish-yellow with the pollen of the flowers, which is the nature of the body, drinks the honey that is the nature of speech, and eats the flowers of the mind. After that, Manmatha is drawn by the practitioner's breath, brought out through the target's nostril, and placed through the practitioner's nostril into the lotus at the heart; and one imagines that with its wings it shakes off the pollen of the flowers of the target's body and disgorges the honey of speech and the flowers of mind. In this way one should meditate intensely, again and again, on these forms of attachment, and at the end of the meditation one should recite the mantra with the target's name inserted.

These too are portions of the knowledge mantra that intoxicate with delight those to whom they are applied. Since one is bringing a person under one's own control, one should bathe beforehand and then meditate. If one comes to see fault in a person's desire, it will then be a fault of not having looked; therefore one should from the very outset examine whether the object is a worthy or an unworthy one, and then meditate. This is the stage of the bee Manmatha.

The rites of fire offering and the like for the activities of pacifying and the rest are well known from following the tantras and other sources, and so they have not been written here.

The sādhana of the two-armed Hevajra who delights in the charnel ground: again, having done everything as before, one imagines at the heart, upon a moon, a vajra arising from a blue syllable hrīḥ, and within its hub a syllable hrīḥ resting on a moon that has arisen there. From the complete transformation of all of this, one should meditate on the Blessed One alone, with the form described earlier, standing in a dance posture upon a half-moon that rests on a corpse and wearing a garment of human skin; the rest is as before. The mantra to be recited is: śrī heruka vajra samaya hrīḥ sarva duṣṭān mudrā prabhañjaka hūṃ phaṭ This too was taught by the Blessed One in the Saṃpuṭatilaka Tantra. Thus, with regard to the meditator, the sādhana of the Blessed One alone, the alternative to the concise and clear practice with the knowledge consort, has been explained.

The elaborate and clear form of this practice is as follows. Within the vajra tent one should meditate on the source of phenomena: triangular through the purity of body, speech and mind; white like the autumn moon because all obscurations have been abandoned; and narrow below and wide above, because of its vajra nature, in which the three bodies are of one ground, and because it is the great birthplace of phenomena. Within it, at the center of the eight charnel grounds, one imagines a blue syllable hrīḥ resting on a moon within the hub of a vajra born from a blue syllable hrīḥ that rests on a moon and sun joined together. This is the elaborate form.

Within the beautiful source of phenomena, on a multicolored lotus, at the center of a crossed vajra, are the four great elements, of the nature of wind, fire, water and earth. They arise from the syllables yaṃ raṃ vaṃ laṃ and are blue, red, white and yellow in color: one bow-shaped, its two tips marked with banners; one triangular, marked with the letter ra; one round, marked with a vase; and one square, its four corners marked with three-pointed vajras. From their complete transformation there is Vairocana with his wisdom consort in purified form, the essence in which the buddha fields are perfectly gathered: a many-storied mansion made of various jewels, the square celestial palace whose very nature is the nonduality of the tathāgatas and ordinary beings.

Its four doors, in the east and the other directions, are the applications of mindfulness, the right abandonments, the legs of miraculous power and the faculties, in purified form. The doors themselves are the very means to awakening. Through the purity of the eightfold path of the noble ones, there are pillars at the eight sides of the doors, pillars close at hand through the qualities of all the buddhas. Through the purity of the four discriminating knowledges, there are four beams upon the pillars, because they have already taken up the burden of the welfare of others. Through the purity of the four meditative absorptions, the heroic progress, the sky treasury, the stainless seal and the lion's play, there are the four firm and beautiful platforms.

Through the purity of the dhāraṇīs of patience, of the Dharma, of meaning and of mantra, on each side; or else through the purity of the division into moments characterized as the patience of knowing the Dharma, knowledge of the Dharma, the patience of subsequent knowledge and subsequent knowledge, with regard to each of the truths; two goddesses each stand in sequence, going clockwise, on the two sides of each of the main doors. They are Vīṇā, Vaṃśā, Mukundā, the goddess of the round clay drum, Murajā, Mālā, Lāsyā, Gītā, Nṛtyā, Puṣpā, Dhūpā, Dīpā, Gandhā, Ādarśā, Rasā, Sparśā and Dharmā. These sixteen goddesses are yellow, red, white, smoke-colored, yellow, red, pale red, multicolored, white, black, golden, yellow, white, red, green and white in color; each has a form in keeping with her name, and each is born from the first letter of her own name.

Through the purity of being completely filled with the nectar of the vast accumulations of merit and wisdom, moistened with love, there are eight vases filled with nectar. Through the purity of the sound of the holy Dharma, which goes everywhere, conforming to beings' dispositions, in its twelve branches known as the collections of discourses, songs, expositions, verses, inspired utterances, accounts of occasions, narratives, accounts of what happened thus, birth stories, extensive teachings, marvels and established teachings, there are banners stirred by the wind, with the sound of bells. Through the purity of pacifying the torments of beings in saṃsāra, applied as is fitting, or through the abandonment of all conceptuality, there is the parasol. Through the purity of accomplishing perfectly the welfare of others by means of jewels and the like, brilliant in accordance with merit, there are the excellent vase and the wish-fulfilling trees.

Through the purity of the supramundane path of meditation, which deals with the root afflictions, there are full necklaces. Through the purity of the mundane path of meditation, which clears away afflictions, there are half necklaces. Through the purity of the awakening mind, the moon. Through the purity of wisdom and primordial wisdom, the sun. Through the purity of the wisdom of the path of the tathāgatas, the yak-tail whisks. Through the purity of the perfect flowers of the factors of awakening, the garlands of flowers. Through the purity of the mirror-like wisdom, the mirror. All of this is taught in the Great Secret Tantra as follows:

That which utterly destroys all afflictions
Is truly explained as the full necklace.
The necklace steals away the afflictions,
Cutting them off entirely and crushing them.
Crushing them utterly and clearing them away,
It is the joining of the two accumulations.
That which steals away half the afflictions
Is called the half necklace.
Luminous by nature, fully pure,
Is the unsurpassed awakening mind;
That which works for the good of all beings
Is called the disk of the moon.
Wisdom and primordial wisdom, pure by nature,
Give birth to the vajra-like meditative absorption;
The great splendor of the world of liberation
Is to be called the disk of the sun.
The meditative absorption utterly freed from the two kinds of conceptual thought
Is the realization of primordial wisdom.
The distinctive wisdom of the path
Is truly explained as the yak-tail whisk.
Likewise the seven factors of awakening
Are the garlands with hanging tassels of flowers.

Through the purity of the unguarded teaching of the Dharma, as befits those of subtle, coarse and middling intellect and those without certainty, there are the unguarded red garlands of bakula flowers. Through the purity of teaching the supramundane, supremely white Dharma, the kramaśīrṣa. Through the purity of the four meditative concentrations, the four porticoes. Through the purity of cutting off what is opposed to the teaching of the Dharma, there is the wheel of Dharma. Its spokes, according to one view, are eight: suffering, origin, cessation and path, the conventional and the ultimate, the mundane and the supramundane, in purified form. According to another system they are twelve: suffering is to be understood; it has been understood; there is nothing further to understand. The origin is to be abandoned; it has been abandoned; there is nothing further to abandon. Cessation is to be actualized; it has been actualized; there is nothing further to actualize. The path is to be cultivated; it has been cultivated;

there is nothing further to cultivate. Thus the turning of the wheel of Dharma in twelve aspects, in purified form, is also the twelve links of dependent origination, in purified form. Through the kindness of having relied on the Dharma taught by the Blessed One, the fully purified and valid teaching, the evil non-Buddhists, like deer and does, follow the Dharma and faith and are joined to the path; and through the purity of this, and of their being intent on what is called the wheel of Dharma, there are the deer and doe with outstretched necks. Through the purity of a sense of shame there are the canopy, the cloths, the garlands and the rest. Through the purity of fully turning the wheel of Dharma in the vajra manner of all the perfectly complete buddhas, free of any particular bondage whatsoever, there is the vajra garland. Through the purity of the vision of perfect wisdom, the blazing rays of light. Through the purity of Vairocana and the others, the colors of the ground and the rest. The other details have not been written down, since they are already well known.

Having meditated on the many-storied mansion in this way, one should meditate on oneself as the Blessed One at its center, by the sequence of the multicolored lotus and the rest, as before. The three syllables oṃ āḥ hūṃ, the nature of body, speech and mind, enclosed within two red syllables ho, known as the being of the intermediate state, accompanied by delight, enter through the mouth of the Blessed One, pass by the vajra path into the goddess's lotus and melt in her womb. And the Blessed One too, at the end, through intense great passion, melts together with his knowledge consort, and abides as the nature of great bliss in the form of the awakening mind, like quicksilver.

Then, immediately, the yoginīs Pukkasī and the rest, whose nature is great love and the other immeasurables, thinking with deep longing, "We are without a protector," fully exhort him with vajra songs:

Rise, noble lord, with your mind of compassion!
I am Pukkasī: protect me!
Leave aside the very nature of emptiness
And resolve to join with me in great bliss.
Without you I shall die;
O Hevajra, rise!
Leave aside the very nature of emptiness
And bring Śavarī's fruit to fulfillment.
Lord of joy, host of the world,
Have you entered into emptiness?
Knowing no direction without you,
We Caṇḍālīs make our plea.
I know your heart.
I, Ḍombī, my mind's recollection failing:
Do not cut off the stream of compassion;
You who possess wondrous human form, rise!

Thus fully exhorted by those who sing vajra songs, and impelled by his former aspirations, he awakens at the mere sound of the vajra song, or rises like one waking from a dream.

Then, on the petals of the east and the other directions, in due order, are Brahmā, Indra, Upendra and Rudra; and on the intermediate petals, beginning with Īśāna, are Yama, Kubera, the rākṣasa and Vemacitrin. At their hearts, on moons, in the middle of a moon and sun joined together, according to the rite, are the syllables gaṃ caṃ vaṃ ghaṃ paṃ śaṃ laṃ ḍaṃ, born from eight drops of the awakening mind emanated from the Blessed One. From these come a curved knife, a ḍamaru drum, a tortoise, a snake, a lion, a monk, a wheel and a vajra; and on these one views the syllables, not other than before, abiding constantly on moons. By the sequence of manifest awakening from the fivefold source, all of this completely transforms, and from it arise the eight yoginīs, Gaurī and the rest, with the colors that have been taught. They are of the nature of the aggregates of form, feeling, perception and formations, and of the elements of earth, water, fire and wind. Each has one face and three round red eyes, and two arms. In their right hands they hold the curved knife, the ḍamaru drum, the tortoise, the snake, the lion, the monk, the wheel and the vajra: these are the magical displays of cutting off conceptual thoughts of entities and the like, of the scriptures, of jealousy, of anger, of pride, of the awakening mind, of cutting off ignorance, and of wisdom wholly virtuous. In their left hands they hold a rohita fish, a pig, a skull cup, a yogin's begging bowl, an axe, a ringed staff, a plowshare, and they make the threatening gesture: these are the magical displays of avarice, of delusion, of emptiness, of cutting off the host of attachments and the like, of restoring the mindfulness of these, of uprooting obstructors, and of the purified essential nature of the single Dharma.

One should meditate on them standing on moons at the hearts of Brahmā and the others in a dancing pose with legs half-crossed, wearing the five seals, with garlands of freshly severed heads, with fierce faces, their reddish-yellow hair standing on end, and clad in the directions.

At the hearts of all of them, on moons, one imagines their own emblems, and in these their own seed syllables resting on moons. Working first for the benefit of beings by emanating and gathering back according to the occasion, then, with the rays of the seed syllable at the heart, the circle of wisdom beings is summoned and emanated. Gaurī summons them with oṃ gaurī ākarṣaya jaḥ Caurī makes them enter with oṃ corī praveśaya hūṃ Vetālī binds them with oṃ vetālī bandhaya vaṃ and with oṃ ghasmarī toṣaya hoḥ Ghasmarī brings them under control.

Then, at the end of the empowerment, Gaurī and the others residing on the petals of the directions are sealed by Akṣobhya, Vairocana, Ratnasambhava and Amitābha. For Pukkasī and the others as well, the purification into the seals of Akṣobhya and the rest, in sequence following those of Gaurī and the others, is as in the Two-Part Tantra. After that, the armor and all the rest are just as before.

As for the tasting of nectar: according to the rite, one's own wisdom consort offers it to the Blessed One himself, and the retinue, Gaurī and the others, should taste it by joining the tongue with the vajra. Again, here the many-storied mansion is as given in the Tantra. The empowerment is correctly explained as the Tathāgata Akṣobhya, the tasting of nectar as that of Amitābha, the praise as Ratnasambhava and the offering as Amoghasiddhi; and the passion is always to be regarded as Vajrasattva. This is to be called the six limbs of accomplishment.

These too should be viewed in the three maṇḍalas that will be explained.

As for entering into luminosity: the three worlds at once, the charnel ground into the charnel ground, the many-storied mansion into the many-storied mansion, and Gaurī and the others into Gaurī and the others, that is, into the pores of the Blessed One of their respective directions. The rest is as before.

Then, as for their becoming the mantras of Nairātmyā and the others: oṃ aṃ svāhā is the heart mantra of Nairātmyā. oṃ līṃ svāhā is that of Gaurī. oṃ eṃ svāhā is that of Caurī. oṃ aiṃ svāhā is that of Vetālī. oṃ oṃ svāhā is that of Ghasmarī, and is the mantra for all activities. oṃ ūṃ svāhā is that of Pukkasī. oṃ raṃ svāhā is that of Śabarī. oṃ laṃ svāhā is that of Ḍombī.

Here, whenever one wishes to perform the recitation of commitment, then, while reciting the heart mantras and near-heart mantras that will be explained, one emanates the assembly of the maṇḍala's deities from the seed at the heart. Having accomplished the benefit of beings, they are drawn back, together with the mantra, in the manner of a garland, and when the breath enters, at that moment they should be placed into that very seed at the heart. When the recitation of each deity's mantra in turn is complete, one should emanate and gather them one by one. This is the recitation of commitment.

Now it is said in the Two-Part Tantra: "Through the yoga of Vajrā, Gaurī, Vāriyoginī, Vajraḍākinī, Nairātmyā, the Sky-Goer and the Earth-Goer, the one who keeps the observance performs paralysis and the other rites." Therefore here too, from the instructions received while keeping company with the guru in mutual inseparability, I shall write out the elaboration of the rites.

Visualize Vajrā, yellow, born from the seed aṃ, holding a skull cup and a khaṭvāṅga in her left hands and a vajra and a curved knife in her right hands, with a yellow seed hūṃ at her heart. First perform the approach, a hundred thousand recitations of oṃ hūṃ svāhā, and at its end perform the fire offering according to the rite, with the mantra together with the insertions that will be explained. For this one too, at the conclusion of the rite: on two unfired clay bowls, using yellow orpiment, draw a crossed vajra resting in a round water maṇḍala marked with a vase, and the spell oṃ hūṃ vajre so-and-so staṃbhaya hūṃ svāhā. Fill one bowl with charnel-ground ash and close it mouth to mouth with the other bowl. On top, draw a pair of Mahendra maṇḍalas marked with vajras at the directions, and place it beneath the ground. Remaining in the meditative absorption of pressing it down, with the left foot for a woman and the right foot for a man, at the end one should recite the mantra. With a thousand recitations one paralyzes the target.

Visualize Gaurī, perfected from iṃ, saffron in color, holding a noose, a khaṭvāṅga, a hook and a vajra, with a red seed syllable aṃ placed at her heart. First perform the approach, a hundred thousand recitations of oṃ aṃ svāhā, and perform the fire offering according to the rite with the mantra together with the insertions. At the conclusion of this rite too, imagine the target, red, with loosened hair, pressed down, her neck bound with the noose, drawn in by the hook piercing her womb, and fallen beneath one's feet; and imagine fire blazing and flaring from that red aṃ in her womb. For a man it is exactly the same, but at the heart. By reciting oṃ aṃ gaurī amukī me baśamanāya svāhā, she will certainly come under one's power.

Viewing Vāriyoginī, born from the seed iṃ, blue, holding a skull cup, a hammer, a vajra and the threatening gesture, with a blue khaṃ resting on a sun at her heart, perform a hundred thousand recitations of oṃ khaṃ svāhā, and perform the fire offering as prescribed with the mantra together with the insertions. At the conclusion of this rite too, imagine the target with loosened hair, pressed down, set on wind, riding a blue camel, facing south, while one wrathfully strikes the target with the hammer. Reciting oṃ khaṃ pāriyogenī amukaṃ ucchaṭaya khaṃ hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā, within seven nights one drives the target away.

Visualize Vajraḍākinī, born from the syllable u, like Vāriyoginī, with a blue jṝṃ resting on a sun at her heart. Recite oṃ jrāṃ svāhā a hundred thousand times, and perform a fire offering suited to the rite with the mantra together with the insertions. The concluding act of this one too: imagine the targets riding a horse and a buffalo, quarreling with one another. By reciting oṃ jṝṃ vajraḍākinī so-and-so and so-and-so bidveṣaṃ kuru svāhā jṝṃ hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā, one sets them at enmity.

Visualize Nairātmyā, born from the seed aṃ, blue, of fierce form, holding a skull cup, a khaṭvāṅga, a vajra and a curved knife, with a blue buṃ on a sun at her heart, and recite oṃ buṃ svāhā a hundred thousand times; then perform the fire offering according to the rite with the mantra together with the insertions. At the conclusion of this rite too: on a brahmin's skull, with poison, mustard seed and blood from the index finger, draw a six-spoked wheel whose spokes bear hūṃ, and in its center oṃ buṃ nairaātmya amuka jāraya buṃ hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā, or else that mantra with all the harms inserted into it. Having drawn it and recited, heat it over embers of chaff; one strikes the target with fever.

Visualize the Sky-Goer, born from aṃ, like Gaurī, with the seed of a red hūṃ at her heart, and recite oṃ hūṃ svāhā a hundred thousand times; then perform the fire offering according to the rite with the mantra together with the insertions. For the rite of this one too, imagine the targets blazing above maṇḍalas of wind and fire. A hook of light rays from the seed at one's own heart goes out through one's left nostril and enters the targets through the channel of their right nostril; it pierces their heart and womb, comes out through their left nostril, and is to be placed, through one's own right nostril, into the seed at one's heart. One should draw them in with the wind of the breath. By reciting oṃ hūṃ khecari amukīnā ākarṣaya hūṃ svāhā, one summons them.

Visualize the Earth-Goer, born from the syllable aṃ, like Nairātmyā, with a blue syllable ghuḥ at her heart. Perform the approach, a hundred thousand recitations of aṃ ghuḥ svāhā, and perform the fire offering as before with the mantra together with the insertions. For the rite of this one too, imagine a blazing vajra born from the syllable ghuḥ driven into the target from the lower door up to the waist. By reciting oṃ ghuḥ bhucari amukaṃ māraya ghuḥ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā, one kills.

Here, with respect to the order of the mantras taught in the tantra, the Earth-Goer and the Sky-Goer are out of sequence. These seven, Vajrā and the others, likewise stand in a dancing posture on a sun and a corpse; they are four-armed, adorned with the five seals, with red hair bristling upward. So it is said.

Then, as for their becoming the heart mantras of Vajrā and the others: oṃ āṃ svāhā, oṃ iṃ svāhā, oṃ īṃ svāhā, oṃ ūṃ svāhā, oṃ aṃ svāhā, oṃ āṃ svāhā, oṃ au svāhā.

Because what is condensed becomes clear by reference to what is extensive, this means of accomplishment together with its maṇḍala has been set out for the two-armed form alone. The same should be understood for the four-armed form and the others. Their distinctive features are as follows.

Jvālājvala, being the purity of having decisively conquered the four māras, is four-armed. With his principal right and left hands he holds a vajra and a skull cup filled with the blood of the māras, and with the remaining two hands he embraces Vajravārāhī, who is like Nairātmyā. The rest is as before. His mantra is oṃ jvala jvala bhyo huṃ huṃ huṃ phaṭ svāhā. Here the three syllables hūṃ are said to be short.

Kiṭi Kiṭi Vajra, being the purity of emptiness, signlessness and wishlessness, or of the three channels, is three-faced: the first face is black, the right white and the left red. Being the purity of the six perfections, he is six-armed. With his principal right hand he holds a vajra; with the left a trident, being the purity of destroying the afflictions of the three realms of existence; with another a curved knife; and with the other a bell, being the purity of the Perfection of Wisdom. With the remaining two hands he fully embraces Vajraśṛṅkhalā, who is like Nairātmyā, and he abides as before. The retinue is just as was taught to accompany them all. His mantra is oṃ kiṭi kiṭi vajra hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā. The three syllables hūṃ are said to be long.

Picu Vajra, again, arises from the syllable hūṃ resting on a sun that rests in a skull cup born from hūṃ. Being the purity of the eight liberations, he is eight-faced. His root face is black, the right white, the left red and extremely terrifying, the upper face smoke-colored and baring its fangs, and the rest the color of bees; each face has three eyes. Being the purity of the sixteen emptinesses, he is sixteen-armed. In the skull cups in the palms of the eight right hands, in order from the top, are an elephant, a horse, a donkey, an ox, a camel, a human and a śarabha, being the purity of destroying the diseases of phlegm, breathing, madness, consumption, that is, wasting, leprosy, swelling of the legs, the liver and the spleen. Likewise, in the skull cups held in the left hands are Earth, Water, Wind, Fire, the Moon, the Sun, Antaka (Yama) and Kubera, the Lord of Wealth, being the purity of bestowing on all beings the accomplishments of mastery, beginning with clarity arising from pacification. With his two principal hands he embraces Nairātmyā. Being the purity of the four means of attraction, he is four-legged. On suns at the hearts of Brahmā, Viṣṇu, Śakra (Indra) and Maheśvara (Śiva), who are the causes that bring about the māras of the aggregates, of the afflictions, of the son of the gods, and of the lord of death, he dances with two of his legs in the half-crossed posture and with the others in the dance with the right leg extended; he has the playful grace of four legs. So say some. The rite of the retinue is as before.

As for entering into luminosity: according to this division, the eight yoginīs, Gaurī and the others, are made to enter the eight faces, the seven faces enter the root face, the fourteen arms together with their signs enter the two root arms, and the two legs enter the two root legs. The gathering of the variegated lotus and the rest is as before, the same as for the others.

His heart mantra is oṃ deva picu vajra hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā. Here too the three syllables hūṃ are said to be long. oṃ vajra karttari hevajrāya hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā is the near-heart mantra, and it is to be understood that this is the near-heart mantra of all of them.

oṃ aṣṭananāya piṅgorhvakeśabartmane caturbiṃśatinetrāla ṣoḍaśabhujāya kṛṣṇajṝmūtabapuṣe kapālamālānekādhāriṇe ādhmātakrūracittāya ardhendudaṃṣṭiṇe māraya māraya kāraya kāraya garjjaya garjjaya tajjaya tajjaya śoṣaya śoṣya saptasāgarān bandha bandha nāgāṣṭakāna gṛhṇa gṛhṇa śatrūṃ hā hā hi hī hu hū he hai ho hau haṃ haḥ phaṭ svāhā This is the garland mantra.

Furthermore, it is said that here, merely through distinctions of the higher mind in the central Picu Vajra, Hevajra is definitively seen as fourfold.

As for the uninterrupted sequence: visualize a vast yellow vajra tent born from bhrūṃ. Within it is a wheel with ten spokes, the spokes being the purity of the ten knowledges: the knowledge of suffering, of the origin, of cessation, of the path, of exhaustion, of non-arising, of the Dharma, subsequent knowledge, conventional knowledge, and the knowledge of full arising. In the middle of the wheel, on a crossed vajra, imagine a variegated lotus and a sun, and on it a moon and a sun joined face to face, in the middle of which is placed a blue syllable hūṃ. Then, by the sequence of manifest awakening that arises from the five, all of that is completely transformed, and from it one visualizes oneself as Vajrahūṃkāra, the color of a blue sapphire, with one face and a jeweled crown, his hands holding a vajra and a bell as he fully embraces his natural consort, pressing down Bhairava and Kālarātri with his left leg extended, and adorned with many-colored garments and ornaments.

Through the union of vajra and skull, one then emanates the ten wrathful ones. On the eastern and the following spokes, in order, are Yamāntaka, Prajñāntaka, Padmāntaka and Vighnāntaka, black, white, red and blue in color, holding a vajra hammer, a white staff, a red lotus and a crossed vajra respectively. They are dwarfish, pot-bellied and wrathful, adorned with the eight great nāgas, their reddish-yellow hair bristling upward, with three round red eyes, wearing the crowns of Vairocana, Ratnasambhava, Amitābha and Akṣobhya respectively, and wearing skirts of tiger skin.

On the spokes of the southeast and the other intermediate directions are Ṭakkirāja, Nīladaṇḍa, Mahābala and Acala, deep black, holding a hook, a staff, a trident and a sword. Here Ṭakkirāja and Acala are adorned with fine garments, jewels and so on, graceful, with jeweled crowns, while Nīladaṇḍa and Mahābala are fierce like Yamāntaka. On the lower spoke is Sumbharāja, black, holding a pestle, with a jeweled crown marked with Akṣobhya. On the upper spoke is Uṣṇīṣa, yellow, holding a wheel, with a jeweled crown marked with Vairocana. The four beginning with Ṭakkirāja wear the crown of Akṣobhya. These ten wrathful ones should be seen standing on a variegated lotus and a sun with the left leg extended, their left hands at the level of the heart holding a nāga noose with the threatening forefinger of the fist.

Having visualized the protection wheel formed in this way, at its center, from the complete transformation of Vajrahūṃkāra, imagine the source of phenomena in the form described earlier; then, by the sequence taught in all the other cases, one should likewise view the four Hevajras with the variegated lotus, the crossed vajra, the many-storied mansion and the rest.

Thus, when through the yoga of having meditated constantly, definitely and without interruption on the single yoga of the generation stage in the wheels of the maṇḍala, it becomes clearer than clear, the signs of the reflection become, for as long as they appear, like an illusion and the rest, free of elaboration and fully clear. So it is said in the Two-Part Tantra:

Through the yoga of the generation stage
the one with vows meditates on elaboration.
Having made elaboration like a dream,
through elaboration itself he makes it free of elaboration.
Just as an illusion, just as a dream,
just as the intermediate existence is,
so too, through the yoga of constant practice,
the maṇḍala itself is held to be.

Therefore it is said: at once the ground of the great Vajradhara, lord of the ten grounds, whose nature is the unity of the two truths, the conqueror of the māras.

It is also said that, through another meditation, to increase the yogin's accumulation of merit and to pacify the obstructors, one should worship the Blessed One with whatever offerings one has obtained. I shall explain the rite of that worship.

The mantrin endowed with the yoga of Hevajra should, at dawn, sweep and so on, as befits the place. Touching the parts of the ground with the hand, he blesses the ground with oṃ rakṣa rakṣa hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ svāhā. He smears it with the five nectars, fine perfumes and the like, and, conceiving it as each of those grounds, he says: "the place and the near place, the field and the near field, the gathering and the near gathering, the meeting place and the near meeting place, the drinking place and the near drinking place, the charnel ground and the near charnel ground." Having made a square maṇḍala, he blesses its perimeter with oṃ vajra rekhe hūṃ. With oṃ surekhe sarba tathāgata adhitiṣṭhantu svāhā he should place a single flower in the center of the maṇḍala.

Then, with the light rays of the seed at the heart, he invites the Blessed One, the Protector who shakes all three worlds, the Hero surrounded by the eight goddesses. First he presents to them the incomparable welcome water and the other offerings. Then, with flowers and the rest, born from hūṃ and consecrated by the three syllables, he first offers a single flower to the Blessed One with oṃ āḥ hūṃ hevajraya vajra puṣpe hūṃ svāhā, and afterwards with the heart mantra and the near-heart mantra. Having made offering with oṃ āḥ nairaātmaye vajra puṣpe aṃ hūṃ svāhā, he should worship with the mantras oṃ aṃ hūṃ svāhā and oṃ a ā i ī u ū ṛ ṝ ḷ ḹ e ai o au aṃ svāhā. On the crowns of both heads: oṃ āḥ akṣobhyaya vajra puṣpe hūṃ hūṃ svāhā. On the petals of the east and the other directions: oṃ āḥ gauryi vajra puṣpe gaṃ hūṃ svāhā and oṃ gaurī hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā; with these he should worship Gaurī. In the same way, with the three syllables interwoven with their respective seed letters, he should go on to worship Caurī and the others. Afterwards, having offered with the vocative form of the name together with the syllable oṃ, he should worship with the letters hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ and the ending svāhā.

In the same way, in place of puṣpe one should insert the respective names, dhūpe and the rest, and make the offerings. Then, with oṃ sarva tathāgata sulalita namitairenamāmi bhagavāntaṃ śrī herukanāthaṃ jaḥ hūṃ vaṃ hoḥ pratīcchamaṃ kusuma añjali nāthāḥ ho, one should offer a handful of flowers and bow. oṃ sarva tathāgata pūja megha prasara samuhe spharahīmaṃ gagana khaṃ hūṃ samanta svāhā: this blesses the offerings. Then, with

Utterly destroying the illusions of Māra,
emptiness and compassion inseparable,
body whose nature is magical display,
O Hevajra, I bow to you,

one pays homage, and then, as far as one is able, one does the confession of wrongdoing and the rest. Then one makes the aspiration:

Born into the family, keeping the commitments,
free of madness, with Hevajra as my teacher,
devoted to the guru, endowed with compassion,
may I be so in birth after birth.
Ringing vajra and bell with my hands,
reading the profound Dharma,
sharing as food the fluid of the consort,
may I be so in birth after birth.

Then, to make good whatever was excessive or deficient in the rite, one recites the hundred-syllable mantra: oṃ vajrasatva samaya manupālaya vajrasatva tvenopatiṣṭha driḍho me bhava sutoṣyo me bhava anurakto me bhava supoṣyo me bhava sarvva siddhi mme prayaccha sarva karmma succha me cittaṃ śreyaḥ kuru hūṃ ha ha ha ha ho bhagavān sarvva tathāgata vajra mā me muñca vajrī bhava mahā samaya satva āḥ. And with

oṃ You have accomplished every aim of beings;
grant us accordingly the corresponding accomplishments.
Having departed to the buddha realm,
please come again,

and with oṃ vajra muḥ, one requests them to depart.

The same is to be done for the maṇḍalas of the four-armed, the six-armed and the sixteen-armed forms: first one worships with the three syllables, and then with their respective mantras. To Vajravārāhī too one makes the offering as to Nairātmyā. The sixteen-armed one should also be worshiped with the offering mantras of eight lines: oṃ aṣnānanāya hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā, oṃ vidgordhvakeśapartmane hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā, oṃ caturvidśatinetraya hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā, oṃ ṣoḍaśabhujāya hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā, oṃ kṛśṇajīmūtavapuṣe hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā, oṃ kapālamālānekādhāriṇe hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā, oṃ ādhmātakrūracittāya hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā, oṃ ardhendudaṃṣṭrīṇe hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā. Having worshiped with these mantras, one makes the aspiration and the rest as before, and requests them to depart.

Next I shall explain the practice of eating. In the feast gathering, first one sprinkles the food, the soups and so on, with the left ring finger, using liquor made into nectar according to the rite. In the center of the source of phenomena one invites the wheel of wisdom beings with the light rays of the seed at one's heart, or they may equally be emanated from the seed at the heart, and one makes the offering with the mantras given above, inserting into them the names of the foods. Afterwards one requests them to depart, cleanses the mouth a little with the substances of commitment, and offers the torma in the manner of the rite that will be explained. Then, touched by the fire of the hearth at the navel, the foods melt and descend, and dissolve into the commitment being and the meditative-absorption being dwelling at the heart, or into the syllable aṃ with its garland of flames resting on the anthers of the lotus at the navel, and so one should satisfy them. This is the rite of the inner fire offering of oneself, using outer substances. Then, at the end, one gives the torma of the remainder with oṃ ucchiṣṭa vajra adhiṭṛṣṭha imaṃ baliṃ gṛhṇa gṛhṇa hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ, and discards the food.

The inner fire offering that, in turn, does not depend on outer substances is as follows. Kindled by the wind as before, the darkness of all conceptual thought and whatever obscures is burned in the hearth of the navel maṇḍala by the exceedingly fierce Caṇḍālī, and one performs the fire offering with the food of the awakening mind. Wisdom and means, sun and moon, having become of one taste, it is filled with an intense blaze. This second one is the unsurpassed fire offering, which has become the ground of worldly exaltation; it is attained through familiarization moment by moment.

Now I shall explain the torma. Perfume, flowers and so on are set to the right. In front, in vessels such as the śakaṭa and the bhara, fish and meat, jambuḍika and the like, fine food and three handfuls of water are provided, together with whatever substances of commitment have been obtained, and set out. Viewing them as before, one makes them into nectar by the sequence of the rite of tasting nectar. Then one should view, one above another, red eight-petaled lotuses resting in the openings of the three sources of phenomena of the three realities, white, red and black.

Then Gaurī, emanated from the seed at the heart, summons them with oṃ gaurī indra ādīna saparivārāna ākarśaya jaḥ. With oṃ corī indra ādīna saparivārāna praveśaya hūṃ, Caurī makes them enter the lotuses. With oṃ vetālī indra adīna saparivārāna bandhaya vaṃ, Vetālī, emanated, binds them with her bonds. With oṃ ghasmarī indra ādīna saparivārān praveśaya hoḥ, they are brought under control.

On the southern petal is Yama, black, with two hands holding a staff and making the threatening gesture, riding a buffalo. On the western petal is Varuṇa, white, riding a makara, with seven serpent hoods, holding a noose and a conch. On the northern petal is Kubera, yellow and stout of body, seated on a treasure, holding a citron and a mongoose. All of these wear jeweled ornaments and jeweled crowns; Yama has a sun seat and the radiance of the sun. On the petal of Īśāna is Maheśvara (Śiva), white, three-eyed, with a crown of matted locks, holding a trident and a skull cup as his emblems, wearing a skirt of tiger skin, riding a bull, embracing Gaurī, with a moon seat and radiance.

On the petal of fire is Agni, deep red and pot-bellied, riding a goat, with tawny eyebrows and matted locks, his hands holding a rosary and a water pot, with a sun seat and radiance. On the petal of Nairṛti is the lord of the rākṣasas, blue, riding a corpse, his tawny hair bristling upward, with crooked brows, holding a curved knife and a skull cup; some say he holds a sword and a shield. On the petal of Nairṛti is Vāyu, blue, holding a fan, riding a deer, with a jeweled crown, a moon seat and radiance, and wearing jeweled ornaments. On the anthers is the earth goddess Pṛthivī, yellow, with jeweled garments and ornaments, holding a vase in her left hand, her right hand making the gesture of one who comes forward first having made a pledge, with a moon seat and the radiance of the moon; she abides surrounded by hungry ghosts and beings of darkness.

To the south is the Sun, holding a lotus marked with the sun, standing in a chariot drawn by green horses, with his own seat and radiance. Mercury, green, holding an arrow and a bow, standing on a lotus. Jupiter, yellow, holding a rosary and a water pot; some say he holds a jeweled bowl and a rosary; he rides a frog. Venus, white, holding a rosary and a water pot, standing on a lotus. Saturn, black, holding a rosary and a staff, riding a tortoise.
