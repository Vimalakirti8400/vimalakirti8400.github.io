About this text

The text known as The True Words (Tibetan: Dentsik Dzokso) is an invocation of truth, a short verse composition that praises generosity and the offering of fine food and drink, likening such giving to a grove of wish-fulfilling trees and to a clear, steady lamp for beings standing at the brink of saṃsāra. It begins with homage to the Three Jewels and goes on to describe how merit and other benefits arise from giving, so that it serves as an aspiration and a statement of truth, a recitation meant to bring about the blessings it names. A Sanskrit title is not evident from the available source lines, so it is not given here. The text is Toh 815 in the Kangyur, the Tibetan canon of translated words of the Buddha, where it belongs among the shorter works of the tantra section.

Translation

The true words are complete. Homage to the Three Jewels.

Fine food and drink, savory and fair in color, fragrant, with rising steam, adorned with every excellent quality; like a grove of wish-fulfilling trees, rare and flawless, covered in ornaments, with branches heavy with clusters of blossoms so that the tips of the boughs bow low, flowers opening and petals beautifully adorned, while bees, drunk and intoxicated, swarm and press upon them so that they tremble and sway gently; and with precious branches and tender, unbroken shoots hanging down and swaying in the breeze, all of these are brought forth. Beings, without even noticing it, stand at the brink of the great abyss of saṃsāra, and this giving is like a lamp burning clear and bright and free of stain. From such giving come merit, causes in harmony with merit, virtue, and causes in harmony with virtue, which are the ground of every happiness.

Therefore may this great benefactor, this person of great and noble nature, whose body is made firm by the ten paths of virtuous action, be endowed with length of life, beauty, strength, splendor, wealth, sovereignty, wisdom, and eloquence. May the benefactor's merit that has not yet arisen come to arise. May the benefactor's wealth that has already arisen increase and flourish.

Likewise, may the benefactor's father and mother, who have undergone the greatest hardship in nourishing, raising, bringing up, and caring for their child, and who have let their child see the radiant world of Jambudvīpa with his own eyes, receive their due share of the first fruits of this merit.

Likewise, may the benefactor's wives, sons, and daughters, and all the benefactor's relatives, friends, and companions, together with every group of kin who live and dwell in this household, have long lives, great wealth, and many attendants. And may those who have died and passed on from this life obtain a distinguished rebirth, a place of birth different from and better than before. Born in the realm of the gods, may they delight in the presence of the noble Maitreya.

Likewise, may the sovereign lord, the supreme of kings, the great king, the holy ruler, glorious Lha Tsenpo, arrive where he has not yet come, and having arrived, may he come to dwell in peace. May he also attain the kingship of a universal ruler, a turner of the wheel of power.

Likewise, may the people of the land who live in the surrounding villages, of the four classes, the brahmins, the nobles, the merchants, and the commoners, be free from sickness and free from harm.

Likewise, may Brahmā, lord of the world of endurance, Indra (Śakra), lord of the gods, the four guardians of the world, the twenty-eight great generals of the yakṣas, the nāga king Nanda, Upananda, the yakṣa general Sumana, Himavat (the Snowy One), Pañcika, Pañcalaṇḍa and the others, Maṇibhadra, Pūrṇabhadra, and the mother Hārītī with her five hundred sons and her retinue, and furthermore all the gods, nāgas, yakṣas, asuras, garuḍas, gandharvas, kinnaras, and mahoragas who rejoice in the teaching of the Blessed One, and the gods who dwell in this benefactor's house, and the special gods whom the benefactor venerates, may their splendor and power, and their palaces, increase and flourish. May they be endowed with splendor, endowed with strength, unconquerable by others, and renowned as great. And may the treasuries and storehouses of this great benefactor, his herds of cattle, his herds of every kind of livestock, and the plowing and tilling of his fields, flourish as well.

May they guard, protect, and shelter, in accordance with the Dharma, all the doors of work and every undertaking, high and low. May harms be turned back. May what is sought for good purpose become manifest.

In the same way, joined with the faith of this benefactor, may all the harmful sufferings of those born in the hells be calmed. May animals cease devouring one another. May all the sufferings of hunger and thirst of the hungry ghosts (pretas) be calmed. May all the sufferings of human beings, their quarrels, anger, wandering, and exhaustion, be calmed. May all the sufferings of the gods, their death and transmigration, their falling, their dispersal and destruction, be calmed.

In short, may all sentient beings and all living creatures born in the three realms touch and attain that supreme Dharma of nirvāṇa, which is unborn and unarisen, uncreated and unfabricated, free from death and transmigration, without abiding, without any object to be grasped; which is bliss, peace, coolness, and well-being; which is a dwelling place, an island, a protector, a refuge, an ally and support, and the unshakable.

Those yakṣas of inexhaustible mounts and armies, who wear divine garments and carry garlands, who have taken refuge in the Buddha, praised by those skilled in honoring the three worlds, the five-crested one, Vajrapāṇi, Vaiśravaṇa, glorious Maṇibhadra, and the rest: may all of them together always guard your house in accordance with the Dharma.

The five-crested one and the child-snatching Hārītī with her beloved children and retinue, Maṇibhadra with his army, the lord of yakṣas Vajrapāṇi with his own divisions and company, and also those other lords of yakṣas, renowned great hosts who delight in the teaching: may all of them, in harmony, always protect your kin and your dear relatives.

By night and in the dawn, at the rising of the sun and at midday, and at the setting of the sun, may auspiciousness always be yours. May the glorious great Vajrapāṇi, Maṇibhadra, Pārśvaja, and the others fully protect you together with your family and friends.

May those who are brave in giving have household and retinue, strength and wealth, accomplished, and free from illness. May the worthy recipients of offerings also increase through a gathering of good qualities, and may the grain flourish abundantly. May the stūpas be honored, and may the teaching of the Blessed One blaze for a long time. May the king rule in accordance with the Dharma and protect, and may all sentient beings experience happiness in the four joys.

This completes the aspiration prayer drawn from the Jātaka tales of giving.

Translator's notes

The passage breaks off mid-sentence in the source, and the translation preserves that. The opening "true words" (བདེན་ཚིག) marks this as a dedicatory or aspiration text, not a sūtra proper. The rendering of ལྷ་བཙན་པོ as the royal name Lha Tsenpo is a proper name; the king's identity is not established within this passage. Several yakṣa and divine names are rendered by their probable Sanskrit equivalents (Nanda, Upananda, Sumana, Himavat, Pañcika, Maṇibhadra, Pūrṇabhadra, Hārītī); the identifications of Pañcalaṇḍa and Pañcika, which the Tibetan gives as two names, are the least certain. The opening simile of the wish-fulfilling grove is syntactically dense, and its images are rendered somewhat freely for flow. The Tibetan has a few corrupt or unclear places. The line concerning the pretas' suffering contains a garbled phrase (apparently a scribal corruption), and I have rendered the sense of the calming of hunger and thirst. The final "four joys" (dga' bzhi) is rendered literally; the reference is uncertain and may reflect a textual variant. Several yakṣa names are reconstructed: the five-crested one is Pāñcika, Vaiśravaṇa is the name behind "wealth-giver" (nor sbyin), and Pārśvaja (འཕགས་སྐྱེས) is a tentative identification. The yakṣa Pāñcika and Hārītī (འཕྲོག་མ, "the snatcher") are standard companions in such protective litanies.
