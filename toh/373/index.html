About this text

The Great King of Tantras Called the Arising of Glorious Saṃvara, known in Sanskrit as the Śrī Mahāsaṃvarodaya Tantrarāja and in Tibetan as Pal Demchok Jungwa, is one of the principal explanatory tantras of the Cakrasaṃvara cycle, a yoginī tantra centered on the deity Heruka. Framed as a dialogue in which Vajrapāṇi questions the Blessed One, its thirty-three chapters address the yogas of the generation and completion stages, the maṇḍala and its deities, mantras, mudrās, empowerment, the torma offering and other rites, and close with verses on the inconceivable, coemergent great bliss in which wisdom and compassion are indivisible. Its colophon presents it as drawn from a vast Heruka Abhidhāna Tantra of three hundred thousand lines, and records its translation by the Indian preceptor Parahitānanta and the Tibetan translator Mönlam Drak, with a later revision by Zhönnu Pal against a manuscript of the paṇḍita Vanaratna. It is Toh 373 in the Kangyur, where it belongs to the Tantra section among the unexcelled yoga tantras.

Translation

In the language of India: śrī mahā sambarodaya tantra rājā nāma. In the language of Tibet: The Great King of Tantras Called the Arising of Glorious Saṃvara.

Homage to glorious Heruka.

Thus did I hear at one time. The Blessed One, the body, speech, and mind of all the tathāgatas, was dwelling in the bhagas of the vajra consorts, surrounded by noble Nanda and others who were free of attachment. In the midst of eighty koṭis of lords of yoga, such as noble Avalokiteśvara, he looked at Vajrapāṇi and smiled. Vajrapāṇi rose from his seat, draped his upper robe over one shoulder, placed his right knee on the ground, joined his palms, and made this request to the Blessed One:

"O Saṃvara, supreme in every aspect,
what are the characteristics of the yoga of generation,
and how, too, is that of completion?
Blessed God, I wish to hear it from you.
How are wind, fire, water, and earth,
and likewise space?
How, O God, are the five aspects?
Lord, what likewise are the six sense objects?
What is the blessing of the three bodies
that abide outside and within?
How is the body of the deity?
Lord, speak likewise of the goddesses.
What, O God, are the moon and the sun?
How are the five paths?
What is the nature of the body?
And likewise, what is the form of the channels?
What is the measure of the number of the channels?
And likewise, what are the constituents of the body?
Lord, please explain to me
the signs of the hidden commitment.
How many signs are there of the sacred places and so on,
of the nature of outer and inner?
How are the grounds and the rest attained?
How do the signs come to be seen?
What are your twelve rites?
How is the recitation of mantra done?
What rosaries are suitable?
How many recitations should there be?
What is the yoga of the deity's form?
How many drawings of the maṇḍala are there?
What, O God, are the accomplishments of mantra?
What is the worship of the maiden?
Which are the days for acting and for not acting?
Lord, what is the intoxicating torma?
How, O God, are the five nectars and the rest?
How are the five hooks and the rest?
Please explain the drawing of the maṇḍala.
How is the laying of the lines done?
How is the ground thoroughly purified?
How is the protection wheel made?
What are the tasks of the master?
How is the disciple taken in?
How many empowerments are there?
And how, too, is the fourth?
What is the determination of time?
And likewise, what is the ransoming from death?
How many names are there for the four times?
How are the four continents?
What are timely and untimely accomplishments?
How is the conduct to be practiced?
How many yoginī tantras are there?
How are the yoga tantras?
What is the number of the sūtra collections?
And likewise, how many perfections are there?
Consecration, fire offering, and sacrificial offering,
how do the accomplishments of mantra come about?
How, O God, is the extraction of essences?
How is the intoxicating drink taken?
How, O God, does secret mantra arise?
How is the extraction of mantras done?
What, O God, is forceful subjugation?
Lord, what is taking into one's care?
Blessed One, what are the realities?
What are emptiness and compassion?
What is the intrinsic nature of emptiness?
How is suchness the intrinsic nature?
Lord, tell of the characteristics of meditation
that fully knows all phenomena."

This is the first chapter, the Request, from the Tantra of the Arising of Glorious Saṃvara.

The Blessed One declared: "Vajrapāṇi, excellent, excellent that you have asked about this arising of the secret.

Then, for that reason, I shall rightly explain
the meditation of the stage of generation.
Through the nature of diverse karma
there come to be the four places of birth:
the egg-born, the womb-born,
the heat-and-moisture-born, and the miraculously born.
Geese, cranes, peacocks,
parrots, swans, and the like are egg-born.
Elephants, buffaloes, cattle, horses,
donkeys, humans, and the like are born from the womb.
Worms, insects, butterflies,
fish, and the like are born from heat and moisture.
The gods and the hell-beings,
likewise those in the intermediate state of existence,
and those of the first eon and the like,
these are miraculously born beings.
In eastern Videha, Godānīya,
and likewise northern Kuru,
the humans of these three continents
live wholly by great enjoyments.
Dull and confused, they make no distinctions;
they are without conceptual thought and without analysis.

Those who are well born in Jambudvīpa,
renowned as the land of karma,
there, for beings, deeds done well and done ill,
superior, middling, and inferior,
come to appear as the ripening
of their former births.
Through pride, avarice, malicious thoughts,
deceit, overweening conceit,
attachment, hatred, delusion, and the like,
they are tormented by fevers, diseases, and the rest.
The foremost and best of the people of Jambudvīpa
are those born in the central land;
depending on their former virtue,
their faculties become sharp, middling, or dull.
To be born as a human being is the first excellent fruit;
to find renunciation and leave one's own home is the second;
having gone forth, to accomplish virtue is the third;
to attain a one-pointed mind is declared the fourth.
The illusion-like samādhi
human beings do not know,
because they have heaped up
the habitual tendencies of afflictions from beginningless time.
Therefore, through karma done before,
birth and death come about.
As long as it has not found the coming together of the parents,
it remains seven days in the intermediate state of existence.
The being in the intermediate state,
like a traveler on a journey,
is thus, by the noose of karma,
born among the six destinies.
When the being full of craving
has seen the union of father and mother and the rest,
consciousness, as one rides a horse,
mounts the steed of wind,
and in an instant, a single moment,
comes with the utmost speed;
and by the force of its great delight
it enters by the path of the mouth.
The seventy-two thousand channels
are stirred, and in that instant,
through the melting of the vowels and consonants (āli and kāli),
supreme joy is attained.
In the midst of the semen and blood
it abides in the form of a drop.
Its first form is the jelly-like;
the oblong is the second;
it grows into the soft lump, the third;
and likewise the fourth is the rounded mass.
Merely by being stirred by the winds
it takes on something like the form of flesh.
When the seed has reached five months,
the five limbs fully emerge.
In the seventh month the hair of the head and body,
the nails, and the sexual signs appear.
In the eighth month the sense faculties
and the limbs of the body come forth.
In the ninth month it is fully complete.
In the tenth month it is endowed with mind.
The jelly-like by nature is Akṣobhya;
the oblong is Ratnasambhava;
the protector Amitābha is the soft lump;
the rounded mass is Amoghasiddhi;
the hardened is Vairocana:
thus it is taught in five aspects.
Urine and blood are Akṣobhya;
the nature of the seed is Amitābha;
the lump is Ratnasambhava;
in what is mixed into a single taste Vairocana dwells.
In the middle of the place of birth are two channels,
abiding likewise on the right and the left.
From the left, it is said, comes semen;
from the right, likewise, comes blood.
These, when gathered into one,
are the nature of the sphere of reality (dharmadhātu).
When the seed of karma has gained its power
and has been fully turned about by the wind,
the face definitely turns
toward the door of the source of phenomena.
If it rests on the right side,
it abides squatting, facing outward;
if it rests on the left side,
it turns to face the belly of the wisdom woman.
The wise mark the moment in time
in the sequence of the falling of the seed.
Whatever seed the wind moves on the right
becomes in every case a male;
whatever seed the wind moves from the left
definitely becomes a female;
whatever seed moves in the middle of both
always becomes neuter.
The water element should be known as the father's;
the fire element is the mother's.
Skin, flesh, and blood
are said to be born from the mother;
sinews, marrow, and semen
are said to be born from the father.
The body made up of these six,
which Vajrasattva has declared:
form, feeling, perception,
formations, and likewise consciousness,
these aggregates definitely arise
as the nature of the five buddhas.
So that, knowing the stage of the production of existence,
one may attain perfect buddhahood,
this reality of fully knowing the aggregates
has been explained by those who teach it.

This is the second chapter, the division of the stages that definitively teaches the stage of generation.

Then I shall rightly and fully explain
the meditation of the stage of completion,
by merely knowing which
one swiftly attains accomplishment.
Relying on the maṇḍala of the body,
there are the Dharma body and the enjoyment body;
because perfect awakening is accomplished in stages,
it is called the maṇḍala of the body.
One of dull faculties should meditate on and contemplate
the maṇḍala of the stage of generation.
One of sharp faculties contemplates, in the manner of an instant,
the maṇḍala that is mind alone.
Meditation on the stage of completion
through the yoga of the instantaneous mode is this:
the three realms are by nature the celestial palace,
and living beings are those of the maṇḍala;
in its center, in the instantaneous mode,
the yogin is the lord of the maṇḍala.
Through the mantra oṃ āḥ hūṃ
there are the maṇḍalas of body, speech, and mind;
in the heavens, on the earth, and beneath the earth,
in an instant all becomes a single body.
In the instantaneous mode there is yoga;
in the instantaneous mode one recites the secret mantra.
Union in equality with all the faculties
is the supreme bliss of the net of ḍākinīs.
The four elements and the five aggregates,
and likewise the nature of the six sense objects,
are the wisdom of the blood-drinking goddesses;
one should not imagine any difference in them.
The maṇḍala is sealed with suchness;
likewise it is emptiness, equal to space.
The manifold is suchness and selflessness;
the means is the power of compassion;
their union, free of conceptual thought,
is the supreme heart of the maṇḍala.
Even though one contemplates the union of nonconceptuality,
the nonconceptual is then beyond thought.
That too is supreme in every aspect:
the lord of bliss, without aspects.
That reality, the nature of thing and nonthing,
is ever made to arise through existence.
Without the concepts of manifold elaboration,
great bliss ever arises.
Through the nature of nonarising,
birth rightly arises from it.
Because it is not inert, it is self-aware.
Since it is not seen by unknowing,
it is formless and abides nowhere at all,
and because it is unchanging, it is stable.
Because conventional birth arises,
it is not annihilation, and so it is not a nonthing.
In the very nature of innate joy
all phenomena are innate.
Self-blessing and self-arisen,
it is indestructible and without decay.
Through tasting the flavor of nonarising,
meditation too is that very reality.
Through discerning emptiness individually,
concentration also becomes wisdom.
The meditation that fully knows all phenomena,
that very meditation is nonmeditation.

Perfect awakening, great bliss,
and likewise the supreme Great Seal (mahāmudrā):
so that one may enter that reality, the Dharma,
these are fully taught by way of their divisions.
Through the instructions of the holy guru
they become clear, and not otherwise.
The Saṃvara of all the buddhas
is fully established in the syllables e vaṃ.
The activities of body, speech, and mind
are the Saṃvara supreme in every aspect.
Saṃvara is awakening, supreme bliss;
it cannot be seen and cannot be expressed.
The secret of all the buddhas
is the supreme bliss of union.
This stage of self-blessing
becomes clear through the kindness of the guru.

This is the third chapter, the division of the stages that definitively teaches the stage of completion.

Then, for that reason, I shall rightly explain
the characteristics of the four elements.
Whatever things there are, without exception,
are of the nature of the elements.
Things rest upon the earth;
fire cooks them all;
water dissolves them;
wind moves them together;
space abides as empty of objects,
and there all things arise from it.
Wherever one of them abides,
all four are present there.
Grass, trees, plants, creepers, and the like
have consciousness only as if they were inert.
Those who are sentient beings of the six destinies
abide as if endowed with consciousness
in each and every joint.
Thus all the things of existence
should be understood by the intelligent.
When sentient beings die,
the winds of all the limbs and lesser limbs
are all set in motion;
in that instant fire gives up its heat;
water dries up altogether,
and then feeling ceases;
only the solidity of the earth element
of the body and the rest remains.
Birth and death
everywhere come about through the elements.
Gods, asuras, and humans
are not born without the elements.
The gods, the guardians of the world, and the rest,
all abide together with them.
All the Vedas and philosophical systems
ever affirm them and meditate on them,
because they go everywhere
and abide in all persons on the face of the earth.
The four elements, the supreme lords,
are rightly explained in all the treatises.
Wind should rightly be seen as the life breath;
fire has the characteristic of sustaining life;
water is the very essence of nectar;
earth abides merely as the field.
The deity who ever abides there
is the lord of supreme consciousness;
into the forms of the five deities
that consciousness is wholly transformed.
Form, feeling, perception,
likewise formations, and consciousness;
the mirror-like, equality,
individual discernment,
likewise the accomplishment of activity,
and the utterly pure dharmadhātu:
these wisdoms are fully present.
Vairocana, Ratnasambhava,
Amitābha, Amoghasiddhi,
and likewise Akṣobhya himself:
the five, of one taste, are perfect awakening.
Form, sound, likewise smell,
taste, touch, and likewise phenomena
are to be known as the six objects;
these are explained as completely pure.
The elements are to be known as eighteen;
they are the very nature of the aggregates.
One should meditate on the supreme syllable
so as to attain the fruit of buddhahood.
One should meditate on the eye and the rest:
the consciousness of the eye faculty
is the magical display of the vajra of mind;
the meaning of the three natures, completely purified,
becomes the state of luminosity.
Sound, the ear, and its consciousness
are of the nature of nonobjectification.
The nose, smell, and its consciousness too
should likewise be known as completely pure.
Taste, the tongue, and its consciousness
are completely pure as the ultimate itself.
The body, touch, and its consciousness too
arise with an illusion-like nature.
The mind, phenomena, and mental consciousness
are completely pure as the very essence.
Because the six consciousnesses enter the foundation of all (ālaya),
they are the Tathāgata.
When the state of luminosity is attained,
glorious Heruka truly abides.
Through the joining of object and subject
the nonconceptual state is reached.
Through abiding in the supreme of all aspects,
the objects should be known as completely pure.
The Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha
are likewise imagined as three in one.
The three refuges and the three realities,
the three bodies, the three liberations,
the three faces, the three syllables, the three deities:
these are the very nature of the three realms.
The three maṇḍalas and the three yogas
are taught as the three paths;
the three commitments and the three virtues,
likewise body, speech, and mind;
wisdom and means,
and their union is the third.
Through the nature of the source of phenomena,
the way the three secrets are seen;
through union that does not objectify the three spheres,
the very nature of the three mantras;
of the nature of outer and inner,
the very essence of the three channels.
The outer is the Dharma of the worldly;
the inner is the nature of the deities and the rest.
Because outer and inner are pure,
the yogin attains buddhahood.
In the nature of the dharmadhātu
the deity should be directly perceived.
The nature supreme in every aspect
should be fully imagined as the goddess.
In the nature of the primordial deity
Vajrasattva dwells.
The signs of the sacred places, the fields, and the rest,
the assembly of heroes and yoginīs,
and likewise, in nondual union,
the deities stream forth;
from the streaming forth of countless deities
one should imagine the measured maṇḍala.
The yoga of the deity is inconceivable,
and the play of the buddhas too is inconceivable.
In the form of the net of ḍākinīs
glorious Heruka is truly united.
Their indivisible essential nature
I have explained for meditation.
All of them attain nonduality,
free of grasped and grasper.
The coarse is said to be sound;
the subtle becomes the nature of mind.
That reality free of thought
is renowned as the supreme state.

This is the fourth chapter, the division of the stages of the purification, as deities, of the four elements, the five aspects, and the six objects.

Then I shall rightly explain
the full division of moon and sun.
Joined on the right and on the left,
they move in their proper order.
On the left, beginning from the palate,
and entering the maṇḍala of the navel,
the channel of the moon faces downward,
and āli flows down as the moon.
On the right, beginning from the navel,
and entering the place of the palate,
the channel of the sun faces upward,
and kāli ever flows as the sun.
The characteristic of the movement of the two channels
from the two doors of the nose is this:
the left channel is the path of entering,
and the right is the path of going out.
From the rising of the sun
until its setting is day;
from its setting
until its rising is night.
Day and night are a full day;
a watch is called a period.
Four periods are known as the day,
and likewise the night has four periods.

In a single day there are
sixteen divisions of transition;
for half a period at a time the wind
ever moves through the nostrils.
Beginning from the first day of the waxing fortnight,
for three days, in each half period,
the wind of the moon moves;
then for three days the wind of the sun;
in this sequence it goes on
until the fifteenth of the waxing fortnight.
Beginning from the first day of the waning fortnight,
for three days, in the morning,
what is known as the wind of the sun moves,
and likewise until the fifteenth.
Since a quarter of each period
is called an hour (ghaṭikā),
in a single day and night the hours
should be known to number thirty-two.
By the measure of sixty-four daṇḍas
in a single day and night,
half an hour is a daṇḍa,
which is said to be an eighth of a period.
The going out and coming in of the wind at the nostrils
is renowned as a single breath.
Those skilled in the yoga of the winds
should know six breaths as one life breath (prāṇa).
Fifty full life breaths,
together with three breaths and a quarter,
make the daṇḍa of the first day
at the time when the sun moves to the north.
At the time when the sun moves to the south,
the night too becomes the same.
The increase and decrease of each daṇḍa
should be known through the divisions of time.
Eleven breaths together with a quarter
are the increase and decrease of each transition.
A third of a breath, a quarter of that,
and a sixth of that change with each day.
If, departing from its movement in half periods,
it shifts in reverse,
disputes and the like will surely come about;
therefore the wise should examine these well.
When for one, two, three, and four,
five, and six days the wind
moves constantly in reverse,
then there will be a great dispute.
If it moves in reverse constantly for half a month,
a great illness will arise.
If it moves in reverse for a month,
dear kin will perish.
If it moves in reverse for a month and a half,
one will die in six months.
This should be known for the ordinary times.
Further, I shall explain the special features.
When the sun has gone into the seven equals,
at the day and month of one's birth,
that is the time called "the expansion,"
the time for the determination of death.
The house of the zodiac in which a person is born,
and the other, the seventh from it:
because the sun has passed there through seven equal houses,
it is explained as "the seven equals."
The winds that ever move
from the path of the sun in every case
the wise should mark as time,
without interruption, again and again.
At whatever moment of time the wind
enters into a different course,
when that moment of time is complete,
one will die; of this there is no doubt.
Beginning with half a day, then a full day, and likewise up to one day and night,
and from that, two days, three days, and likewise up to four days,
whatever wind, relying on the channel, moves by the path of the sun, not the left,
from that one should know these: fourteen, twelve, ten, eight, six, and four.
From five to twenty-five, these days proceed by increasing five at a time;
then, by increasing one at a time, up to ten tripled with three added.
From that, at the time of the expansion, there are three, two, and one years; six, three, two, and one months;
and for the rest, one lives for fifteen, ten, five, three, two, and one days.
Having drawn a wheel of three tiers
with thirty-seven squares,
one should draw in order the winds of life span and of life breath
and the signs of the days.
For the days in between,
from the fifth day to the twenty-fifth,
whatever sixteen numbers have not been explained,
I shall state them in full.
If the wind moves in the sequence
of the sixth, seventh, eighth, and ninth days,
even one endowed with good qualities dies
in three years less twenty-four days.
If it moves in the sequence of the days
eleven, twelve, thirteen, and fourteen,
even one endowed with good qualities dies
in two years less twenty-four days.
Likewise, if the wind moves in the sequence
of the sixteenth day,
the seventeenth, the eighteenth,
and the nineteenth, then even one with good qualities,
in one year less twice twelve days,
being without refuge,
will without doubt go
to the city of Yama.
If the wind moves in the sequence
of the twenty-first, twenty-second, twenty-third, and twenty-fourth days,
even one endowed with good qualities dies
in six months less six days.
Having drawn a wheel of two tiers
with thirty-two squares,
there one should draw in order the life span,
the divisions, the wind, and the signs of the numbers.
When one has known in every aspect
all these signs of death,
if the intelligent one, by ransoming death,
wishes for the stable state,
then by thoroughly purifying the channels
the wind is purified to the same extent;
the yogin should press it down
again and again, in the order of each.
Having drawn it into the left nostril,
one should bind the channel and press it down;
likewise, drawing the wind
into the right, one should press it down slowly.
The number of breaths
that move in a single day for sentient beings
is twenty-one thousand
with six hundred more.
Through the Mahendra wind of earth comes kingship;
through water all aims are rightly achieved;
when the wind of fire moves,
there come hunger, thirst, weariness, suffering, and illness,
and all activities fail:
so the supreme Vajradhara declared.
The wind of air brings disputes, sorrow, and confusion,
loss of wealth, and affliction.
The Mahendra wind brings gain, acquisition,
and the gathering of wealth, grain, and the like.
When the wind of water moves,
it brings about every desired accomplishment;
therefore its application is supreme and foremost,
as Vajrasattva declared.
The wind is the very nature
of the Blessed Heruka, and becomes threefold:
the left is of the nature of wisdom,
and the right is the very self of compassion.

The union of the two, indivisible,
also moves through both places.
Therefore the one who knows that reality
should know there virtue, nonvirtue, and the rest.
For destroying poison and the like, and for the rest,
for producing auspicious glory,
the nature of wisdom is highly praised.
Ever glorious is the power of compassion:
for battle array, play and sport, and feasts,
for cutting and for piercing,
for the rites of burning and of boiling,
the nature of compassion is highly praised.
Further, the vajra holder whose nature is both
should be known to bring doubt;
since virtue and nonvirtue are not certain,
the knower of the winds should mark it here.
If the lord moves in āli
and someone abiding in kāli asks a question,
or if, when it moves in kāli, he abides in āli,
the aim he desires will fail.
If someone abiding on the side
from which the lord is moving asks,
all his aims will be fully accomplished.
From abiding in both comes doubt.
Through the nature of the wind
the three bodies of the lord should also be known:
entering is the Dharma body,
abiding is the enjoyment body,
and going out is called the emanation body;
these are held to be the three bodies.
Whether it is the questioner's or one's own,
with the Dharma body all is virtuous;
with the enjoyment body there will be doubt;
the emanation body shows conflict.
The breath control of the yogin
abides as the very essence of the five buddhas.
In whichever place, left or right,
it moves in its proper order.
The light that moves from the right
is the maṇḍala of fire;
its color is like a forest flower;
that is the deity Amitābha.
The light that moves from the left
is ever the maṇḍala of wind;
it appears green in color;
the deity is the supreme Amoghasiddhi.
The light that moves from both
appears like the light of gold;
this is the wind of the Mahendra maṇḍala;
Ratnasambhava ever moves there.
That which moves slowly from below,
white like the kunda flower and the moon,
moves as the maṇḍala of water;
it is the great light, the Vajra Lord.
The wind that accompanies the whole body
and makes all minds enter
is the lord Vairocana,
renowned as the going out.
The yoginī, in meditative equipoise,
should count the entering wind
up to a number such as a hundred thousand,
always reciting it free of sound.
When the practitioner has completed
the hundred thousand recitations free of sound,
even one whose life has declined
will live five years here; of this there is no doubt.
Always rising in the morning,
one should count the wind up to a thousand.
Therefore, in the yoga of the wind
one should always remain in meditative equipoise.
Or else, through the yoga of the vase,
one should always conquer the Lord of Death.
The one who knows himself
should fill everything with wind down to the soles of the feet;
having made the vase retention stable,
it is explained that one destroys him by three degrees.
The lowest is about thirty-six;
double that is likewise the middling;
triple should be known as the great;
with that vase retention one should conquer.
Having first learned the vase retention,
sitting oneself in cross-legged posture,
one rubs the hands three times
and then snaps the fingers six times.
As long as it takes to reach thirty-six,
so long one should hold the vase retention.
Destroying him with the great, the triple,
is with a hundred and eight.
One who desires the stable state
should conquer through that very thing with diligence.
Because one conquers through the practice of the vase,
one goes far from the Lord of Death.
When one has learned the vase retention
and abides steadfast in cessation,
even in a thousand eons
the Lord of Death will not come near him.
Whoever contemplates in meditative equipoise
the wind that has reached the center of the heart lotus
in the form of a white hūṃ
will not perceive sense objects and the rest.
Going upward, the wind is saṃsāra;
traveling downward, it is nirvāṇa;
in the lotus of the heart
it abides as nonabiding nirvāṇa.
The winds that move above and below
are joined together by the mind;
if one trains through that union,
one attains the stable state.
Whoever does not know the yoga of the wind,
or, knowing it, does not practice it,
becomes a worm tormented
by the many masses of the suffering of saṃsāra.
The wind that goes and comes
should be examined by the intelligent.
All is blessed by the wind;
the wind goes everywhere.

This is the fifth chapter, the division of the stages of the instructions on the moon and the sun.

Then I shall fully explain
the definitive ascertainment of the five excellent, supreme paths.
The practitioner who is perfect in the aims of self and others
should examine virtue and nonvirtue.
From the movement of the maṇḍalas
of fire and of wind,
and likewise of Mahendra and of water,
the characteristics of pacifying, increasing, subjugating, and summoning,
and likewise of killing and expelling,
should be examined by the wise.
One who does not know their application
will labor to no purpose.
Through fire comes death;
through wind wealth declines;
through Mahendra comes kingship;
water bestows wealth and grain.
The element that moves from the right
is the very maṇḍala of the fire god (Agni);
red in color and clear,
it is the arising of the Lotus Lord.
The element that moves from the left
is the very maṇḍala of wind;
green in color and clear,
it is the arising of the Karma Lord.
The element that moves from both,
clear like the color of gold,
is the very maṇḍala of Mahendra;
it is the movement of the Jewel Lord.
The element that is slow and without movement
is the very maṇḍala of water;
appearing like pure crystal,
it is the movement of the Vajra Lord.
It fully gathers all the elements
and rightly holds support and supported.
Vairocana is the great wind
that moves out from the body of the dying.
Without knowing the reality of the wind,
neither rites nor nonrites are accomplished;
because the wind travels everywhere,
the logicians do not know it.
With the reality of the wind going first,
one should accomplish the reality of mantra.
That which has become the life of sentient beings,
called the wind, performs all actions.
This is the mount of consciousness
that brings attainment of the state of buddhahood.
It is the secret of all the tantras,
the means that is the cause of awakening.

This is the sixth chapter, the division of the stages that definitively teaches the five paths.

Next, I shall fully explain,
in due order, the wheel of channels.
The channels that run throughout the body
number seventy-two thousand.
The channels and the secondary channels
all rest upon their sites;
the channels numbering one hundred and twenty
are declared to be the principal ones.
The sites and lands of the channels
are twenty-four in number,
and the three channels at their center
pervade them all and are their support.

At the head is Pullīra,
abiding as the channel through which teeth and nails flow.
At the crown of the head is Jālandhara,
abiding as the channel through which head hair and body hair flow.
Oḍiyāna is the channel of the right ear,
through which skin and impurities flow.
At the back of the head is Arbuda,
the channel through which flesh flows.
Godāvarī is the left ear,
the channel through which the sinews flow.
Between the eyebrows is Rāmeśvarī,
through which bone always flows.
At the eyes abides Devīkoṭi,
the channel through which the liver flows.
Mālava is the two shoulders,
where abides the channel flowing to the heart.
At the armpits is Kāmarūpa,
through which the eyes always flow.
The two breasts are Oḍi,
the channel through which bile always flows.
Triśaku is the navel,
where the channel abides through which the lungs flow.
The tip of the nose is Kosala,
abiding as the channel through which the coils of the intestines flow.
The mouth is Kaliṅga,
abiding as the channel through which the colon always flows.
The throat is Lampāka,
the channel through which the stomach always flows.
Kāñci is the site of the heart,
the channel through which feces flow.
Between the thighs is Himālaya,
the channel through which flows the juncture of the supreme lady.
Pretapurī is the sexual organ,
the channel through which phlegm flows.
Gṛhadevatā is the anus,
through which pus always flows.
The two thighs are Saurāṣṭra,
through which blood always flows.
At the two calves abides Suvarṇadvīpa,
the channel through which rajas flows.
The toes are Nagara;
know it as the channel through which fat always flows.
The soles of the feet are Sindhu,
the embodied one through which tears flow.
Marudeśa is the big toes,
abiding as the channel through which saliva always flows.
Kulatā is the knees,
abiding as the channel through which mucus always flows.

Among the channels abiding at their center,
in Lalanā urine flows.
The channel on the right through which blood flows
is known as Rasanā.
The one in the central part fully enters,
reaching into the middle of the lotus at the heart.
Like the blossom of a plantain's spike,
it hangs with its mouth turned downward;
it blazes like a lamp of sesame oil,
and the awakening mind flows through it evenly.
Know this to be Avadhūtī,
the bestower of the innate.

The channels Lalanā and the others
are the chief of all the channels;
therefore the others here rely on them,
just as other rivers enter the Gaṅgā and the Sindhu.
Having become one, it is the khakhamukha;
that itself is the source of birth, and nothing else.
Know that, with the nature of the enjoyment body (saṃbhogakāya),
it abides in the body.
Women too have three principal channels,
the channels Lalanā and the others.
Lalanā is by nature wisdom,
and Rasanā abides as skillful means.
Avadhūtī, in the center,
is utterly free of grasped and grasper.
Lalanā is the enjoyment body,
Rasanā is the emanation body,
and Avadhūtī is the dharmakāya;
thus they are explained as the three bodies.

All these channels
make the body wholesome.
From their assembly the body-mass arises,
and that mass is of the nature of the deity.
Transcending form, it becomes the mass;
transcending the mass, it is the deity itself.
Therefore, through inconceivable union,
the self that is suchness goes everywhere.
By whatever rite the practitioner
abides in the state beyond the mass,
through that he becomes of its very nature,
and so attains buddhahood itself.

This is the seventh chapter, the division of the stages of the means of the wheel of channels.

Next, I shall correctly explain,
in due order, the commitments
by merely knowing which
accomplishment comes swiftly.

In one's own home or a secret place,
secluded and delightful to the mind,
in mountain thickets and ravines,
on the shore of the great ocean,
in a house of the mātṛkās or a charnel ground,
or in the midst of a confluence of rivers,
one who desires the unsurpassed fruit
should properly draw the maṇḍala.
The patron, with great faith,
should invite the field-born, place-born, and mantra-born
yoginīs and the yogins,
the master, and all the deities.

The master may be a householder or a novice,
or likewise a monk;
some masters are worldly;
he may be a monk abiding in the teaching,
or someone endowed with good qualities,
or likewise one who has attained the higher knowledges.
Among these, the patron
should make chief whomever he has faith in.
With the master going first,
the virtuous maṇḍala is drawn;
the master who is empowered and endowed with good qualities,
who is not reviled by worldly people,
and who has entirely abandoned the ten nonvirtues,
should be made the chief of the feast gathering.

The intelligent patron should never
make one who is without compassion, angry and cruel,
arrogant, greedy, given to praising himself,
and not settled in meditative equipoise.
One who indulges in the enjoyments of household life,
a ploughman or a merchant,
or one who in delusion sells the sacred Dharma,
is not to be chief of the feast gathering.

Rather, one who possesses all good qualities,
who holds the banner of omniscience,
who is disciplined and truly endowed with diligence,
free of craving and free of pride,
always attentive to the service of sentient beings,
adorned with the appropriate ornaments,
delighting in ornaments of skulls,
and holding vajra and bell together:
that wise one should also seat
the left-hand partner firmly on his left side.
A master endowed with such qualities
is highly praised for all rites.

In due order, when the master
and the deities who were invited have arrived,
they are cleansed by washing their feet
with whatever scented water can be obtained.
Then, at the place where the ground has been thoroughly examined,
with the master going first,
they should enter according to the distinction of elder and younger
and take their places on the seats.

Those without good fortune and the proud,
likewise those who revile the guru,
those who have not seen the commitment, even one's own son,
and likewise such men and women:
the practitioner who desires accomplishment
should not admit them into that commitment.
If those who are like cattle enter,
accomplishment will come only after long delay.
Through disparaging the commitment, body and mind
suffer, and likewise one falls from one's station,
is kept far from glory,
and is tormented by sufferings of many kinds.
Knowing this, one should exclude them,
and gather those whose sphere is worship.
In the order of elder and younger,
with flowers, incense, lamps, and

and especially with the scent of sandalwood,
the intelligent one should always make worship.
The torma, adorned with parasols and banners,
should be carefully prepared by the master.
The deities are worshiped for the sake of propitiation,
and whatever rite of pacifying or enriching
the patron desires in his mind,
having drawn it for the sake of accomplishment,
one should consecrate that rite,
whichever is wished for.
Liquor of honey, liquor of sugar, and grain liquor,
whatever can be obtained, should be offered.

One who is pure, disciplined, and of heroic mind,
who has entirely abandoned craving and delusion,
and who regards all things as equal,
should be appointed as the action vajra.
Food to eat and food to chew, and likewise drink,
tooth-sticks, and likewise the offering fee,
should be presented by the patron
right in front of the maṇḍala.
Afterward the wise action vajra
distributes the substances.
First, the commitment substance
together with the hook should be enjoyed.
Then the master
consecrates everything in full.

"To all the sovereign ladies of the heroines
who dwell in the sites and the secondary sites,
in the fields, the meeting places, and the charnel grounds,
I bow down with respect.

The goddesses are the authority, the commitment is the authority,
and the speech they have taught has become the supreme authority;
by the power of that truth, may it become the cause
for the goddesses here to take me into their care."

Furthermore, before the maṇḍala,
joining the palms firmly at the heart,
the patron too should make aspiration prayers
as a dedication:

"You who have destroyed attachment to existence and to peace alike, and overcome conceptual thought,
you possess the vision of all things as having a nature equal to space.
Protector, with the water of your heart, moistened by the weight of the water of compassion,
act upon me; goddesses, I pray you, show me your deepest love.

Your mind is utterly pure through partaking of the yogic nectar of a single taste,
your body is utterly pure through traveling to the lands of the sites and the rest;
protector of the supreme wheel, the maṇḍala at the center of the glorious site,
to you, the supreme guru, I always bow my head with respect.

The body of all the heroes is ever beautiful,
adorned by the embrace of the precious bodies of the goddesses;
protector abiding in the wheel, of stainless innate joy,
heart of yoga, Saṃvara, to you too I always bow.

Buddha, omniscient one, abode of utter purity,
the goddesses are made beautiful by the abode of the innate;
guide, the source of innate joy,
to you, the stainless supreme innate one, I bow."

With these three verses one should rightly offer praise,
and bow down as well, as one pleases.
With the mind elated, as one pleases,
in the great delight of joyful clamor,
one should adorn the ground with garlands of flowers,
and worship the body with various flowers.
With the joy of the feast of liquor,
one should fill the place with vajra songs.
In supreme joy one should dance,
dancing the postures that are the signs of the sites,
and dancing with mudrās and mantras.
There one should sound pleasing music of many kinds,
with paṭaha drums and the like,
small hand drums, auspicious drums,
long drums, and so forth.

The heroes together with Heruka,
and the supreme yoginīs on the left:
after that, the chief of the feast gathering
should think of the virtue of the patron.
The assembled yogins and yoginīs
then in an instant give voice to auspicious words:
he will possess perfect happiness,
freedom from illness, and a virtuous mind;
he will truly attain pleasure, liberation, and the rest,
and his accomplishment will be perfect.

The elaborate maṇḍala
should be dissolved as the rite explains.
Having gathered up the remaining torma,
one gives it to the bhūtas who seek it.
With pleasant fragrances one satisfies
the hosts of yoginīs of the sites and the field-sites.
All the heroes who were invited
are requested to depart into great bliss.

This is the eighth chapter, the division of the stages of the rite of the signs of the commitments.

the signs of the left hand,
by merely knowing which
the practitioner swiftly gains accomplishment.

To one who shows a single finger,
show two in reply, meaning "welcome."
When someone closes the fingers of the left hand,
know it as the sign asking "are you well?"
To one who shows the ring finger,
one should show the little finger.
To one who shows the middle finger,
one should show the thumb.
To one who shows the forefinger,
one should clearly show the throat.
To one who shows the batisa,
one should show the trident.
To one who shows the breasts,
one should show the hairline.
To one who shows the teeth,
one should show the wheel.
To one who shows a wrathful frown,
one should show the crown knot.
To one who shows the forehead,
one should show a playful gesture.

A woman who approaches from the left,
a yoginī always from the left,
who speaks out in a loud voice,
who gazes with a glance to the left,
and speaks with delight to women:
know her to be one who holds the commitment.
When the women are entreated,
she clearly declares the seed syllable of her family,
does not abandon the activity of her family,
and recites the vidyā of her own family.
When someone inquires about the vidyā,
she puts her left hand on her own head
and tousles her hair,
and the practitioner especially
will then recall her own vidyā.
She points a finger at her cheek,
or at her throat, or at her nostrils,
and constantly, with a sidelong glance,
looks toward her own family.
The yoginī who goes about, truly arisen,
is hard to find and surely holds the commitment.
In her own house she draws a skull cup,
an axe, a sword, a banner,
a wheel, a yak-tail whisk, a conch,
and a trident, and delights in them;
she always delights in meat and liquor,
and she is one who destroys fear and shame.
She arises truly from the family of the ḍākinīs,
and is explained as the one called the Innate Lady.
She is born in land after land,
and practitioners always rely on her.

The sites and the secondary sites,
likewise the fields and the secondary fields,
the chandohas and the secondary chandohas,
the meeting places and the secondary meeting places,
the charnel grounds and the secondary charnel grounds:
these truly abide in Jambudvīpa.
The site is known as Pullīra,
and likewise Jālandhara is a site;
Oḍiyāna is likewise a site,
and likewise Arbuda too is a site.
The secondary sites are Godāvarī,
likewise the one by the name of Rāmeśvarī,
the one called Devīkoṭi,
and Mālava too is a secondary site.
A field is the one named Kāmarūpa,
and the one called Oḍi is also a field.
A secondary field is Triśaku,
and Kosala too is a secondary field.
Kaliṅga and Lampāka
are likewise chandohas;
Kāñci and Himālaya
are, in particular, the secondary chandohas.
The meeting places are Pretavāsī
and likewise Gṛhadevatā;
Saurāṣṭra and Suvarṇadvīpa
are the two secondary meeting places.
Pāṭaliputra is a charnel ground,
and Sindhu is likewise a charnel ground;
Maru and Kulatā, these two,
are explained as the secondary charnel grounds.

Thus they are known as the outer sites;
the inner sites are said to be the body itself.
Here, in the form of the channels of one's own body,
they are well known by the names of the sites.
That form is the aspect of the deity,
and so it abides as the inner self.
Therefore this body, the abode of that mass,
is equal to all the buddhas.

The site is the ground Very Joyful (Pramuditā),
and likewise the secondary site is Stainless (Vimalā).
The field is the ground Luminous (Prabhākarī),
and the secondary field is Radiant (Arciṣmatī).
Know the chandoha as Manifest (Abhimukhī),
and the secondary chandoha as Hard to Conquer (Sudurjayā).
The meeting place is Gone Far (Dūraṃgamā),
and the secondary meeting place is known as Immovable (Acalā).
The charnel ground likewise is Good Intelligence (Sādhumatī),
and the secondary charnel ground is Cloud of Dharma (Dharmameghā).
Thus the purity of the grounds, of the sites and the rest,
has been explained in due order.
By relying on the sites, the secondary sites, and the rest,
people become free of stains.

The intelligent one, free of conceptual thought,
should wander and examine the signs carefully.
Having recognized the women of various forms and of terrifying forms,
fierce, and laughing in an unbearable way,
through the yoga of one's own chosen deity
one should proclaim the sound hūṃ.
Having entirely abandoned all doubt,
the practitioner should act like a lion.
By seeing, by touching, and even by hearing,
accomplishment will very swiftly come.

Next I shall properly explain
the worship of Vajrayoginī.
If one is unable to travel to the sacred places and so on,
one should worship the outer yoginīs.

one should first perform the approach and the rest.

endowed with bathing, cleanliness and the like,
one invites them with a mind of faith,
and in one's own home, having properly seated them
facing east, one worships the goddesses.
With saffron, eye ointment,
and likewise vermilion one anoints their heads;
their feet, hands and faces likewise
one anoints with unguents and worships them.
One hangs garlands of flowers on their heads
and perfumes them with fragrant incense.
One, three, four or five,
and especially seven or nine,
up to as many as twenty-four:
the patron, with steadfast faith,
with food, intoxicating drink,
fish, meat and the like,
having consecrated them as mudrās, worships them,
and the hero should show the signs.
The assembly of Vajravairocanī,
as having the nature of the deity, one always worships.

one offers five lamps and food,
and fully endowed with songs and praises
one worships the vajrayoginīs.

abundantly with intoxicants and meat,
and, devoted to the glorious Vajra Vehicle,
worships them with faith through a feast,
their hearts are pleased and they will bestow the supreme,
for the supreme rests in their hands.
Having worshiped the yoginīs,
one should examine the signs of pacifying and enriching:
in the instant of worshiping the maidens,
one examines the karmic results of virtue and nonvirtue.
If one of them rubs her two hands together,
her mind has not been pleased,
and so illnesses will increase
and suffering will be met with.
If at the moment of the worship
she lets out the sound of laughter,
freedom from illness will not be attained,
and death is beyond doubt.
If right upon the worship she weeps
and rubs her two eyes,
it is said one will die in an instant.

if pair by pair, each to each,
they turn their faces toward one another,
the maidens are always showing
that there will be quarrels everywhere.
If a yoginī looks behind her,
or bites her nails with her teeth,
she has not been satisfied with the meat dishes,
and the rite will have to be done again.
If they are not pleased by the feast
of all the food and drink,
sudden illness, from weapons and the like,
will arise at once.
If they sing and make music like this
and laugh again and again,
the wise should discern that these maidens
are showing dangers from kings and thieves.
If, having drunk the intoxicant, they dance
again and again in many ways,
great illness will come upon those concerned.

If they throw away all the food of the feast
and gaze about in the directions,
it is the fault of having let one's conduct lapse:
the deities are angered with oneself.
If they fall into conversation with one another,
or if they laugh at one another,
know that the maidens are showing
that the rite of the principal has lapsed.
If one vomits immediately after eating,
it shows that the disease of madness will arise.
If the beautiful one, as she leaves,
looks back behind her,
the rite will have to be done again,
and it shows illness arising after a long time.
If she rubs the ground with her foot,
it signifies a change of dwelling.
If she falls down at the time of the worship,
it shows that people will do battle with one another.
When it is free of these changes,
the worship should be taken as accomplished.
Therefore, having worshiped with diligence,
the practitioner should always examine.
Worship the vajrayoginīs:
they grant every desired result.

accomplishment is carried to completion:
the first flower of the woman renowned as a dyer
is highly praised.
If one finds a caṇḍāla woman who keeps the samaya,
one should always rely on her.
Meat, fat and likewise blood
the wise should take.
The practitioner who desires accomplishment
takes whatever of the five hooks he finds;
or, since here killing is taught as the chief means,
if he properly finds them, he takes them.
Cow meat and horse meat,
elephant meat likewise,
human meat and dog meat
the wise should take.
These, as Vajrasattva has taught,
are renowned as the five lamps.
Having found them, at the time of the tenth day
one properly gathers these substances.
Having mixed them with fragrant substances,
one combines them in great secrecy.
The practitioner, in secret,
properly forms them into pills.
At that very moment, with a complete array
of special food and drink,
having consecrated them in the midst of the gathering,
one then properly begins the rites.
Mantra recitation and likewise meditative concentration
bring forth the fruit of every wish;
they grant rejuvenation through the body's essence
and the accomplishment of supreme liberation.
One who constantly relies on the pills
is granted the fruit of every wish.
If they are applied to all the rites,
the state of the sky-goers will be attained.
This is the sixteenth chapter, the definitive teaching on the accomplishment of the five nectars.

Next I shall properly explain
the supreme rite of drawing the maṇḍala.
Thus, when someone makes a request,
or when one wishes for merit oneself,
first, abiding in the circle
whose nature is one's own deity, one performs the prior approach.
Having gained mastery through the prior approach,
one should then give torma there.
One who is steadfast and knows the profound Dharma,
who has gone to the far shore of consecration and torma,
who knows the reality of the fire offering and the maṇḍala,
who is learned in all the fields of knowledge,
who has faith and devotion toward the way of secret mantra,
who is of fine form and lovely to behold,
and who has respect and love for the guru:
to such a one the Saṃvarodaya is to be explained.
In a temple, at the site of a stūpa,
in a dwelling, an assembly hall, or a clean place,
or in a charnel ground that has existed from the beginning:
there one should begin the maṇḍala.
On ground where the rite has been performed before,
one does not dig and so forth;
touching it with the hand,
one purifies the ground by reciting the syllable hūṃ as mantra.
One should purify an area of ground
twice the size of the maṇḍala's portion.
When one's own mind is completely pure,
by that very fact the ground becomes pure.
The master, the nature of all the buddhas,
whose identity is the body of the deity,
should supplicate the hero who holds vajra and bell,
together with the ḍākinīs.
The intelligent one, whirling the vajra
and diligently ringing the bell,
expels the gods, the asuras, the guhyakas,
and the hosts of extremely vicious obstructors:
"I, through the power of glorious compassion,
by fully applying the wheel of protection,
whatever you may be, kaṭapūtanas and the rest,
hosts of obstructors, go elsewhere!
If, when I have commanded, anyone should transgress,
by the body of the Vajra Lord
arisen from the three bodies he will be split apart;
here he will be destroyed, and nothing else."
Having taken possession of the ground,
one should bind the boundary with a fence.
With the threatening mudrā of the right hand,
one praises the earth goddess Pṛthivī, arisen from the syllable baṃ,
yellow in color and holding a golden vase,
and earnestly urges her:
"I, named so-and-so, am drawing the maṇḍala;
goddess, it is by your leave."
One worships her with flowers, incense and the rest,
and the wise one, having presented the offering water,
supplicates the tathāgatas,
the Blessed One, the vajra holder worshiped by the wrathful ones:
"Perfect buddhas, please consider me!
Out of love for the disciples,
and in order to worship you as well,
O Protector, I wish to draw
the maṇḍala of the arising of the innate.
Therefore, Blessed One, I pray you,
grant your kindness to me who am devoted.
Whatever other secret mantra deities there are,
and the deities who protect the world,
and the bhūtas who rely on perfect awakening
and rejoice in the teaching,
all of you who possess the vajra eye:
since I am drawing the maṇḍala,
the maṇḍala of the Saṃvarodaya,
I pray you, generate loving care
for me and likewise for the disciples."
The thread, endowed with the five wisdoms
and divided into twenty-five strands,
is to be twisted together, strand with strand,
as the essential nature of all phenomena.
The practitioner, uttering the syllable hūṃ,
should anoint it with the five nectars.
In length it is twice the circle,
in thickness a twentieth part of the gate.
Having said jaḥ three times, at the center
one holds it with the left fist,
and the intelligent one strikes the line in the sky,
and likewise strikes it on the ground.
With a thread that is clear and beautiful,
of proper measure and clean,
the one with wisdom should strike the lines
of the glorious maṇḍala of the arising of the innate.
Beginning from half a cubit
up to as much as a hundred cubits:
first are the lines of Brahmā,
and second one strikes the diagonal lines.
In the positions of the west and the south
the master properly stands, without fail;
in the positions of the eastern and northern directions
the disciple stands, in equipoise.
First, in a fourfold measure,
one strikes the lines of one square.
Then, in an eightfold measure,
one strikes well the lines of one square.
Again, in an eightfold measure,
one strikes the lines of one square.
Then again, in a twofold measure,
one strikes well the lines of one square.
Then, in a fourfold measure,
one strikes the lines of one square.
Again, in a twofold measure,
one strikes well the lines of one square.
Then the wise one, in a fourfold measure,
strikes the lines of one square.
Then, in a twofold measure,
one strikes the lines of two squares.
Then one draws the half in two tiers.
This is the laying of the maṇḍala's lines.
The marks of the line cord are sixty-four:
these are the characteristics of the maṇḍala's lines.
Up to the very outer edge of the circle
one strikes the lines as the rite has taught.
With powder of the five precious substances,
or else with rice and the like,
white, yellow and likewise red,
green and likewise black,
the master goes to the direction of Īśāna,
and there, in equipoise,
with the well-concealed left fist
lays out the five strips of color.
Between each of them one draws
a gap the width of a single barley grain.
If the strips are thick, there will be illness;
if thin, wealth will decline;
if crooked, there will be quarrels here;
if broken, death will occur.
In the eastern direction is a great white;
the south is endowed with yellow;
the western portion is red;
the north is endowed with emerald;
and the portion of ground in the center
blazes with the light of sapphire.

At every portion of the corners
and at the junctures of the door projections,
in equipoise, one should properly draw them,
adorned with vajras and with jewels.
Within the vajra fence
it is adorned with the eight charnel grounds:
Caṇḍogra, and likewise Gahvara,
Vajrajvala, and Karaṅkin;
in Īśāna, Aṭṭahāsa;
in Agni, Lakṣmīvana;
in Nairṛti, Ghorāndhakāra;
in the direction of Vāyu, Kilikilārava.
In the east the śirīṣa and the aśvattha tree,
the kaṅkela, and especially the cūta tree,
the paṭā, and likewise the karañja,
the latāparka, and the pārthiva.
Indra, and likewise Dhanada (Kubera),
the lord of nāgas (Varuṇa), and the lord Yama,
then Īśāna and Agni,
the lord of rākṣasas, and the lord of wind (Vāyu).
Vāsuki and Takṣaka,
Karkoṭa and Padma,
Mahāpadma, Huluhulu,
Kulika, and Śaṅkhapāla.
Thundering and Great Thundering,
Fierce, Moving, and likewise Steady,
Filling, and likewise Pouring:
these fierce ones are the lords of the clouds.
And besides, many of various kinds:
crows, owls, and vultures,
jackals and she-jackals,
foxes and she-foxes,
lion-faced and tiger-faced ones,
females with fierce and terrifying faces,
snakes with the heads of cattle,
two-faced ones and others roaming in the directions;
skeletons, and bodies impaled on stakes,
hanging bodies, half-burned bodies, and heads,
skulls, shinbones, headless trunks,
heads with bared teeth, and bones;
the various accomplished vidyādharas,
the yogins who practice the conduct of the pledges,
the hosts of yoginīs,
yakṣas, vetālas, rākṣasas and the rest,
the lords of the great cry of kili kili,
and the hosts of masters who have fully
attained accomplishment and miraculous power:
these are to be visualized in the midst of the charnel grounds.

This is the seventeenth chapter, the section teaching the characteristics of laying out the lines of the maṇḍala.

Next, the characteristics of the master
I shall properly explain.
He has a peaceful and disciplined bearing
and grants fearlessness to all sentient beings.
He knows the application of mantra and tantra,
is learned in the treatises, and is endowed with compassion.
He regards all sentient beings as his children
and speaks pleasantly in every situation.
He always delights in generosity and the rest
and strives intently in meditative concentration and yoga.
He harms no one and speaks the truth;
he has compassion and a mind intent on benefit;
he has an impartial mind and is endowed with the mudrā.
He has become the protector of sentient beings
and knows the particular dispositions of sentient beings;
he is the friend of those who have no protector.
His body has all its faculties complete;
he is well formed and beautiful to behold.
He knows the reality of the meaning of empowerment;
his words are clear, and he is a great ocean of good qualities;
he is always and continually steadfast.
Such a one should be recognized as a master.

Disciples of good family who delight in the Dharma
the master should properly gather.
Those without compassion, wrathful and malicious,
arrogant, greedy, attached, and distracted,
violent, deluded, and indolent,
without tenderness for the lives of others,
and covetous of the wealth of others,
the guru should always reject.
Those who are steady, disciplined, and intelligent,
patient, upright, and without deceit or guile,
who have entirely given up the ten nonvirtues,
who look upon sentient beings with loving-kindness,
who, as if it were blazing fire or poison,
do not touch the wealth of others,
who always and continually honor the guru,
who are eager to behold the holy Dharma,
who always delight in generosity and the rest,
and who long for the world beyond:
these are commended as disciples,
and the virtuous maṇḍala should be shown to them.
Joining their palms together,
they should petition with a respectful mind:
"Great hero in union with the supreme yoginī,
you, my teacher,
have shown the way of great awakening.
Great protector, I beseech you:
grant me that very pledge;
grant me also the mind of awakening.
The empowerment of the heroes and heroines,
and likewise of Vārāhī and Heruka,
fill the secret place and the rest completely,
and please explain the arrangement upon the body.
The Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha,
the three refuges, grant to me as well.
Into the supreme city of great liberation,
O protector, please make me enter."
"Come, my child: of the Great Vehicle
you are a vessel of the great way.
The ritual of the conduct of secret mantra
I shall properly explain to you.
Through the power of the vajra secret mantra
one truly attains incomparable wisdom.
Therefore, to attain omniscience,
my child, form this resolve:
honor the guru, and likewise be loving;
be devoted to the buddhas and tender toward beings;
give up the root transgressions entirely,
and give up the gross transgressions as well.
You should attend to sentient beings;
do not long for the Lesser Vehicle.
Carry across those who have not crossed;
liberate those who are not liberated;
bring relief to the sentient beings
tormented by the suffering of saṃsāra."
The disciples who properly possess these trainings
should undergo the preparatory rite.
In accordance with the ritual, they should be bathed
and purified with fumigation, and a tooth stick given to them.
In particular, with a red thread
tied on the upper arm, they are always protected.
Kuśa grass over which the mantra of the syllable dhī has been recited
should then be given to them,
and their dreams, virtuous and nonvirtuous, examined.
The vows of body, speech, and mind are also to be given.
Having had them take loose flowers
together with the offering gift,
and then tying on a blindfold there,
one should lead them into the maṇḍala.
The negative deeds amassed in former births
are purified by seeing the maṇḍala.
One should ask, "O, who are you?"
and they reply, "I am the fortunate one."
The pledge water, the oath,
and the casting of the flower onto the maṇḍala:
wherever the flower falls,
to that family they will belong.
The water, the crown, the vajra,
the bell, and the name empowerments;
the empowerment embodying the five tathāgatas;
the observance, and the prediction;
the permission and the encouragement;
and the irreversible empowerment too should be given;
the parasol, the seat, the consort, and the rest,
complete with vajra, bell, and the rest:
the master empowerment, entirely complete,
is the one that arises from the vase.

The second is the supreme secret;
the third is the wisdom of insight;
the fourth, likewise, is just so.
One who properly possesses these empowerments
should be known as a holder of the pledges.
"Having entered supremely into the maṇḍala
of the supreme secret and beheld it,
today you are completely freed
from all negative deeds, and abide as an excellent one.
The permission and the supreme encouragement
spoken in unison by all the buddhas,
the accomplishments, the pledges, and the vows:
these you must always guard."
The disciple, through the power of faith,
having bowed at the feet of the guru,
should say, "Just as the Lord has commanded,
so shall I do."
Then, as the Tathāgata has taught,
various ornaments, garments, and the like,
and in particular one's own body,
are to be offered to the guru as the offering gift.
Having obtained the principal empowerment,
with the great mind of one who has done what was to be done,
one should, moreover, say this with great joy,
and so bring the conclusion to completion:
"Today my life bears fruit;
my living is fruitful.
Today I am born into the family of the buddhas;
I have become a child of the buddhas."
One should complete it with a fire offering,
and invite the Saṅgha to a feast.
Then one should hold a feast gathering,
and give also to the sick and to those without protector.
Afterward, in accordance with the instructions,
by striving in the conduct of the pledges,
with one's continuum made a vessel,
through the stages of meditating on the wheel and the rest,
endowed with the perfect, true instructions,
accomplishment will come, and not otherwise.

This is the eighteenth chapter, the section on the stages of empowerment.

Next, the characteristics of certain death
I shall explain supremely.
The omens, in one's own body or outside it,
should be examined by the wise.
If one feels as if pierced in the soles of the feet,
and when one comes to feel as if pierced at the navel,
then, beyond three days
and before five, one will depart.
If at the times of defecation and urination
a sneeze occurs at the same moment,
then within one year, at that very time
of day, one will die and perish.
If, while the two organs are joined in union,
in the middle and at the end a sneeze
occurs at the same moment, then
one will certainly die within a month.
If at the heart and the middle of the throat
a stabbing pain arises at the same moment,
then, if one does not rely on the Dharma,
one will die within a month and a half.
If the image of the pupil of the left eye
is not seen in a mirror,
then, if one does not avert it,
one will certainly die within seven days.
The roots of the ears, the middle of the brows,
and the tip of the crown of the head: if these four
are pierced with pain at the same moment,
then one will die immediately.
If one suddenly feels it,
and is disturbed by emaciation, anger, and fear,
whoever does not rely on the Dharma
will die within one year.
If on the first day of the waxing fortnight
one sees one's semen to be black,
then one will die within six months;
red shows that one will be struck by illness.
Constant dripping from the eyes,
seeing forms distorted,
whoever does not see his own shadow
in a mirror or in water,
seeing a rainbow at night,
seeing a circle of stars by day,
seeing flashes of lightning in the south
when there are no clouds,
seeing a path of shadow by day,
and likewise seeing meteors fall,
seeing geese, crows, and peacocks
mingled together as one,
two moons and two suns,
and likewise one's own head blazing,
seeing a city of the gandharvas
on the tops of trees and the peaks of mountains,
seeing terrifying pretas and piśācas
and other beings as well,
trembling violently all of a sudden,
and fainting again and again:
if one sees even a single one of these,
one will die within one month.
The moon without its hare mark,
the sun devoid of light,
the sun at night, the moon by day,
and likewise one's own eyes blazing,
stars the size of Mount Meru,
appearing like oceans and rivers,
and semen, feces, and urine
discharged at the same moment:
then, if one does not rely on the Dharma,
one will die within half a month.
There, too, by day, one's shadow
should be looked at as having a person's form;
if its head is not seen,
one will die within one year.
If the left hand is not seen,
one's son and wife will certainly perish.
If the right hand is not seen,
father, son, and the rest will die with great hardship.
If the urine comes in five streams,
swirling round from the left and foul smelling,
or if sour urine and the like comes forth,
one will die within six months.
Whoever, at the end of a dream,
climbs a heap of sand or of ashes,
or a temple or a stūpa,
will die as described before.
If, riding a donkey or a monkey,
one climbs an anthill or a rubbish heap
and goes toward the south,
whoever sees this at the end of a dream;
a woman clad in black,
dark in color, making love with the man:
she is called Kālarātri,
and he will go into the belly of Yama.
If one sees in a dream that one is eaten
by vultures, jackals, dogs, and crows,
by bears, pretas, and piśācas,
one will certainly die within one year.
If one sees in a dream that one is covered with red clothing,
adorned with a red garland,
and anointed with sesame oil,
one will not live six months.
One endowed with the instructions as they are
will ransom death,
and by that very means will conquer death.
It is through the Dharma that one conquers death,
by accomplishing the stages of perfect awakening;
therefore think of the Dharma as supreme.

Furthermore, the special features of the meditation
shall be properly and beautifully explained.
When the breath is cut off for an instant
and various omens are truly seen,
by the practice of scattering and filling
one should purify the maṇḍala of the body.
When the time of death has truly come,
one should perform the supreme yoga of transference.
The channels that reach the nine openings
should be filled by inhalation (pūraka);
the breath should be pressed down by exhalation (recaka);
it should be bound by retention (kumbhaka);
and the doors of the openings should be purified.
Through utter pacification it becomes peaceful.
The practice of drawing out consciousness,
and, further, for traveling to the beyond,
the wise one should apply it
with the vowels and consonants joined together.
Placing the syllable hūṃ at the heart,
the one and a half syllables
should be placed below and above.
In the portion beneath it is the seed syllable
of wind, facing downward.
Making the seed syllable of wind twofold,
the one possessed of yoga should join them together.
In a sequence of twenty-one,
one recites the mantra of one and a half syllables.
The mount of consciousness is wind;
if one visualizes the door of the wind
through which it is to travel,
it grants the accomplishment of liberation.

The distinction between the highest and the lowest
I shall explain: listen, Lord of Secrets.
Through the navel one goes to a happy destiny in the desire realm;
from the drop, to a body possessing form;
from above, into the formless realm:
these are what lead to happy destinies.
From the nose one becomes one of the yakṣas;
from the ears, a kinnara;
if, O Goddess, from the eyes,
one becomes a king among humans.
From the door of the mouth, the pretas;
from the urinary passage, likewise, the animals;
from the anal passage one goes to hell;
from liberation one goes elsewhere.
When the time has come, one should perform transference;
out of time, one would be killing the gods.
Merely through having killed the gods,
a person will be cooked in hell.
Therefore the wise should properly know
the signs of death.

This is the nineteenth chapter, the section on the stages of observing the omens of death and the yoga of transference.

Next, therefore, the measure of the four ages
I shall properly explain,
by merely understanding which
one quickly comes to know the eons.
Twenty thousand and eight thousand,
one million and seven hundred thousand:
these are declared to be renowned
as the eon of the Perfect Age (Kṛta Yuga).
At the juncture of the Perfect Age and the Tretā Age
there are, likewise, eight thousand.
One million, two hundred thousand,
ninety thousand and six thousand:
this is renowned as the Tretā eon;
so the wise should understand it.
At the juncture between the Tretā and the Dvāpara
six thousand should be understood.
Sixty thousand and four thousand
and eight hundred thousand make the Dvāpara;
gathered from all the treatises,
this is properly renowned as the Dvāpara eon.
At the juncture of the Dvāpara and the Age of Strife
there are, likewise, four thousand.
Thirty thousand and two thousand
and four hundred thousand should be known as the Age of Strife (Kali Yuga).
At the juncture of the Age of Strife and the Perfect Age
there are four thousand.
These are renowned as the ages;
there is no doubt of accomplishment here.
"The nature of the four continents,
O Protector, please explain."
Eastern Videha, Kuru,
and likewise western Godānīya:
these three continents
abide as lands of the fruition.
Those well born in Jambudvīpa
should be known to be in the land of accomplishment.
For birth in the three continents
there abides merit from which fruition arises;
the people of Jambudvīpa
accomplish liberation and accomplishment itself.
These should be understood as the continents
taught by the buddhas of the past.

This is the twentieth chapter, the section on the stages of the definitive teaching on the four ages.

Next, the supreme practice
that has reached the far shore I shall properly explain,
by which the practitioner, through the very cause of accomplishment,
reaches the culmination of accomplishment.
In the common yoga tantras
the secret is not explained:
the supreme accomplishment among accomplishments,
the supreme observance among observances.
Having attended upon and served a holy guru,
and having heard much of the unsurpassed tantras,
having heard reality just as it is
from the mouth of the guru, one should always meditate.
Wealth, wife, and likewise life
should be given up as a gift.
If one is freed from these three knots,
one will always practice the conduct.
With great enthusiasm one recites the vidyā,
and likewise delights in speaking the truth.
One abides firmly in what one has vowed,
as one began it earlier with faith.
Desire, anger, and fear,
craving, delusion, and pride should be given up.
Examining disciples, and likewise teaching,
and compiling texts, one should always give up.
Pure and impure, purification,
drinkable and undrinkable, one should not discriminate.
Anger at blame and praise
and manifest pride one should entirely give up.
Always without attachment and without elation,
one abides in equanimity toward all.
There is no fire offering and no worship,
no rosary and no mantra recitation;
the days of the week, the planets, the stars,
and the dates of the month as well, one should not consider.
In a form whose nature is self and other,
one should abide without doubt.
One should not act without desire;
toward all, one acts with a little desire.
Making a tiger skin one's garment,
adorned with the five mudrās,
the yogin whose nature is wisdom and means
should meditate on glorious Heruka.
Through the conduct of Samantabhadra
the mind should abide in bliss.
One should stay a single night in a village
and stay five in a town.
Through practice that accords with the mind,
one should dwell upon the ground.
Or else one practices, with bliss and joy,
the conduct called "the wind."
One always roams without companions,
alone, with one-pointed mind;
relying on the observance of a madman,
one wanders like a leaf blown by the wind.
In a charnel ground, at a lone liṅga,
at a solitary tree, or in a forest,
on a mountain peak, or on the bank of a river,
or on the shore of the great ocean,
in a pleasure garden or a mountain cave,
in a mansion or an empty house,
at a crossroads, before a gate,
and likewise at the gate of a king's palace,
in the dwelling of caṇḍālas, or a cattle shed,
or a workshop, one dwells in concealment.
Garlands of flowers discarded in the marketplace,
any old flowers whatsoever,
garlands of flowers from charnel grounds and lone liṅgas:
with these one makes offering to the body.
A garland of flowers at one's own throat,
and in particular a sacred thread
and a girdle are also to be tied on;
on the two feet one wears anklets.
Speech is known to be of the nature of mantra recitation;
the movement of the hands is mudrā.
Through the practice of nonconceptuality
the yogin abides as he pleases.
Giving up all apprehensions,
the practitioner should act like a lion.
Or else, relying on the unwavering observance,
one practices the conduct of yoga.
Dwelling in a pleasure garden or an empty house,
in the house of a lowly or wicked person in a village,
for as long as it takes to gain power,
one should abide in the practice of silence.
However one sleeps, walks, or sits,
whether awake or again awakening,
eating merely whatever is obtained,
without hunger, one abides well.
If one lives by begging alms,
one abides eating from the vessel of the hands.
Through the intrinsic nature of nonconceptuality
there is no doubt one will accomplish it here.
Among these three,
relying on whichever observance one wishes,
and having gained a little power,
one practices the conduct as one wishes.
Having given away one's body as a gift,
afterward one properly undertakes the conduct.
The yogin who roams by means of this conduct
will certainly become stainless.
One should not be mistaken about this:
the miraculous power of the buddhas is inconceivable.

This is the twenty-first chapter, the section on the stages of the definitive teaching on conduct.

Next, I shall explain something further:
the definitive account of the yoga tantras
and the extent of the yoginī tantras.
I shall explain it: listen, Lord of Secrets.
The extent of the yoga tantras
is definitely six koṭis;
likewise the yoginī tantras are renowned
to number sixteen koṭis.
The sūtra collections other than the Great Vehicle
are taught to be eight hundred million.
Likewise, the way of the perfection of wisdom
is a million and five hundred million.
All of these were spoken by the Lord of Sages,
whose nature is the three bodies;
they were taught by the Sugata.
All the topics of the Vinaya and the rest
were taught for the śrāvakas themselves.
The great explanations of the various sūtra collections
are the teaching for bodhisattvas.
The tantras of yoga and of yoginīs
are the secret, the domain of the buddhas.
So that, through the yoga whose nature is wisdom and means,
by meditating on great bliss,
one may swiftly attain buddhahood
in the Dharma, enjoyment, and emanation bodies,
they were spoken, for sentient beings of meager merit,
by the one whose nature is the deity.

and all the fierce and frightening forms
should be drawn at ten parts.
One's own form, which delights the mind,
should be drawn at nine parts.
The deities of the maṇḍala wheel
should be arranged one after another in proper order.
The vajra king in the center
should be drawn with a single set of characteristics.
If the three, the elbows, the neck and the shanks,
are too long, it is a great fault:
the mantrin himself will surely
fall from his position and be driven out.
If the three, the nose, the ears and the fingers,
are too short, it is a great fault:
accomplishments will surely be destroyed,
and likewise fortune will decline.
If the three, the shanks, the face and the cheeks,
are too thick, it is a great fault:
the practitioner himself will surely
be far from glory, and māras and obstructors will arise.
If the three, the jaw, the chest and the flanks,
are sunken, it is a great fault:
one's aims will decline,
and obstructors will arise here without doubt.
If the three, the breasts, the ears and the forehead,
are crooked, it is a great fault:
the practitioner himself will quickly
be tormented by the quarrels of enemies.
If the three, the thighs, the waist and the two armpits,
are deficient, it is a great fault:
there will be danger from bandits and thieves,
and death, without doubt.
If the ears, the lips, the forehead
and the nose are deficient and the eyes are crooked,
the mantrin himself will surely
meet with various obstructors and die.
If the figure gazes upward, as if with agitated breath,
or leans from its seat as though it were moving,
one will always be shifted from one's own place,
and one's aims will be destroyed.
The faults of the body, in the neck, the armpits and so on,
are known to be countless.
If here the eyes are crooked,
or the mudrās and hand emblems are mistaken,
one will be sorely tormented by grief and suffering.
Various faults like these should be examined.
If the three, the backrest, the parasol and the seat,
have the fault of being small and cramped,
friends will speak unpleasant words,
fortune will decline, and death will follow.
These faults are great faults;
the wise should examine them well.
One who is disciplined, who knows all the crafts,
who has seen the rite and rests in meditative equipoise,
should draw with all the characteristics complete
and with beautiful forms.
For the peaceful, a body of peaceful manner;
for the fierce, fierce and terrifying;
for the heroic, heroic and with a proud body;
for the graceful, a smiling face.
Having marked out the characteristics in this way,
the mantrins should draw the painted images.
The mantrin who is a beginner
should make images and the like;
if one practices with a painted image,
one will swiftly attain vast accomplishment.

This is the thirtieth chapter, the stage-by-stage classification of the definitive teaching on the characteristics of images such as paintings.

Next I shall properly and fully explain
the auspicious characteristics of the yoginīs.
Ḍākinī is Padminī;
Lāmā is Hastinī;
Khaṇḍarohā is Śaṅkhinī;
Rūpiṇī is Citriṇī.
The wise should examine
these four types in their own nature.
I shall explain the characteristics of Padminī.
Her face is round like a disc;
her nose is like a sesame flower;
her nails are like copper, her feet arched like a tortoise's back,
and the soles of her feet rest evenly.
Her breasts are like the fruit of the palmyra palm;
likewise her body hair is curled,
and her supreme place of birth has three folds.
Her chest, too, is very beautiful.
Her gait is like that of an elephant in rut;
she has the fragrance of the lotus and the voice of a goose,
and her touch is like a lotus.
One should enjoy her as one desires in the lotus posture.
Seizing her by the hair and the breasts,
one should press her lips with one's teeth,
and insert a finger into the bhaga; in this way
one should make love to the lotus.
Next I shall likewise properly explain
the characteristics of Hastinī.
She smells of liquor and has thick shanks,
a crooked nose and curled body hair;
she is intoxicated with desire and full of craving,
stout of body and untamed.
One should sport with her
in the uraṭa posture.
Hastinī is round in shape.
One should strike her head with one's fingers,
embrace her fiercely and knead her breasts,
touch her mouth and bite her,
and the skilled one should draw his nails across her.
Hastinī laughs with a roar
and delights in song and music.
One who properly possesses these characteristics
should be known as Hastinī.
Śaṅkhinī, too, I shall explain.
Her hair is long and her nose is long;
she is neither too thin nor too stout;
her breasts are like orange fruits,
and she delights in feasts of curd and milk.
By seizing her hair with the left hand
one should give rise to joy,
and press her lips with one's teeth.
With great joy, fiercely,
one should scratch her loins and her heart with the nails.
She has the smell of a donkey,
is rough like a cow's tongue,
and Śaṅkhinī has the voice of a crow.
One who properly possesses these characteristics
is always called Śaṅkhinī.
Next Citriṇī, too, I shall explain.
Her body is short and her chest beautiful;
her breasts are like bilva fruits;
she is very wrathful and without shame,
always delighting in quarrels;
her shanks are slender, and she lies on her back;
her lips droop, and her voice is a dove's;
she has the smell of flesh,
and Citriṇī's shoulders are full.
I shall explain the play of joy with her.
First one presses the bhaga with the hand,
kisses her and kneads her breasts,
strikes her head with one's fingers,
delights her by drawing the body together,
and embraces her fiercely.
One should let her suck one's lips.
One who properly possesses these characteristics,
explained in metrical verses,
is Citriṇī, who is Rūpiṇī.
Next I shall properly explain, further,
the characteristics of the divisions of the shifting.
The outer shifting is coarse;
the inner nature is said to be subtle.
At the crown is the cakra of great bliss,
a lotus with four petals,
the subtle seat of the awakening mind.
Since it is the form on which all things rest,
it is the very nature of the essence of awakening.
Outside what has become the seed
is a lotus with thirty-two petals.
At its center the letter hā,
facing downward, lets bliss trickle down.
The moon, whose nature is the awakening mind,
has the nature of fifteen digits;
it always flows down as great bliss.
The yoginī is of sixteen digits.
On its two sides
are lalanā and rasanā,
in their own nature the vowels and consonants;
by the nature of cause and result,

she who has the form of the four joys,
whose nature is coemergent joy,
the supreme sovereign lady of nonduality,
conventionally like the kunda flower,
ultimately has the form of bliss.
The support of the awakening mind of the buddhas
is Vajradhara.
At the throat is the cakra of enjoyment,
red, with sixteen petals;
at its center is the letter oṃ.
Above it, by way of the opening
of the uvula, nectar
flows down without interruption.
At the heart is the cakra of Dharma,
a multicolored lotus with eight petals;
at its center is the letter hūṃ,
which abides facing downward.
Above it is a subtle lotus,
resembling the abode of Brahmā.
The consciousness at its center
always dawns, pervading all;
it is the support of self-arisen wisdom,
the supreme lord of consciousness.
At the navel is a lotus, blue in color,
with sixty-four petals;
at its center is the letter oṃ,
blazing like a jewel.
Below it a subtle lotus
should be placed at the site of the knot;
it is said to be the support and the root
of the seventy-two thousand channels.
Lalanā has the nature of wisdom;
method abides as rasanā.
The goddess who has entered between them,
the letter oṃ, is she of manifold form,
the goddess whose nature is the four bodies,
who bestows all accomplishments
and bestows great bliss on all:
to her I always truly bow.
To whatever thing
people apply their minds,
by that the goddess becomes its very nature,
she of manifold form, like a wish-fulfilling jewel.
Stirred by the fire of wisdom, when the things that are objects are analyzed, they are free of any continuum.
As Caṇḍikā blazes and radiates, stainlessness itself is truly known and clear.
The conceptions of the aggregates are burned, and objectless self-awareness trickles down,
and the nectar of the equality of all things pervades space and possesses the true state.
Next I shall properly explain further
the shifting of the form of the bindu.
From the first day of the waxing fortnight
up to the full moon:
on the first day of the waxing fortnight,
in the toes, the letter ā;
on the second day, in the shanks, the letter ā;
on the third day, in the thighs, the letter i;
on the fourth, in the place of birth, the letter ī;
on the fifth, in the navel, the letter u;
on the sixth, in the heart, the letter ū;
on the seventh, in the breasts, the letter ṝ;
on the eighth, in the throat, the letter ṝ;
on the ninth, in the palms, the letter ḷ;
on the tenth, in the cheeks, the letter ḹ;
on the eleventh, in the eyes, the letter e;
on the twelfth, at the tip of the nose, the letter ai;
on the thirteenth, in the forehead, the letter o;
on the fourteenth, at the fontanelle, the letter au;
on the full moon, at the crown, right and left,
is the very nature of the letters aṃ and aḥ.
From the first day of the waning fortnight
up to the new moon,
it shifts in that same way.
On the left is āli, the moon,
whose nature is subtle;
on the right is kāli, the sun,
whose nature is coarse.
By the divisions of the awakening mind
there are likewise sixteen shiftings.
By the divisions of the half-watches of activity
sixteen shiftings are taught.
The seizing of the moon and the seizing of the sun,
the cessation of the bindu and the cessation of space:
properly together with these,
the sixteen shiftings should be known.
Non-conceptual great bliss
is the form of the wisdom one longs for.
This is like the threshold and the steps of the door
to the house of joy and bliss.

This is the thirty-first chapter, the stage-by-stage classification of the teaching on the four yoginīs, the sequence of the four cakras, and the shifting of the awakening mind.

Next I shall fully and clearly explain,
just as it is, the reality of the torma.
For the maṇḍala, the fire offering, mantra recitation,
consecration, pacifying and enriching,
subjugating, expelling and killing,
the accomplishment of bhūtas and yakṣiṇīs,
and the times of practice at the sacred sites,
it is supreme in all rites.
Without liquor and torma
the rites will not be swiftly accomplished.
Therefore the torma is highly praised;
the buddhas of the past have taught it.
First, endowed with the yoga
of the form of the deity, two-armed Saṃvara,
one should properly protect with the two protections
and proclaim the sound of hūṃ.
With the twenty-four sacred sites
one should adorn the limbs of the body.
Through the yoga of the instantaneous form,
in an instant one recites the secret mantra.

First, set out the food, the drink, the feast and so on properly in front of oneself, and purify them. In front, from the letter yaṃ, there is a wind maṇḍala; above it, a fire maṇḍala arisen from the letter raṃ; on that, a lotus vessel, a skull cup arisen from a white letter a, resting on a hearth of three skulls. Upon the food and so on in its center, bless as complete the forms of the five nectars and the five lamps, arisen from the syllables oṃ aṃ traṃ khaṃ hūṃ and lāṃ māṃ pāṃ tāṃ bāṃ. Stirred by the wind, the fire blazes up, and by its boiling the seed syllables and the rest melt; view them as an essence like the rising sun. Above it, imagine a white khaṭvāṅga, the color of mercury, arisen from hūṃ, of the nature of nectar and pointing downward, melting completely; view it as having become of one taste with the essence. From the transformed vowels and consonants, the letters oṃ āḥ hūṃ come to abide stacked one upon another. From them the deities of the maṇḍala wheel are emanated and act for the benefit of beings; then, preceded by union, they melt and are absorbed into their respective seed syllables. By reciting the three syllables as many times as one wishes, one should bless the offering.

Knotting the index fingers, firmly join the middle fingers;
Join the thumbs together firmly as a vajra.
Placing that at the center of the forehead,
One should circle it, revolving it all around.
Pressing down with the feet and looking upward,
Proclaiming upward the sound pheṃ,
One summons the heroes and yoginīs
Who dwell in the world systems of the ten directions.

Then again one should circle the right and left hands, reciting three times samaya stvaṃ, samaya ha, samaya hoḥ, and circling and circling to the right and to the left. Preceded by complete release, in which there is no distinction between what is offered to and the one who offers, one should make offering to the deities with the supreme bliss of the net of ḍākinīs. In the east: indra saparivārānākarṣaya jaḥ. In the south: yama saparivārānākarṣaya jaḥ. In the west: varuṇa saparivārānākarṣaya jaḥ. In the north: kubera saparivārānākarṣaya jaḥ. In the quarter of Īśāna: mahābhūta saparivārānākarṣaya jaḥ. In the quarter of Agni: agni saparivārānākarṣaya jaḥ. In the quarter of Nairṛti: rākṣasa saparivārānākarṣaya jaḥ. Having recited this once, one circles in due order the Blessed One together with his Wisdom consort, and the Ḍākinī and the others, up to and including Yamamathanī. Then one should make the offering, reciting this once, twice, three, four or five times: oṃ ā ralli hoḥ jaḥ hūṃ vaṃ hoḥ vajra ḍākinī samaya stvaṃ drīśya hoḥ

Then, presenting water for rinsing, tooth cleaner and so forth into their hands, one worships them properly, preceded by the circling of the hands in the lotus gesture, and praises them:

Through the sameness of existence and peace you have destroyed attachment and vanquished the conception of things as real; You possess the vision that sees all things as having a nature equal to space. Protector, with the water of your heart, moistened by the weight of the waters of compassion, Act on my behalf; goddesses, I pray, be exceedingly loving toward me.

With this one should receive the accomplishments.

Then, circling as before for the guardians of the directions of the eight charnel grounds and the others: oṃ kha kha khāhi khāhi sarva yakṣa rakṣasa bhūta preta piśāconmādāpasmāra ḍāka ḍākinyādaya imaṃ baliṃ gṛhṇaṃtu samaya rakṣantu mama sarvva siddhimme prayacchantu yathaivaṃ yatheṣṭaṃ bhuñjatha pibatha jighratha mātikramatha mama sarvva kāryāṃ satsukhaṃ viśuddhaye sahāyikā bhavantu hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ phaṭ svāhā

Having praised them properly with the mantra of eight lines, one should receive the accomplishment one desires, and one should display the signs with the sign gestures of the hands. Reciting oṃ yoga śuddhāḥ sarvva dharmmā yoga śuddho'haṃ, one should please them with the mudrā of the circling lotus.

Preceded by an embrace that does not break apart the mudrā of withdrawal, one should snap the fingers with the thumb and ring finger:

oṃ You have accomplished all the aims of beings
And granted accomplishments in accord with them.
Though you now depart to the buddha realm,
Please abide in bliss, just as you please.

Reciting oṃ vajra muḥ, one should request them to depart. All the deities of the maṇḍala should be absorbed into oneself.

After that, by gathering up the leftover torma, one should show the accomplishing of the sacred commitment:

Filling the mouth with intoxicating liquor,
One should bind the blazing mudrā.
With the fierce sound of hūṃ,
One should give it into the vessel for the leftovers.
Having pleased them by giving flowers, incense and so forth,
One should recite the mantra:
oṃ sarvva kha kha khāhi khāhi ucchiṣṭa baliṃ ucchiṣṭa bhakṣakebhyaḥ svāhā
Endowed with the moods of compassion and the rest,
One should likewise sing the auspicious song.
The aim in every aspect is completely pure,
The great bliss that is without aspects;
The practitioner, whose nature is wisdom and skillful means,
Will accomplish every one of the rites.
If one has the means, each single day,
Or likewise each month or each year,
The form of the mind of perfect awakening
Should be fully dedicated into suchness.

Through generosity and all the rest, he completely fulfills the supreme wishes of beings' minds;
Glorious Heruka, the Ḍākinī and the rest have crushed to dust all the cities of conceptual delusion;
The guru who has fully turned the wheel, the source of the wisdom of the Victors, bestows the ultimate:
That is the ever swift auspiciousness of the noble ones, who are ruled by great compassion.

This is the thirty-second chapter, setting forth the torma offering.

Now I shall explain the vow of accomplishments
Arising from primordial wisdom.
The methods in their various modes
Are the supreme cause of accomplishments.
The body, together with its outer and inner,
Is stainless like the sky.
Thus, oneself being equal to space,
One sees oneself as ever liberated.
Bodiless, with neither beginning nor end,
Free of qualities such as sound,
Completely liberated from duality,
In every way it abides as anything at all.
Relying on things that are no things,
One should make them things without support.
Having made the mind into no mind,
One should not think of anything at all.

Abiding on one's seat, and having made firm
The lineage of the gurus,
One should then meditate on the mind
Of one taste, equal to space.
Liberated from the forms of meditative concentration,
Having fully abandoned the workings of speculation,
The mental factors become stable as mind;
Beings too are likewise in their intrinsic nature.
Equal to space, it abides truly as space,
Like a pure crystal jewel,
A form with neither beginning nor end,
Free of elaboration, not the domain of the senses,
Changeless and without appearance,
Empty of all, without afflictions,
The lamp of beings, destroying the bonds
Of existence, free of verbal expression,
Not even the domain of the mind,
Liberated from duality, without afflictions:
To that very reality, unmoving,
Which bestows ultimate liberation, I bow.
When one touches that reality just as it is,
It cannot be conceived by any thought.
When one thinks of what cannot be conceived,
Then it becomes inconceivable.
Just as sentient beings are without thought,
So, just as without thought, are the Victors too.
Because the buddhas, free of thought,
Have fully taught this thought,
In that inconceivable mind
It passes beyond the reckoning of all thoughts.
Without the concepts of manifold conceptualization,
It is great bliss without attachment,
The supreme of all aspects,
Beyond all the senses, without aspects.
Its nature is thing and non-thing,
Yet it fully abandons thing and non-thing.
Because it is not inert, it is self-aware;
Unknowing, it does not see at all.
Formless, it abides nowhere at all;
It is changeless and stable.
Among phenomena that have no entity,
What is there to take up or reject?
All phenomena to be taken up or rejected
Are just like a dream.
Through fully knowing joy,
The supreme perfection of wisdom,
Abiding in the fruit of joy:
This is awakening without entity.
The two, of one taste and indivisible,
Are the very great bliss of joy.
The distinction of wisdom and compassion
Is like that of a lamp and its rays.
The indivisible nature of these two
Is the mind of a thing of one taste.
Through the union of wisdom and skillful means,
One accomplishes perfect awakening;
In that very thing all the unsurpassed
Buddhas fully abide.
Whoever abides in the correct awareness of indivisibility
Is Vajrasattva.
As long as, for the cause of perfect awakening,
One restrains it through bliss,
For so long will the practitioner experience it,
Until Saṃvara is perfected.

Toward beautiful illusions and magical displays, that knowing is just as it is;
One should thoroughly train in the sameness of all delight and weariness.
There is nothing that frightens, nor is there any arising of bliss;
Through that very reality the practitioner follows suchness as the self.

How wonderful! Through the desire of great bliss
The three worlds are filled.
How wonderful! The bliss of peace descends;
Manifold full awakening expands.
How wonderful! The great bliss of bliss!
How wonderful! However it may be, so let it be enjoyed!
How wonderful! The great self of the coemergent,
The essential nature of all phenomena!

Seeing beings as like the moon in water,
One rightly engages what is heard as like an echo;
The mind too should be viewed as like a mirage in the desert.
Thus food and drink are like space;
Scents should be smelled as like a flower in the sky;
The mind should pervade like the sun and moon.
That true abiding too is like Mount Meru;
It should be regarded just like a maiden's dream.
Conventions should be traversed as like illusions and magic shows.
Just as these are, so is the arising of coemergent bliss:
Existence devoid of intrinsic nature, a form beyond conception,
Ever dawning. I bow to and praise the supreme path of the Sugatas.

Abandoning all other offerings entirely,
One should properly undertake offering to the guru.
By pleasing him, one will attain
The supreme primordial wisdom of omniscience.
If one fully worships the master,
Unsurpassed in his deeds, as Vajrasattva,
What merit has one not made?
What austerity is there that one has not displayed?
To one who has abandoned fear and wrongdoing,
Who is endowed with bliss,
And who guards the conduct of the sacred commitments,
The stages should be fully taught.
By reading, keeping, or writing out
The Glorious Heruka Abhidhāna Tantra,
One will attain accomplishment, miraculous powers, good fortune,
And the state of a bodhisattva.
The Ḍākinījālasaṃvara, in union with all the heroes:
When one meditates and reflects
On the Glorious Saṃvarodaya Tantra,
One has great wealth and great bliss,
And the suffering of poverty is destroyed.
For sentient beings of many inclinations
He teaches manifold conduct,
And to train them in various ways
He has rightly taught the methods as well.
When the profound Dharma is taught,
Even if someone does not come to feel faith in it,
He should not disparage it;
For the nature of reality, indivisible emptiness and compassion,
Is wholly inconceivable;
The play of the buddhas is inconceivable.
Glorious Heruka, on whom the hosts of ḍākinīs rely,
Is in perfect union.
Those sentient beings who enter through faith
Delight in all of these,
And abide in the state of glorious Heruka,
In union with all the ḍākinīs.

This is the thirty-third chapter.

Through the firm faith born in me for the text of the tantra,

May the noble and the learned be patient with it.

Translator's notes

Here སྡོམ་པ in the opening line of the request is the deity and tantra Saṃvara, not "vow," given the title of the work. The bhagas of the vajra consorts (rdo rje btsun mo, vajrayoṣit) is the standard tantric setting. སྒྲ་མི་སྙན is the standard rendering for the northern continent Kuru (Uttarakuru). The five embryonic stages (Tibetan nur nur po, mer mer po, ltar ltar po, gor gor po, mkhrang gyur) are rendered descriptively; they correspond to the Sanskrit stages usually given as kalala, arbuda, peśī, ghana, and the hardened form, though the sequence of equivalents varies between sources. The "wisdom woman" (shes rab) whose belly the female embryo faces is the mother. Āli and kāli here are the lunar and solar currents associated with the vowels and consonants. The "measured maṇḍala" translates dkyil 'khor tshad can, a maṇḍala with fixed dimensions, contrasted with the countless emanated deities. The calculation of the time of death in this passage uses symbolic number words, rendered here as numerals: earth (fourteen, as the worlds), sun (twelve), directions (ten), auspicious signs (eight), the fierce one (eleven, the Rudras), eye or pair (two), moon (one), lunar day (fifteen), arrows (five), qualities (three). The value of yan lag ("limb") as thirteen is inferred from the sequence of days and is uncertain. The term rendered "the expansion" (rgyas pa) names the critical period around the birth day and month. The "lord" (mgon po) whose movement in āli or kāli is examined is the breath itself, identified with the deity. The "Lotus Lord," "Karma Lord," "Jewel Lord," and "Vajra Lord" are the heads of the corresponding buddha families (Amitābha, Amoghasiddhi, Ratnasambhava, and the Vajra family head). The twenty-four sites are given as the Tibetan spells them; ཏྲེ་ཏ་བུ་རི is read as Pretapurī, the usual name of this site in the list, the initial ཏྲེ being an evident slip for པྲེ.
