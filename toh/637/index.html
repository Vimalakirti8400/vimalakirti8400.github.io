About this text

Toh 637 in the Kangyur is a dhāraṇī text known in English as "The Noble Samantabhadra's One Hundred and Eight Names, Together with Dhāraṇī and Mantra," in Sanskrit as the Ārya Samantabhadra Aṣṭottaraśataka Nāma Dhāraṇī Mantra Sahita, and in Tibetan as འཕགས་པ་ཀུན་ཏུ་བཟང་པོའི་མཚན་བརྒྱ་རྩ་བརྒྱད་པ་གཟུངས་སྔགས་དང་བཅས་པ. It belongs to the dhāraṇī genre of canonical Buddhist literature, presenting one hundred and eight names of the great bodhisattva Samantabhadra — a figure associated with boundless compassion, the vast expanse of the Dharma, and the bodies of the three times — along with accompanying dhāraṇī and mantra for practice and recitation. Despite some discrepancy between the colophon's reference to Ākāśagarbha and the body of the text's focus on Samantabhadra, the text as translated concerns the veneration and invocation of Samantabhadra through his sacred names and protective formulas. It is found among the dhāraṇī collections of the Kangyur as Toh 637.

Translation

To the Protector I will always turn, and always practice the mantra as well. The Noble Ākāśagarbha's One Hundred and Eight Names, together with dhāraṇī and mantra, is complete.

In the language of India: Ārya Samantabhadra Aṣṭottaraśataka Nāma Dhāraṇī Mantra Sahita. In the Tibetan language: The Noble Samantabhadra's One Hundred and Eight Names, Together with Dhāraṇī and Mantra.

Homage to Samantabhadra, the supreme great bodhisattva (bodhisattva) endowed with great compassion, who has attained the luminous manifestation of the Dharma equal in essential nature to the boundless and inexhaustible expanse of space, who has attained the body pervading all the maṇḍalas of the earth without distinction of difference, equal to the bodies of the three times, who is the supreme and most excellent of great bodhisattvas with his manifold forms, the supreme bestower of what is excellent and sacred, sovereign over all the grounds of the body of great qualities.

Then at that time the supreme great bodhisattva Samantabhadra of manifold forms entered meditative absorption into the samādhi called the Great Sovereign of the Inexhaustible Grounds of All Bodhisattvas. And the very moment the great bodhisattva Samantabhadra entered that absorption, there arose an inexhaustible multitude of bodies as numerous as the atoms of the great thousandfold world-system, pervading everywhere. The bodies belonging to the realm of space were arrayed throughout the many maṇḍalas of the earth.

Then the Tathāgatas dwelling in all the inexhaustible buddha-fields extended their right hands and, stroking the crown of the great bodhisattva Samantabhadra's head, declared: "Excellent! Excellent, son of good family! Proclaim the great dhāraṇī that is the very heart of all the arising of buddhas."

Then that supreme great bodhisattva, his body illuminated by hundreds of thousands of rays of light, caused all the buddha-fields to tremble and proclaimed this dhāraṇī:

Tadyathā: Oṃ namo namastrī bhuvanīśvare mahābhogāya asamasama amitasama anantasama gaganasama tribhuvanasama aśeṣasama veṣasama paramārthasama svabhāvasama tathatāsama arajaḥsama śuddhi-sama buddhi-sama dharmatathātāsama abhiṣamasama samasama ekānāya sāgara viśuddhe āloka hī hī labdha labdha vijaya vijaya oṃ prasagata naya naya hi nira adhiṣṭhāna saṃbhoga aṃ analaya laya dharmaagra dhara dhara jaya jaya hūṃ hūṃ dhama dhama svāhā.

Then Samantabhadra the bodhisattva, the eldest son among the Victor's children, the sole protector of all worlds, wishing to benefit all sentient beings, gazed upon the face of the Blessed One, caused the maṇḍalas of the earth to tremble, and spoke these words, filling all three worlds completely.

Thereupon all the great bodhisattvas, together with gods and asuras, blazing with golden rays of light, their bodies adorned with a supreme quivering radiance, rose from the great vajra-flower cushioned thrones throughout the earth, stood up from the maṇḍala, uttered the cry "Ha ha!", and spoke:

"Ah, the great dhāraṇī, inexhaustibly vast as an ocean of boundless great compassion and immeasurable qualities! Ah, the great arising of buddhas! This great dhāraṇī abides in order to fully ripen the inexhaustible realm of sentient beings, equal to the three grounds without boundary or center, abiding in the Dharma-maṇḍala without fixed abode!"

"Alas! This great dhāraṇī dispels the habitual tendencies of evil cast forth from aeons without number, drains the ocean of afflictions, and scatters the mountains of wrong views. One who has not properly gathered the accumulation of virtue's roots across aeons without number, who has not directly beheld many hundreds of thousands of Tathāgatas, who has not accomplished awakening's purpose, who has not yet attained in full the ocean of distinctive qualities that belong to the ground of omniscience — how could any such person so much as hear this great dhāraṇī, the very essence of all Tathāgatas? To grasp it, recite it, or master it completely — what need even be said? To hear even the name of this great dhāraṇī is rare over tens of millions of aeons.

Alas! Those sentient beings in whose hands this great dhāraṇī rests have found a good finding. They live a good life. They have an excellent foundation for direct knowledge. Their roots of virtue are completely perfected. They dwell in the domain of the Tathāgatas. All qualities are perfectly complete in them. By reciting it even once, one will be freed from all the sufferings of the lower realms. By reciting it twice, one will be freed from all misdeeds. By reciting it three times, one will be freed from all afflictions. And so, by mere recitation, the qualities increase progressively, and one will come to behold the Tathāgatas. If one recites it while walking, one will be freed from all suffering. If one recites it at the foot of a single standing tree, all accomplishments will be attained. If one recites it while on a journey, all sentient beings will come under one's sway. If one sits cross-legged inside a great forest and recites it seven times, one will attain great meditative concentration and will also become free from all afflictions.

If one sits cross-legged before an image of the noble Samantabhadra and offers whatever flowers and other things one has, then recites this great dhāraṇī until the body grows weary, and upon being weary lies down right there, one will behold the bodhisattva Samantabhadra in a dream. He will touch that person with his right hand, grant him perfect solace, and bestow upon him the great radiance of Dharma. He will also speak thus: 'Excellent! Excellent! O child of the Victors! You have entered the great maṇḍala of the Tathāgata. When you pass from this life, you will attain the great ground.' Then one will awaken possessed of physical ease, mental ease, great joy, clarity, and supreme delight. One will also be renowned as one of great power and great authority. The gods themselves will come to behold that person. Rising at dawn, one should read the sūtras fully gathered by the bodhisattvas. One should also arouse a mind of benefit and happiness toward all sentient beings. Thereupon all the perfections will be completely fulfilled, separation from all misdeeds will come about, the forces of affliction will be vanquished, and the Tathāgatas themselves will sing one's praise. Gods and others will strive to guard, protect, and shelter that person. A child of the Victors who practices exactly as has been taught in this way will, without long delay, enter upon the great ground of Samantabhadra."

together with Ratnajālinī and the other great goddesses of vidyā-mantras, together with the goddess Tārā and the other deities of the maṇḍala of the great bodhisattvas, together with Mahādhvaja, Surūpa, Atidarśanīya, and the other youths, and the young women, the male attendants and the female attendants, together with Acala and the other great leaders of the wayward, together with Śreṣṭhakāya and the other lords of rākṣasas, together with Māmakī and the other rākṣasī-women, together with the Great Lord (Śiva) and the other divine sovereigns — all of these, together with Vajrapuṣpakūṭa and all the bodhisattvas and others assembled in the great maṇḍala, rose from their seats and offered praise to the supreme noble bodhisattva, the great being Samantabhadra, by means of one hundred and eight names, in a hymn called "The Illumination of Unsurpassed Qualities."

Translator's notes

The text opens with the colophon closing the preceding text on Ākāśagarbha's names, then immediately begins the new text on Samantabhadra's one hundred and eight names. These are treated as two sequential items and translated accordingly.

The Sanskrit dhāraṇī syllables are reproduced as-is from the Tibetan phonetic transcription rather than reconstructed, as is standard practice for dhāraṇī passages; minor scribal variants in the Tibetan transliteration are preserved rather than emended.

The phrase rendered "without fixed abode" for "gnas pa med pa'i" carries the Mahāyāna technical sense of the non-abiding nirvāṇa (apratiṣṭhitanirvāṇa), where the bodhisattva abides in neither saṃsāra nor a quiescent nirvāṇa — a doctrinally significant point worth noting. The phrase rendered "the ground of omniscience" translates a compound meaning the ground or stage characterized by omniscience, likely indicating the buddha-ground. The list of figures at the close — Vajraketu, Mahānetra, Vajragarbha, Vajrapāśa — are names of specific deities and bodhisattvas within the maṇḍala retinue; they are kept in Sanskrit reconstruction since the Tibetan renders them by meaning. "The great ground of Samantabhadra" refers to the bodhisattva stage associated with Samantabhadra, a well-attested usage in this textual tradition. The sentence "accomplished awakening's purpose" renders a phrase that could also be read as "accomplished the merit of awakening"; the former reading is adopted as contextually more fitting within the rhetorical negations. The names "Ratnajālinī," "Mahādhvaja," "Surūpa," "Atidarśanīya," "Acala," "Śreṣṭhakāya," "Māmakī," and "Vajrapuṣpakūṭa" are back-rendered from the Tibetan epithets, following established equivalences in the Vajrayāna maṇḍala literature; the Tibetan forms are transparent compounds. The phrase rendered "Companion of knowledge" for ཤེས་ཉེན་ཅན is somewhat compressed and may mean "one who is close to knowledge" or "one in the company of the knowledgeable." The "ten diligences and ten skillful means" and "dispeller of the ten" are standard enumerated sets in this genre; the specific referents of "dispeller of the ten" are left unglossed as they would require commentary to specify. "The Capable One" for ཐུབ་པ refers to Śākyamuni as the sage or the capable one, a common epithet.
