About this text

The Commentary on the Vajra Song, known in Sanskrit as the Vajragītibhāṣya and in Tibetan as Dorjé Lu Shepa, is a short tantric commentary that explains the meaning of a vajra song, an esoteric verse in the Apabhraṃśa language of the kind sung by practitioners at tantric gatherings. After paying homage to the holy guru, to the heroes and heroines who dwell in the sacred sites and fields, and to the vajra master who has crossed beyond saṃsāra, the author says he writes this account of the excellent song for his own sake. He then works through the song phrase by phrase, showing how its cryptic lines point to the sacred sites, secondary sites, fields, and secondary fields of tantric geography, and to the compassion and inner yogic experience they symbolize. The work is Toh 1208 in the Tengyur, where it belongs among the commentaries on the tantras.

Translation

In the language of India: vajragītibhaṣyaṃ nāma. In the language of Tibet: Commentary on the Vajra Song.

I pay homage at the feet of the holy guru.

To those who examine yogic practice, who partake of the fruit of the essence, protector and sovereign of the three worlds, To the hosts of good qualities dwelling in the sacred sites and fields, the heroes and the sovereign heroines, To the vajra master, the great guru, a treasure of good qualities, who has crossed to the far shore of saṃsāra: Paying homage, I shall write, for my own sake, this pure account of the excellent song.

kolla a reṭṭhṛ a bolā teaches all the sites and secondary sites without exception. mummuṇi re kakkolā teaches all the fields and secondary fields without exception. ghaṇi kṛpiṭāho bājjai kāruṇe kiai ṇarolā teaches all the cchandoha sites without exception. tahiṃ bala khājjai gāḍheṃ maana bājjiai teaches all the secondary cchandoha sites without exception. hale kaliñjara pani ai dudhura bājjiai teaches all the meeting places without exception. causama kacchuri sihlā kappūra lāiai teaches all the secondary meeting places without exception. malāindhanaṃ śāliṃja tahiṃ bharu khaiai teaches all the charnel grounds without exception. niraṃśu aṅga caḍavi tahiṃ ja sāra ba paṇi ai teaches all the secondary charnel grounds without exception. phreṃkhaṇa kheṭa karante śuddhaaśuddha ṇa muṇi a i teaches all the drinking places without exception. mala āṃce kunduru baḍai ḍiṇḍima tahiṇṇa bājja ai teaches all the secondary drinking places without exception. This is a summary of the sites and the rest, following the sequence of the song.

There is also another way of explaining it. In kolla i reṭṭhi a bo lā, ko is the vajra of body, lā is the vajra of speech, re is the vajra of mind, and ṭṭhi a means abiding in the bola.

In mummuṇi re kakkola, mu is the rasanā channel, mmu is the lalanā channel, ṇi is the avadhūtī channel, re is what makes the three channels clear, and kakkola is the place where they abide.

ghaṇe kṛpiṭ ho bājja i is the three vajras entering into union within the three channels.

kāruṇe ki a i na ro lā is the arising of the awakening mind from the avadhūtī channel, as a result of bringing the three vajras into union.

tahiṃ bala khājja i gāḍheṃ ma a ṇa pijja a i means that there, when the awakening mind arises, bala is eaten and madanaṃ is also drunk; "intensely arising" is the condensed meaning of this.

In hale kāliñjara paṇṛ a i duṃdhura bajja a i, hale is a call. kāliñjara is one who is fortunate, and such a one should be admitted. duṃdhura is one who is unfortunate, and such a one should be excluded, at that time.

causama kacchuri sihlā kappūra lā a e i means rolling pills from the four substances, feces, urine, flower and seed, and also eating them. There, at that time, this is for the sake of satisfying one's own mind and of destroying one's own conceptual thought; it is the supreme armor.

In mala indhana śāliñja tahiṃ bharu khā i a i, māla indhanaṃ is cooked food, śāliṃjja is the great flesh, tahiṃ means at that time, and bharu khā i a i means eating, having understood how to combine them in the proper order.

In phreṃ kha ṇa khe ṭa karante śuddha a śuddha ṇa mu ṇi a i, phreṅ is the gathering of the heroes and sovereign heroines who are born from the sites, born from the fields, and born from names. kheṭa karante is the nature of those heroes and sovereign heroines being one with the yoga of one's own chosen deity. śuddha a śuddha ṇan mu ṇi a i means that at that time one does not discriminate between whom one may approach and whom one may not, what may be eaten and what may not, what may be drunk and what may not. Whatever women or men are at that site, outcastes (caṇḍālas) and the like, those born in border lands, or those born of low families, all of them are to be worshipped through the yoga of one's own chosen deity; their substance is not to be disparaged.

nirāṃ śu adge ca ḍā bi a i ta hiṃ ja sā ra vā pa ni a i means that the practitioner, being free of all conceptual thought because things lack intrinsic nature, and free of dualistic mind, takes on the pride of glorious Heruka. Then, for the sake of the great offering, with a satisfied mind, he should take a flawless human body from a charnel ground or a battlefield, bow to the heroes and sovereign heroines, and offer it to that gathering.

mala a je kunduru ba ṭa i ḍiṇḍima tahinna bājji a i means this. There, at the time of the great gathering, the heroes and sovereign heroines, their minds delighted from eating bāla and drinking liquor, feel great desire for the practitioner and wish to press him to their hearts. Therefore, with a satisfied intention, as the cause of their taking the meritorious practitioner into their care, the practitioner, with great satisfaction and a stainless intention, having taken on the pride of glorious Heruka, should worship that very body, born of an outcaste or similar family, utterly vile and nauseating, fully envisioned in the form of the samaya consort Nairātmyā, and should not reject it.

This is a little of the meaning of the oral instruction of the song.

In kolla i reṭṭhi a bolā mummuṇi re kakkolā, kolla i re is skillful means, and "abiding" refers to this. In this way skillful means is called kolā. mummuṇi, by the nature of wisdom, refers to the source of phenomena of wisdom, which is called kakkola.

Alternatively, ko is kappūr, and lla is dissolving; the dissolving of that kappūra is called mala. o is the word for skillful means, the nature of the three channels and the twelve. giri is the site of the head. That head has eight skulls; the mountain too is adorned with eight peaks. The mountain trickles unceasingly with many different streams; that head likewise, through its thirty-two channels, trickles unceasingly day and night. The mountain, together with its outer slopes, is filled with various jewels; that head is adorned with many kinds of jewels, such as the awakening mind. For this very reason it is the abode of all the gods. The head too is the abode of the gods.

This meaning that is truly present in the body is called "god." For this very reason the self, the intrinsic nature, of all the gods is the awakening mind. By this reasoning the head is called giri.

ba is the vajra. o is that which draws the vajra. la is the arising of the ornament. "Abiding" means abiding in the hub of the vajra. a is the nature of supreme joy. mu is all the limbs. mmu is the eight-petaled lotus together with its anthers. ṇa is she who has the form of the utterly imperceptible avadhūtī channel, right in the center of that lotus. ro is its illuminating and opening. ka is bliss. kko is desire. la is the emission of the ornament that has become fluid. a is the nature of space.

As for ghaṇe kṛpiṭa ho bājja i: ghaṇa is the vajra. kṛpiṭa is the lotus. ho is the suchness of the vajra portion and the lotus portion. bājja i is the sound of vajra and lotus striking together through intense attachment to kunduru.

As for kāruṇe ki a i na rolā: kunduru is supreme joy. At that very time of kunduru, the time when supreme joy arises, there are the scratches made with the nails and the wounds made by biting with the teeth. Whatever is done in that battle, such as the cries ha and si, is called "play."

As for tahiṃ bala khājja ī, gāḍheṃ ma a ṇa bājji a i: tahiṃ means that at that time of battle one should bite the lower lip, and, through intense attachment to bliss, by the practice of the various kinds of kissing and the practice of the lower lip, one should drink what trickles down. By this practice, what is called eating bāla and drinking madana is the point.

As for hale kāliñjara pani a i, duṃdhura bājja a i: hale is a call. kāliñjara is the fortunate one. paṇi a i means that the excellent fortunate one should be made to enter there. Alternatively, ha is the vajra. la is play, that is, engaging in kunduru. i is the lotus.

Again, as for kāliñjara: the practitioner is endowed with yoga through the pride of Picu, and the consort is perfected in the form of Nairātmyā. Then, at the time of kunduru, the lord among practitioners should visualize the seed syllable hūṃ on a moon disc at his heart. Having visualized it, he sends out rays of light, black in color and shaped like hooks, and invites the principal deities, the buddhas such as the glorious Heruka who have come into the midst of the sky. He makes them enter his own body. He then sends forth the awakening mind and should visualize it passing by the vajra path into the lotus of the consort. When the mind becomes delighted in the purity of the equal union of the two, one should emanate in the form of the deities and gather back in the form of bliss. This is the meaning of "kāliñjara should be made to enter."

Alternatively, kā is camphor. li is the union of vajra and lotus, because it brings about well-being. ja is what that union of vajra and lotus produces and emits. Alternatively, that which, as the awakening mind itself, produces the three realms, whatever there is, the stationary and the moving, all the buddhas, and the bodhisattvas, is called ja. repha means that through being imbued with great passion all the buddhas are delighted; because it arises from great passion, it is therefore called kāliñjara.

By this practice, out of the beauty of supreme joy, immediately the awakening mind of saṃsāra, having the form of great bliss, at the time when it neither remains clearly at the mouth of the Lord of the Family nor has yet come forth, all phenomena in the aspect of the three worlds, the three realms, the stationary and the moving, at that time enter and go inward with the nature of constant single taste; whatever nature is thus said to have dissolved, that itself is called "kāliñjara should be made to enter."

In dudhura, du is delusion, dhu is aversion, and ra is attachment. The thorough illumination and abandonment of the principal ones, attachment, aversion and delusion, and of all the afflictions without exception that are enumerated, is called the state of equanimity.

kā is said to be desire. thu is said to be the basis of desire. ra is slight cooking, and joy is called kathuri. siṇaṃ is said to be bliss. hlā is the one who causes joy; being great through the gladness of the mind, it is called supreme. ca is said to be the bhaga. u is said to be what illuminates. śā should be known as the equal face. ma is the nature of union. ka is the form of the fluid. ppū is said to be the lotus-born. ra is delight in the lotus. kappūra is said to be the joy of cessation.

This teaches that at the time of entering into kāliñja, the arising of the nature of the ultimate awakening mind, through the nature of the four joys, is what has been shown and entrusted, the declaration of the summit of the samaya ground.

As for mala indhanaṃ śāliṃ ja, tahiṃ bharu kha u a i: ma is the head. la is the place of destruction. indhaṇaṃ is what illuminates. śuni is bliss, that is, the awakening mind. liṃ is dripping. tahiṃ means at that time of the four moments. bharu means that very stream through the night. khā i a i means focusing on the uninterrupted experience of bliss. Alternatively, the point is that the awakening mind released at each occasion should be consumed and not cast away.

pa is the lotus together with its center and anthers. e is the shape of the letter E. ra is the shape of the three-pronged vajra, which opens the lotus. khā is the opening of the ḍākinī's face, the point being the opening of the avadhūtī channel. ṇa is the bliss that is the nature of the drop of the awakening mind, abiding clearly at the tips of the vajra and the lotus. kheṭa karante means the point that through the union of the two, vajra and lotus, the two drops come together and are made nondual.

śuddhā śuddha na muṇi i: this means that at that time of excellent bliss, when the two drops become nondual, entity and non-entity, self and what belongs to self, the grasped and the grasper are not accepted. That is, ma ni a i carries the sense "is not accepted."

In ni rāṃ śu aṃ ge ca ḍā bi a i, ni rāṃ śu is the mudrā whose nature is the five buddhas: Vairocana, Akṣobhya, Ratna, Ārolik, and finally Amogha. aṅga caḍa bi means that with this form of the buddhas one should hold the pride of being the five aggregates. ta hiṃ means "at that time when the two embrace." ja means all phenomena, whatever is still and whatever moves. sa is connate joy. rva is clarity. ba is the cycles of mind seized by the two habitual tendencies. These are thoroughly purified by connate joy and released through the union in which all that moves and all that is still arise as a single nature; and the sense is that the nature of whoever practices this meditation is to be placed in that.

In ma la a je kundu ru ba ṭā i ḍiṇḍi ma ta hiṃ naṃ bājja a i, ma la a je is the gathering of all the heroes and heroine-queens. The words "all the heroes" refer to the six universal monarchs beginning with Śāśvata. The words "all the heroine-queens" refer to the six female universal monarchs beginning with Mohavajrā. As many heroes and heroine-queens as there are, equal in number to the atoms, born from the very self of that: the practitioner meditates all those heroes within his own body, and all those heroine-queens he meditates within her. By this application, the meditation in which the foremost of practitioners joins in equal union with one who is like himself is the sense of ma la a je. The meditation that places vajra and lotus in equal union is the sense of "it is kundu ru."

ḍiṇḍi ma: because the awakening mind, which has the form of the connate, is untouched, it is ḍiṇḍima. The form of awakening that has the form of the connate is hard even to touch; it is mere self-awareness, beyond grasped and grasper. For this reason ultimate awakening mind is called ḍiṇḍima. ta hiṃ means that at the time when the connate arises, the awakening mind is to be held as far as one is able and not released.

This is the explanation, without conceit, of the meaning of the supreme king of songs spoken in the Picuvajra Tantra. Delighting at having found the supreme song, Nairātmyā, chief of yoginīs, attached with great passion, requested it of the Blessed One at the time when she had perfectly worshipped him with vajra worship, in order to gather up the excellent meaning that is the utmost secret of the great secret, and to dispel doubt; and he spoke it so that the yoginīs of his greatly delighted heart might understand it.

Next, the explanation of the dance is given:

In the yoga of unstolen mindfulness,
the dance in the form of Heruka:
with a mind free of attachment, undefiled,
it is meditated with a mind of passion.

Śrī is nondual wisdom: whatever awakening mind arises through the union in which emptiness and compassion are nondual, that itself is nondual wisdom. He means empty of causes and the rest; he is the vajra. E is the lotus. The bliss that arises from vajra and lotus having become the cause is nonconceptual, and that itself is empty of causes and the rest. Ru means free of aggregation, that is, free of the afflictions such as attachment. Ka means not abiding anywhere: to be free of having been thrust into anything is liberation itself, and through the nature in which all phenomena are of one taste it abides as the self of all. This is not abiding anywhere. Because one moves with this nature of glorious Heruka, it is called dance. "In the yoga of unstolen mindfulness" means: by this undivided, untroubled mindfulness of mind through the form of glorious Heruka. "By this meditation, with a mind of passion" means with a mind that is not free of attachment.

By Vajradharma and the buddhas,
by yoginīs and by mātṛkās:

by these, the songs and dances such as "the form of glorious Heruka dwelling on Mount Kolla" must certainly be sung and danced.

By this very thing the gathering is guarded,
and likewise it is protection itself;
by this the world is brought under control;
this is itself the recitation of mantra.
Where one sings with devotion,
where one dances with devotion,
having first placed the lord of the gathering,
there scent is to be indicated.

In "where with devotion song and dance," "with" means bliss. "Devotion" means longing for bliss; that itself is longing for bliss. Where one sings and dances: this has the sense of performing kunduru, by the guru's instructions, for the purpose of generating the connate. "Having first placed the lord of the gathering": the gathering is that of the heroes and heroine-queens, that is, many. "Lord" means the chief, the leader, the wheel-holder among those heroes and heroine-queens. "Having placed first" has the sense that the practitioner himself, with the pride of glorious Heruka, performs the union, entering equipoise with the consort perfected in the form of Nairātmyā. "There" means at that time of being intent on the equipoise of kunduru. "Scent is to be indicated" means that, through the distinctions of the four joys and the four moments, the inexhaustible nature of the awakening mind is to be indicated, that is, to be observed.

The scent of garlic comes first of all;
then, after that, the scent of vulture;
camphor and malayaja sandal after.
The characteristic of the song's blessing:
ru ni taṃ haṃ sa sya bhra ma ra sya
is to be heard at the song's end;
and the cry of the jackal
is to be noticed in the outer grove.
This is the characteristic of the song's blessing.

"The scent of garlic" means joy. "The scent of vulture" means supreme joy.

"Camphor and malayaja" means this. From the beauty of supreme joy, the lord of the family of awakening circulates inward, and at its mouth it is gathered in the form of a drop. To state this clearly is the substance of what is called kappūra malayaja.

"Then, the characteristic of the song's blessing": this means that after that, at that instant of camphor and malayaja, one should examine the song's blessing, the blessing of self-awareness, the innate joy.

rutaṃ haṃsasya bhramarasya "At the end of the song one should make it equal." That is the line. ha is the awakening mind that has been sent forth. sa is the sending forth together with the awakening mind, which is nothing but the true knowing of bliss. bha is the meditation on these. ma is self-awareness. ra is joy, through which the practitioner becomes free of evil.

ruta is the substance of what is called "utterance." "Sound" is the substance of what is called "conception." This is the substance of what is called "one should listen to the geese and the bees."

go is Vairocana. ma is Akṣobhya. yo is Amitābha. ra is Ratnasambhava. pi is Amoghasiddhi. "Sound" is what is realized by those five Tathāgatas.

"It is to be noticed in the outer grove": this means that the awakening mind, released at the joy of cessation and abiding in the lotus of the consort, should be imagined as having the intrinsic nature of the circle of the maṇḍala, and then eaten. Having eaten it, one should visualize oneself in the form of the circle of the maṇḍala. The purpose is "one should meditate."

You who have crossed to the far shore of the ocean of mantra,
you of good fame and endowed with merit, please be patient.
Merely as a reminder for myself,
I have written this just as I received it.

This completes the Commentary on the Vajra Song, composed by the master Tārā-Endowed Host.

It was translated by the Indian preceptor Gayadhara and the monk Shākya Yeshé.

Translator's notes

The song is in Apabhraṃśa transliterated into Tibetan, and the commentary glosses it in the coded language of the Heruka tantras: bala as flesh, madana as liquor, "flower" as menstrual blood, "seed" as semen, kakkola as the lotus. The Tibetan "ma ngan" in the passage about the heroes drinking evidently stands for madana and is rendered "liquor". "Born from names" is a literal rendering; the usual triad is the field-born, mantra-born and innate ḍākinīs, so "names" may stand for mantra. "Three channels and the twelve" is translated as written, but it may be a slip for the thirty-two channels mentioned a few lines later. "Streams" renders "su", which is taken as a slip for "chu", water. The commentary glosses the Apabhraṃśa words of a vajra song syllable by syllable. These words are kept in romanized form as the Tibetan spells them. "Picu" (pi tsu) is the figure whose divine pride the practitioner assumes; it is not securely identified and is kept as written. "The joy of cessation" renders dga' bral (viramānanda). The phrase rendered "through the night" (mtshan du) could alternatively mean "as a mark." The Apabhraṃśa song words and the Sanskrit line quoted in the verse are kept as written in Tibetan letters. The Sanskrit line refers to the humming or crying of the goose and the bee heard at the song's end. "The lord of the gathering" renders ཚོགས་ཀྱི་བདག་པོ (gaṇapati). In this context the commentary explicitly explains it as the chief of the assembly of heroes and heroines, not the god Gaṇeśa. "Picuvajra Tantra" (པི་ཙུ་རྡོ་རྗེའི་རྒྱུད) is given as written; its exact identity is uncertain. In "ཐམས་ཅད་ཀྱི་ན་བདག་ཉིད" the stray ན has been read as a slip, giving "the self of all." In the list of five buddhas, Ratna and Amogha stand for Ratnasambhava and Amoghasiddhi, and Ārolik is the Hevajra name for Amitābha. The concluding sentence of the song section is a single long Tibetan sentence, framed by "This is ... the explanation of the meaning." It has been split into two English sentences. Whether "delighting at having found the supreme song" qualifies Nairātmyā or the song itself is grammatically open. The author's name, sgrol ma can gyi sde, could not be identified with confidence as a Sanskrit form, so it is rendered literally as "Tārā-Endowed Host." "Joy of cessation" renders rnam par dga' ba, taken here as one of the four joys (viramānanda). The single syllables are glossed letter by letter from the song's words, as the commentary presents them. In the quoted line "At the end of the song one should make it equal," the Tibetan reads mnyam par bya ("make equal"), where the verse earlier read "is to be heard" (probably mnyan par bya). This may be a scribal variant, but it is translated as it stands.
