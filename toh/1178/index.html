About this text

In Praise of the Birth Stories, known in Sanskrit as the Jātakastava and in Tibetan as Kyepa Rab kyi Töpa, is a devotional hymn in verse addressed to the Buddha. It celebrates the perfect conduct he showed in his former lives as a bodhisattva, recalling one birth story (jātaka) after another: the gift of his children and wife, the hare who offered his own body in the fire, the patience he kept while his limbs were hacked apart, the six-tusked elephant who gave up his tusks, King Śibi weighed on the scale to save a bird from a hawk, and the gift of his eyes and of his head. Its colophon names the master Jñānayaśas as its author and records that it was translated from an Indian manuscript by Dharmapālabhadra, the translator of Zhalu, and the Tibetan version sets the Sanskrit verses alongside their translation. The work is Toh 1178 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the Praises (stotra) section.

All-pervading Lord who possesses the ten powers, the mastery of your unmingled conduct,
Unfolding through the stream of your births: how could this ever be mine, dull as I am?
Yet because toward you even a mere particle brings forth fruit that is not in vain,

With a great and terrible gush of blood.

Hoods reared in fury, the lord of waters (the ocean) heaving with its terrible garlands of waves, its sea monsters:
With your body, O great Victor, you brought them to the shore.
By that surpassing deed, all without exception shone.

You made it bear full fruit; and so from you the sprouts of the precious true Dharma have sprung for the wise.

Translator's notes

This piece interleaves the Sanskrit verses with the Tibetan translation, and the Tibetan breaks off mid-verse where the Sanskrit resumes; the English follows the Tibetan in its own order. Here རྒྱུད renders the continuum of the Bodhisattva's lives (santati), not "tantra", and སྐྱོན་མཐར་བྱེད is "putting an end to faults", not Antaka (Death). སེམས in the elephant verse is the verb "I think". བཞན is read as བཞིན ("like"), an evident copyist's slip. ད་ན is taken as "now". ཆང་འཕོགས་པ is taken as "deprived of drink" (the lost travellers of the elephant story); this is uncertain. In the jewel simile the Tibetan lacks the rhetorical question ("who takes the jewels deep within?") implied by the verse; it is translated as the Tibetan stands. The piece alternates Sanskrit verse lines, given in romanization, with the Tibetan rendering; the English follows the Tibetan in its own order. Here དྲན་པ is Smara, the god of love, not mindfulness; ཆད་པ is the "cutting-off" of a little water, not the doctrinal term annihilation; and ཞི་ལྡན་རྒྱལ་པོ is King Śibi. གྲོང་བྱ, literally "village bird", is the bird of the jātaka on honoring elders. འགྲིས is read as འདྲིས ("thoroughly practiced"), an evident copyist's slip. Some of the Tibetan follows the Sanskrit word by word and misreads it: "like a doubt" (ཐེ་ཚོམ་བཞིན) answers Sanskrit sandoham iva, "like an abundance", and "the vital points of the cakras" answers the Sanskrit verb cakre, "made"; both are translated as the Tibetan stands. In the Śibi verse the Tibetan makes Māra the one who renders the body light. The lines on the serpents' worship and on Glory trembling are obscure and rendered tentatively. ཐེག་པ་གཅིག་པར could also be read "in the one vehicle"; the Sanskrit verse (yānaikatāna, "intent solely on the vehicle") supports "single-minded in the vehicle", which is followed here.
