About this text

Toh 234 in the Kangyur is a Mahāyāna sūtra known in Sanskrit as the Ārya-Mahāmegha-vāyu-maṇḍala-parivarta-sarvanāgahṛdaya and in Tibetan as Phags pa sprin chen po rlung gi dkyil 'khor gyi le'u klu thams cad kyi snying po, rendered in English as "The Noble Great Vehicle Sūtra: The Chapter on the Maṇḍala of the Wind of the Great Cloud, the Heart-Essence of All Nāgas." The text belongs to the sūtra division of the Kangyur and centers on the world of the nāgas, serpentine beings associated with rain, water, and the natural elements, presenting the Buddha teaching in a jeweled ocean palace before a vast assembly of nāga kings possessed of great miraculous power. It is concerned with esoteric heart-essences or dhāraṇīs connected to nāgas and the wind-maṇḍala of the great cloud, themes that link it to both the sūtra and dhāraṇī literature of the Mahāyāna tradition. The text thus sits at the intersection of narrative sūtra and ritual-protective literature, reflecting a widespread Indian and Tibetan interest in harnessing the power of nāgas for rain, fertility, and spiritual protection.

The nāga king "Anāvatapta" renders the Tibetan "Ma dros pa," meaning "the Unheated" — a well-known nāga king of that name in Buddhist cosmology, whose cool lake is frequently mentioned in sūtra literature. The rendering is therefore given by name rather than as a literal translation.

The closing lines beginning "Hey, great nāgas" followed by syllables such as "mulu mulu, cata cata" and so forth are a dhāraṇī-style invocatory formula, likely a ritual summoning cry addressed to the nāgas. The syllables are not translatable as ordinary language and are reproduced phonetically as they stand.

The Tibetan title gives "parivarta" (chapter or section) as part of the compound, suggesting this translated passage is the opening of the sūtra through to the beginning of its first chapter on the wind maṇḍala and the nāgas. The passage contains extensive dhāraṇī mantra syllables and onomatopoeic sound-words representing thunder, wind, and rain. These are rendered phonetically as they appear in the Tibetan, following standard convention for dhāraṇī passages where the syllables are understood as having intrinsic power rather than propositional meaning.
