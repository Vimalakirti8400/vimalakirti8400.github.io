About this text

Toh 616 in the Kangyur is a dhāraṇī text known in Sanskrit as the Ārya-Saptavetāḍaka-nāma-dhāraṇī and in Tibetan as the Noble Dhāraṇī Called "The Seven Great Corpses That Rise." As its name suggests, the text centers on a powerful dhāraṇī spoken by the Buddha in response to a dramatic incident in which rival ascetics sent seven animated corpses to attack him; unable to harm the Blessed One, the corpses instead overwhelmed his attendant Ānanda, prompting the Buddha to reveal the protective formula. The narrative is set on Vulture Peak Mountain near Rājagṛha and follows the familiar sūtra opening of Ānanda's testimony, placing this work within the broad tradition of dhāraṇī scriptures that emphasize the protective and apotropaic power of sacred speech. It belongs to the dhāraṇī section of the Kangyur, reflecting the canonical status accorded to such texts as authoritative word of the Buddha.

In the language of India: Ārya-Saptavetāḍaka-nāma-dhāraṇī. In the language of Tibet: The Noble Dhāraṇī Called "The Seven Great Corpses That Rise."

Homage to all buddhas and bodhisattvas.

Thus did I hear. At one time the Blessed One was dwelling together with a great Saṅgha of twelve hundred and fifty monks on Vulture Peak Mountain near Rājagṛha, and together with him also was the venerable Ānanda, who followed close behind.

At that time, certain wandering tīrthikas sent seven great animated corpses in order to harm the Blessed One. When those corpses went toward the Blessed One, they found no opening and no target. Finding no opening and no target, they went to where the venerable Ānanda was, approached him, and touched his body. The moment they touched him, Ānanda was overcome with unbearable suffering, severe and burning, with feelings as though struck by death. Then the venerable Ānanda thought to himself: "I am afflicted with unbearable suffering, severe and burning, with feelings as though struck by death — has the Blessed One not noticed? That I have fallen into such distress — has the Tathāgata not noticed?"

At that time, the venerable Śāriputra and the venerable Mahāmaudgalyāyana were sitting not far from the venerable Ānanda. They heard of that great affliction that had come upon the venerable Ānanda. The two of them then went to where the venerable Ānanda was, and upon arriving, spoke these words: "Venerable Ānanda, having experienced this unbearable, severe, and burning suffering, are you saying that the Tathāgata has not noticed you? Have you truly said such words?" When these words were spoken, the venerable Ānanda replied to them saying: "Venerable ones, the sensation that has come upon my body is like, for example, having the top of one's head split open with a sword — what touched Ānanda was just like that. Ānanda, do not be afraid. We will place you on a litter and carry you to the Tathāgata. The Blessed One will protect you completely." Then the venerable Śāriputra and the venerable Mahāmaudgalyāyana placed the venerable Ānanda on a resting litter and went to where the Blessed One was, bringing him to the presence of the Blessed One.

Then the Blessed One said to the venerable Ānanda: "Ānanda, have you truly been struck by unbearable, severe, and burning feeling?" The venerable one replied: "Blessed One, it is so."

"Ānanda, therefore listen well and hold this in mind — I shall teach. The seven great animated corpses that touched you are mighty, hard to tame, and harmful to behold. What are the seven? They are these: the one called Garland-Holder, the Jewel-Holder, the Fierce One, the Outcast, the Peacock's Heart, the Life-Exhausted One, and the Horned One. These seven great animated corpses are mighty, hard to tame, hard to approach, possessed of great miraculous power, and of great force. Each of these animated corpses, if it so desires, can hurl even Mount Meru, the king of mountains, with the big toe of its left foot, and shatter the king of mountains, Mount Meru, into pieces the size of mustard seeds. If it gazes upward, it splits the earth open. If it gazes in any direction, beings die."

"If it gazes toward the intermediate directions, it sets fire by burning. These shall touch you. Nevertheless, by the power of the Tathāgata, by the reverence born of having heard the Dharma, and by the blessing of the Tathāgata, your body still lives."

Ānanda said: "Blessed One, please drive them away properly. Please turn them back. Please bind them with a seal. Please bind them with a cord."

The Blessed One declared: "Ānanda, therefore listen well and hold it in mind, for it shall be taught.

Ānanda, these forty-two great commanders of yakṣa hosts are those who steal splendor in the night. Ānanda, at the moment the sun rises, there is one called the Great Zombie, the Harm-Doer. At the time of autumn, it enters into the trail of the sun's rays, and whomever those rays touch — touching them through the net of light — they become afflicted with disease. In every direction for a hundred leagues,
by my cutting them all down,
what can the spirits do to me?
I have crushed all who harbored hatred.

By whatever power all the buddhas hold,
and by whatever power the arhats hold,

By the constant splendor of the noble Buddha,

The ritual is performed by reciting it sixty-one times, and knots are tied in a red cord of kaimeру thread, clean wool, and similar materials. Likewise, sprinkling with water is to be done. For all spirits, and those who cause forgetfulness, those who cause mental distraction and so forth, those who administer poison, those who cause poisoning, and for beings with two legs and beings with four legs — this is the supreme means of accomplishing universal protection.

When the Blessed One had spoken these words, the venerable Ānanda, together with the gods, humans, asuras, and gandharvas, rejoiced and praised what the Blessed One had said.

The seven proper names of the animated corpses are rendered from the Tibetan as descriptive epithets rather than transliterated, since the Tibetan forms are themselves already translations or calques from Sanskrit and the underlying Sanskrit names are not recoverable with certainty from the Tibetan alone. The term "ro langs" literally means "corpse that has risen" and corresponds to what in the Tibetan zombie or vetāla tradition is a reanimated body animated by a malevolent spirit; "animated corpse" is used throughout as the most precise rendering. The Sanskrit title in the colophon reads Saptavetāḍaka, meaning "seven vetālas," a vetāla being a spirit that inhabits a corpse; this confirms the rendering. The mantra syllables throughout this passage are transliterated Indic material embedded in the Tibetan text. They have been transcribed as closely as possible into standard romanization following the Tibetan phonetic rendering, without emendation, as is conventional for dhāraṇī texts. No claim is made that these represent recoverable Sanskrit originals in every case; some syllables appear to be purely ritual phonemes.

The term rendered "cord of kemo-ruka fiber" translates a compound where "ke mo ru ka" appears to be a transliteration of an Indic plant or material name rather than a Tibetan word; the identity of this fiber is uncertain.

The passage ends mid-sentence, as the source text provided breaks off at that point. The phrase rendered "raises all risen corpses" refers to an awareness-mantra that causes reanimated corpses (Tibetan ro langs, Sanskrit vetāla or vetālika) to rise and stand upright — a category of harmful sorcery this mantra is said to counteract.

The term "kumbhāṇḍa" (Tibetan grul bum) denotes a class of spirits associated with the retinue of the southern Great King Virūḍhaka.

"Vetāla" (Tibetan tshor ba pa) is a class of spirit that inhabits and reanimates corpses; it also appears as a proper category in the list immediately above.

"Tosala" and "Mudgara forest-dweller" appear to be names of specific regional or sectarian mantra-wielding traditions; they are left as transliterations since no standard identification is certain.

The repeated phrase "the awareness-mantra made by X is cut" employs a ritual declaration of nullification, standard in apotropaic dhāraṇī literature, countering mantras of every conceivable source. The names Paropāṭi and Śerudro appear to be transliterations of obscure spirit-entity names and are rendered phonetically as they appear in the Tibetan; their Sanskrit originals are uncertain.
