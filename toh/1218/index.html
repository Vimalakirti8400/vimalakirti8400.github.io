About this text

The Means of Accomplishment of Glorious Hevajra, known in Sanskrit as Śrī Hevajra Sādhana and in Tibetan as Dpal dgyes pa rdo rje'i sgrub thabs, is a tantric meditation manual devoted to the deity Hevajra and his consort Nairātmyā. Opening with a verse of homage to the two deities and an acknowledgment of the kindness of the vajra master, it sets out the ritual procedure by which a practitioner meditates on and seeks to accomplish Hevajra. It describes the preliminary requirements: generating the altruistic resolve for the sake of all beings, serving the guru, entering the Hevajra maṇḍala and receiving empowerment in proper sequence, and keeping the tantric pledges. It then turns to the practice itself, which is undertaken with a consort in a charnel ground or another suitable place. This work is Toh 1218 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the large class of sādhanas, or practice texts, that Indian authors composed as commentarial and ritual literature on the Hevajra cycle of the highest yoga tantras.

Translation

In the language of India: śrī hevajra sādhanaṃ. In the language of Tibet: The Means of Accomplishment of Glorious Hevajra.

Homage to glorious Hevajra.

To glorious Hevajra
And to Nairātmyā I bow with reverence.
Through the kindness of the vajra master
I shall explain the rite of his sādhana.

First of all the yogin generates the resolve for the sake of all sentient beings and properly serves the guru, the Blessed One. He enters the maṇḍala of glorious Hevajra according to the rite and receives empowerment in the proper sequence. Properly authorized, he obtains the utterly pure and unerring wisdom of suchness, finds the signs, and keeps the pledges of the five nectars and the rest. One who wishes to accomplish glorious Hevajra stays with a consort in a charnel ground or another pleasing place, and makes offerings, torma and so on. Then he views a blue syllable hūṃ upon a sun arisen at his own heart. From that hūṃ five kinds of light rays radiate throughout space. With those rays he summons the Blessed One arisen from the vajra, blue in color, with arms and hand emblems as described below: sixteen-armed, or six-armed, or four-armed, or even two-armed. He embraces his own consort, has three eyes, and his tawny hair stands upright. He wears the six seals and is surrounded by eight goddesses, and his forms radiate out and gather back in. Having drawn him into the space in front, one should meditate on him.

In front of him is Gaurī, black, holding in her hand the moon of bodhicitta. In the south is Caurī, the color of madder, holding in her hand the flower called "self-arisen sun." Behind is Vetālī, yellow, holding a vajra water vessel. In the north is Ghasmarī, like emerald, holding the secret medicine. In the northeast, the corner of Īśāna, is Pukkasī, blue, holding various kinds of flesh. In the southeast, the corner of fire, is Śavarī, white, holding a corpse in her hand. In the southwest, the corner of the rākṣasas, is Caṇḍālī, pale red, sounding a small drum. In the northwest, the corner of Vāyu, is Ḍombinī, multicolored, embracing her own body.

Having viewed in space the body of the Blessed One in this way, radiating and gathering, one worships him properly with the special outer, inner, secret, and suchness offerings, through light radiating from the seed syllable that rests on the sun at one's heart. Then in his presence one should perform the confession of misdeeds and the rest:

"Whatever sinful deeds I have committed, accumulated through mistaken conceptions throughout beginningless saṃsāra, all of them I confess in the presence of the Blessed One, and from now on I will restrain myself. I rejoice in all the virtue of all sentient beings, however much there is, and I dedicate it to unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. Furthermore, I go for refuge to the buddha Hevajra, in whom all three realms are of one taste in the form of great bliss; to the Dharma he taught, mantra, mudrā, maṇḍala and so on; and to the assembly of goddesses who abide as the retinue in his circle."

One should make the mind firm as the very nature of emptiness endowed with the supreme of all aspects, which is the cause of working for the benefit of all sentient beings. Then one should cultivate great loving-kindness toward all sentient beings, as toward an only child. After that, seeing the helpless beings sunk in the ocean of conceptual thought, one should make firm the nature of great compassion that wishes to deliver them all. The mantrin who has thus attained great joy should regard all imagined objects with equanimity, and then recite the mantra of suchness, the essence that gathers all things without remainder: oṃ śūnyatā jñāna vajra svabhāva ātmako'haṃ. After this one should meditate on the meaning of the mantra: oneself and the three realms are devoid of essential nature.

Then, through recollecting one's former aspiration, one should see in space, in the middle of a sun disk arisen from raṃ, a vajra fence with a mass of flames as vast as one wishes, arisen from the light rays of a three-pronged crossed vajra that is of the nature of five lights and has arisen from the syllable hūṃ. Within it one visualizes a vajra net that dispels obstructors. In its center one should meditate on wisdom, white, shaped like the letter e, pervading space. On it is the earth maṇḍala arisen from laṃ, square and green. On that is the water maṇḍala born from vaṃ, round and white. On that is the fire maṇḍala born from raṃ, triangular and red. On that is the wind maṇḍala born from yaṃ, shaped like a half moon, blue in color. Then one sees consciousness resting in its center.

From these, once they are settled, one should meditate in a mere instant on the maṇḍala, the palace of the wheel, shining with light. One imagines it as produced from mind: square, with four doors, beautified by eight pillars, adorned with nets, half-nets, archways and the like. In its center one sees a curved knife and a skull cup, arisen from the two seed syllables hūṃ and aṃ placed between a moon and a sun joined face to face, their centers marked by the two seed syllables of the deities. From the complete transformation of all these, one should see oneself in the form of glorious Hevajra, blue in color, eight-faced, sixteen-armed and four-legged, together with Nairātmyā, of one taste in the great bliss of supreme joy, filled with passion through mutual union.

Then with these, oṃ padma sukha dhara mahā rāga sukhaṃ dada catur ānanda bhaga viśva hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ karyaṃ kuruṣṭa me and oṃ vajra mahā dveṣa catura ānanda dāyaka khamukhe karāso nātha hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ karyaṃ kuruṣva me, one should bless the vajra and the lotus for the outer act of passion.

Then, through the union of churning and being churned, one should emanate the wheel of deities. At the eastern and the other doors, in order, are Brahmā, Indra, Upendra, and Rudra. Likewise in the corners beginning with Īśāna's are Yama, Kubera, Nairṛti, and Vemacitrin. In this way one should meditate on the eight seats. On them one sees the nature of the eight seed syllables gaṃ caṃ vaṃ ghaṃ vaṃ śaṃ laṃ ḍaṃ, each placed between a moon and a sun joined face to face.

From the transformation of those, one should imagine the emblems of Gaurī and the others: a curved knife, a small drum, a tortoise, a snake, a lion, a monk, a wheel, and a vajra. On top of these, too, are the eight seed syllables as their marks. When each of them has transformed into one, one should bring Gaurī and the others to completion.

Then, at the eastern door, one should set Gaurī. She is the nature of the aggregate of form and arises from the syllable gaṃ. She is blue, holds a curved knife in her right hand and a rohita fish in her left, and treads on Brahmā.

At the southern door one should set Caurī. She is the nature of the aggregate of feeling and arises from the syllable caṃ. She is the color of madder, holds a small drum in her right hand and a pig in her left, and treads on Indra.

At the western door one should imagine Vetālī. She is formed from the aggregate of perception and born from the syllable vaṃ. She is the color of refined gold, holds a tortoise in her right hand and a lotus vessel in her left, and treads on Upendra.

At the northern door one should behold Ghasmarī. She is the nature of the aggregate of formations and born from the syllable ghaṃ. She is like an emerald, holds a skull cup in her right hand, and treads on Rudra.

In the corner of Īśāna one should meditate on Pukkasī. She is the nature of the earth element and born from the syllable vaṃ. She is blue, holds a lion in her right hand and an axe in her left, and treads on Yama.

In the corner of Agni one should meditate on Śabarī. She is the nature of the water element and arises from the syllable śaṃ. She is white, holds a monk in her right hand and a fan in her left, and treads on Kubera.

In the corner of Nairṛti one should meditate on Caṇḍālī. She is the nature of the fire element and born from the syllable laṃ. She is the color of the sky, holds a wheel in her right hand and a skull cup in her left, and treads on a rākṣasa.

In the corner of Vāyu one should meditate on Ḍombinī. She is the nature of the wind element and arises from the syllable ḍaṃ. She is many-colored, holds a vajra in her right hand, makes the threatening gesture with her left, and treads on an asura.

One should meditate on all of them as three-eyed, with bared fangs, their tawny hair standing upright, naked, bearing the five mudrās, abiding in a dance with the half-crossed posture, and arisen from the awakening mind that has streamed forth.

At the close of this, the Blessed One, utterly enraptured, melts together with his consort, joined in one taste in supreme great bliss, and comes to abide in the form of a seed. Then the four goddesses, Pukkasī and the others, knowing that they are without their lord, are seized by great distress. Tormented by grief, they rouse the Blessed One with vajra songs that awaken his mind:

"With a heart of compassion, Blessed One, arise!
Protect me, Pukkasī, completely.
Abandon the samādhi of emptiness
And, longing for great bliss, unite with me.

Without you here, I die.
Abandon the nature of emptiness;
To fulfill what Śabarī needs,
O Hevajra, arise!

You who have overpowered the world with desire,
Lord, why do you dwell in emptiness?
Without you here, I am lost in every direction;
I, Caṇḍālī, make this plea.

I seek your heart:
Arise, like an illusion!
Lord of beings, I am Ḍombinī;
Do not let your compassion's stream be cut!"

As though awakened from sleep by this song of suchness, the Blessed One arises from his nectar form, which is like a melted moon. Above that, upon the two emblems that arose from the two seeds, they are marked by the two seed syllables hūṃ and aṃ. When all of these, together with the sun disc, have transformed into one, in the center of the sun disc one should emanate oneself as the Blessed One, glorious Hevajra, arisen from the syllable hūṃ, in perfect union with Nairātmyā, who has arisen from the syllable aṃ.

His body is deep blue and exceedingly fierce. He has three eyes, his tawny hair blazes upward, and his head is marked with a crossed vajra. Above his forehead he is adorned with a garland of five skulls. His first face is blue, with a wrathful frown of knitted brows; the right face is white; the left face is red; the further two left faces are blue, and likewise the further two right faces are blue; the upper face is smoke-colored and snarling. All these faces bare their fangs and have three eyes. In his eight right hands, resting in eight lotus vessels, are, in order: an elephant, a horse, a donkey, an ox, a camel, a man, a dog, and a buffalo. Likewise in his eight left hands are Pṛthivī the earth goddess, Varuṇa, Agni, Vāyu, the Moon, the Sun, Yama, and Dhanada (Kubera). His body is adorned with a garland of freshly severed heads. He bears the six mudrās, is naked, and stands with his right leg extended. One meditates on him abiding on the sun disc, sporting in a dance that tramples evenly the four māras: the māra of the aggregates, the māra of the afflictions, the Lord of Death, and the māra who is a son of the gods.

Nairātmyā is blue, and in her two hands she holds a curved knife and a skull cup. With her right arm and the other she embraces him closely. She is naked, bears the five mudrās, has three eyes, and her head is adorned with a garland of skulls. Her body is adorned with a garland of dried male heads. Her left leg is extended. Through her union with the Blessed One she delights in supreme bliss; one should meditate on her as the Blessed Lady.

One should also behold the wheel of the maṇḍala of the assembly of vidyās just as it is, and meditate that the mass of one's own light rays completely pervades all of space.

Then light rays stream forth from one's own seed syllable abiding at one's heart. Those rays pervade the whole of the three realms and are then drawn back into that very syllable. Again they stream forth across the expanse of the sky, and one invites the wisdom wheel directly into presence. When it has arrived before one, one worships and praises it properly, then causes it to enter one's own samaya wheel. Once it has entered, one should generate the pride of being one with the deity.

Then one properly worships, with a variety of distinct offerings, the tathāgatas, bodhisattvas, vidyā goddesses, wrathful kings and the rest, who have arisen from the mass of light rays of one's own body. One should meditate that they confer empowerment upon oneself with vases filled with the five nectars.

Thus, in their proper order, from the four moments of variety, maturation, consummation, and the moment beyond characteristics, the characteristics of joy, supreme joy, the joy of cessation, and innate joy become manifest. From the purification of the four, laughing, gazing, embracing, and union, one should receive the four empowerments: the master empowerment, the secret empowerment, the wisdom-knowledge empowerment, and the empowerment of the great bliss of supreme joy.

Through the empowerment, each goddess is marked on the head by the lord of her own family. Thus the Lord of Mind, the Eternal One, Ratnasambhava, and Amitābha should seal Gaurī and the others. Aversion, delusion, pride, and attachment should mark the crowns of Pukkasī and the others. Together with the circle of the Blessed One, they are the nature of the five wisdoms.

Within this, the moon transformed from the vowels has the nature of mirror-like wisdom. The sun transformed from the consonants has the nature of the wisdom of equality. The seed syllable and the emblem that arise in their midst are discriminating wisdom. All of these transformed into one is the wisdom that accomplishes activities. The complete image is the utterly pure wisdom of the dharmadhātu. In this way one should resolve that the whole of the three realms has the form of the wheel of the maṇḍala.

One should understand that the deities have arisen from the awakening mind released, of one taste with the great bliss of supreme joy, together with such a wisdom consort. One should then imagine the eight goddesses, Gaurī and the others, entering in order into the eight faces of one's own body.

Having thus made the whole of the three realms into the single nature of method and wisdom, one seals each in its own place. On the head is the wheel of great bliss, a lotus with thirty-two petals. At its center one sees a white haṃ with white, radiant light. At the throat is the wheel of enjoyment, a lotus with sixteen petals; at its center is a red oṃ. At the heart is the wheel of Dharma, a lotus with eight petals; at its center is a blue hūṃ. At the navel is the wheel of emanation, a lotus with sixty-four petals; at its core one should meditate on an a having the nature of five lights. Having meditated properly on these wheels, one sends out the mass of their light rays into space and gathers it back, and dissolves it into them.

Then one should put on the armor of the six mudrās that are present on one's own body, whose nature is the tathāgatas:

The crown wheel is Akṣobhya;
The earrings have the nature of Amitābha;
The necklace is the Jewel Lord, Ratnasambhava;
In the bracelets abides Vairocana;
In the girdle dwells Amoghasiddhi;
In all the limbs moves Vajradhara.

Likewise:

The two eyes are Mohavajrī, the Vajrī of Delusion;
The ears are Dveṣavajrī, the Vajrī of Aversion;
The nose is Mātsaryavajrī, the Vajrī of Avarice;
The mouth is Rāgavajrī, the Vajrī of Attachment;
Touch is Īrṣyāvajrī, the Vajrī of Envy;
The mind is the yoginī Nairātmyā.

These have become the samaya.

Then comes the mantra recitation for the various rites. As one utters the string of mantra syllables, the syllables come forth from the goddess's mouth and, together with light rays, enter one's own mouth. They come out through the vajra path, and one makes them descend into the lotus of one's own goddess. Then again they pass from the goddess's mouth into one's own mouth. In this way one should make the mantra circulate many times without interruption. According to the different kinds of rite, one makes the colors different, and thus one recites the mantra fully until one tires of it.

Then, at one's own heart, between the sun and the moon, one meditates on a body of blue form that sends out and gathers in. At the heart of Nairātmyā, too, one sees an aṃ upon a moon. One contemplates that the Blessed Lady, with the a and the moon disc melting together, enters the heart of the Blessed One.

Then, from the hūṃ amid the moon and sun at the heart of the Blessed One, masses of light rays of the vowels and consonants stream forth. Sent out and gathered back with the outgoing and incoming breath, they enter one's own reality and become one with it. At that time, abiding as the body of the seed syllable within the joined moon and sun, one reflects thus:

Without beginning and without end,
It is not saṃsāra, nor is it nirvāṇa;
It is not other, nor is it self:
This is the supreme great bliss.

Likewise, preceded by the articles of offering and torma, one should meditate on the seed syllable mingled with the moon and sun, in sequence: in the form of a ball of light whose nature is nectar, and as something like the tip of a lamp flame.

oṃ inda jama jala jakkha bhūta bahni bāu rākkha canda sujja māda pāppa tala pātāle aṭṭa sappa svāhā idaṃ bali bhuñja jighaṃ phulla dhūppa māṃsa piṃga aṃba sappa kajja sadā khanti khuṇi pheṭ gāda oṃ akāro mukhaṃ sarva dharmāṇāṃ ādyanutpanna tvat oṃ āḥ hū phaṭ svāhā

This is the torma mantra for all the bhūtas.

oṃ aṣṭānanāya piṃgordha keśa vartmane caturbiṃśati netrāya ṣoḍaśa bhujāya kṛṣṇa jīmūta vapuṣe kapāla mālānekā dhāriṇe ādhmata krūra cittāya ardhendu daṃṣṭṛṇe oṃ māraya māraya kāraya kāraya garjjaya garjjaya tarjjaya tarjjaya śoṣaya śoṣaya sapta sāgarān bandha bandha nāga aṣṭakāna gṛhṇa gṛhṇa sarvva śatrūṃna ha hā hi hī hu hū he hai ho hau haṃ haḥ phaṭ svāhā

This is the root mantra of the Blessed One.

oṃ deva picu vajra hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā

This is the heart mantra.

oṃ vajra kartari hevajrāya hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ

This is the near-heart mantra.

oṃ a ka ca ṭa ta ba ya śa svāhā

This is the mantra for stirring a city.

At the time of the rite of offerings and torma, both at the beginning and at the end, one should sing the song of the supreme samaya:

kolla i re tthi apolā
mummu ṇi re kakkolā
ghaṇe kṛpi taho bājja i
karuṇe kiya bina rolā
tahiṃ bala khājja yi
gāḍhemma aṇā bijja yi
hele kāliñjara paṇiya yi
dundura bājji a i
cahu samā katthurī sitlā
kappura lāyī a i
māla indhaṇa sāliṃja
tahiṃ bharu khāhi a i
phreṃ khaṇa kheṭa karente
suddha asuddha na muṇi a i
nirāṃsu aṃge caḍāpi a i
tahiṃ ja sarāva paṇi a i
malaya je kunduru bāṭa i
ḍiṇḍima tahinna bājjiya yi

In this way, especially while staying in retreat, and at the time of the feast offering, the mantra practitioner holding vajra and bell should sing this song very slowly, in order to draw near all the accomplishments.

Following the sequence just as it was taught, within the circle of the maṇḍala one should meditate on the Blessed One with one face and two arms, holding a vajra in the right hand and in the left a skull cup and a khaṭvāṅga, naked, his right leg extended, trampling a corpse.

Likewise, the four-armed form holds a vajra in the right hand and a skull cup in the left, and with the remaining two arms embraces his own consort.

In the same way as these, the three-faced, six-armed form has a red left face and a white right face. In his left hands are a trident and a bell, in his right hands a vajra and a curved knife, and with the remaining two arms he embraces his own consort. His Blessed Lady embraces the Blessed One equally, with hands holding a curved knife and a skull cup together.

These two-armed, four-armed and six-armed forms are all blue in color, three-eyed, their tawny hair standing upright, adorned with the six mudrās, the right leg extended, trampling a corpse; so one should imagine them. However, one who does not meditate on the eight-faced form should still meditate on placing the wheel of deities within one's own body.

oṃ trailokyākṣepa hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā
This is the mantra of the two-armed form.

oṃ jvāla jvāla bhyoṃ hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā
This is the mantra of the four-armed form.

oṃ kiṭi kiṭi vajra hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā
This is the mantra of the six-armed form.

The remaining rites are to be performed as before.

Furthermore, this should be understood:

The eyes of compassion are red;
The blue limbs are the mind of loving-kindness;
The four means of attraction are explained as the four legs;
The eight faces are the eight liberations;
The arms are the sixteen emptinesses;
The bone ornaments are the five kinds of mudrā;
Wrathful, he tames the wicked.
Flesh is explained as Pukkasī;
Blood is to be understood as Śabarī;
Caṇḍālī is the form of semen;
Ḍombinī is marrow and fat;
The skin is the seven limbs of awakening;
The bones are the four noble truths.
Having thus made his resolve, the mantra practitioner
Moves freely in the yoga of the deity.

Then one should make aspirational prayers:

Born of the family and never falling away,
A keeper of samaya, a holder of Hevajra,
Compassionate and devoted to the guru:
Birth after birth, may I become such.
With vajra in hand, sounding the bell,
Proclaiming the profound Dharma,
Possessing the essence of union with wisdom:
Birth after birth, may I become such.

This brief sādhana
I have written through the force of faith.
For me, whose intelligence is very poor,
May those who know reality be patient.

Devoted to the lotus of the guru's feet and loving toward beings,
Rejoicing greatly in the lineage of the moon-drop,
Victorious by myself over the enemy of manifest pride,
Having found the sign of the deity, I have written this.

Through whatever merit has been gained
By composing this means of accomplishing
The hard-to-find Hevajra,
May the world attain the state of Vajradhara.

The Sādhana of Glorious Hevajra, composed by the master Padma, is complete.

Translator's notes

The eight goddesses are identified by their positions with the standard Hevajra retinue. Some Tibetan names here are unusual renderings: spos mo for Pukkasī, dur khrod ma ("charnel-ground woman") for Śavarī, and gtum mo for Caṇḍālī. The phrase rendered "a sun arisen at his own heart" (snying gar las byung ba'i nyi ma) is somewhat elliptical and may imply a seed syllable from which the sun arises. "The six seals" are the six bone ornaments. Upendra is Viṣṇu and Rudra is Śiva. Nor bdag ("Lord of Wealth") is rendered Kubera. The eight seats of the gods are the four Māras and worldly gods trampled by the deities. The second emblem, given here as "a small drum," is written as a word meaning "small pebble" in the Tibetan; the Hevajra system assigns Caurī a ḍamaru drum, and the same word appears as the object in her right hand, so the drum is taken as intended. "Fan" renders a word meaning "cooling implement" in Śabarī's left hand; the exact object is not certain. The phrase rendered "utterly enraptured" uses a word that ordinarily means "greedy" or "miserly"; it appears to express intense absorption in passion. Some lines of the goddesses' song, notably "You who have overpowered the world with desire" and "I seek your heart," are compressed in the Tibetan and are rendered by their most natural sense. Vetālī is said to be "formed from" rather than "the nature of" the aggregate of perception, following the wording of the Tibetan, which may be corrupt here. The fourth of the four moments is written as "the characteristic of aspect" in the Tibetan. In the Hevajra system the fourth moment is the one beyond characteristics (vilakṣaṇa), and it is rendered that way here. Likewise the third joy is written "the joy of aspect" in the Tibetan. It is rendered by its standard Hevajra name, the joy of cessation (viramānanda). The Tibetan name of the second group of goddesses, led by one called "sbos mo," is taken to be Pukkasī, who heads the intermediate-direction goddesses of the Hevajra maṇḍala. "The Lord of Mind" and "the Eternal One" are translated literally as family lords; they probably stand for Akṣobhya and Vairocana. The phrase about Vajradhara "moving" in all the limbs renders the Tibetan "spyod" literally. "The Blessed One" in this passage is Hevajra. The colophon names the author as "master Padma." He is generally identified with Saroruha (Padmavajra), who wrote a well-known Hevajra sādhana. The line "a keeper of samaya, a holder of Hevajra" corresponds to the Sanskrit "samayī hevajradhṛk."
