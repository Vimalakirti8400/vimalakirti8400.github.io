About this text

The Interwoven Praise, known in Sanskrit as the Miśrakastotra and in Tibetan as Spel mar bstod pa, is a devotional hymn addressed to the Buddha as the Omniscient One. It opens with the voice of someone sinking in the bottomless ocean of saṃsāra, devoured by the sea monsters of attachment and the other afflictions, who asks to whom they should turn for refuge, whose teaching they should follow, and whom they should praise and serve. It answers that refuge belongs to the Buddha alone, the one entirely free of faults and complete in every good quality, who exhausted his faults and cultivated his virtues for the sake of others' higher rebirth and liberation rather than for himself. The work is traditionally attributed to the master Dignāga, and its title is generally understood to reflect the way it weaves together verses of praise, drawing on the celebrated hymns of Mātṛceṭa. It is Toh 1150 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the collection of praises, the class of treatises devoted to hymns honoring the Buddha.

Translation

In the language of India: miśrakastotranāma
In the language of Tibet: The Interwoven Praise

Homage to the Omniscient One.

I dwell within the ocean of saṃsāra,
whose depths have no bottom,
my body devoured by the terrible sea monsters
of attachment and the rest:
to whom shall I go for refuge today?
As long as I still have a mind,
in whose teaching shall I abide,
whom shall I praise, and whom shall I serve and honor?

The one in whom all faults
are utterly and entirely absent,
and in whom, in every aspect,
all good qualities abide;
the one whose faults were exhausted
in order to remove the harm that befalls others,
who cultivated good qualities for others' higher rebirth and liberation,
not for his own sake:
if one has any mind at all,
it is right to go for refuge to him alone,
to praise him, to honor him,
and to abide in his teaching.

What is a fault is what harms others;
what is a good quality is what overcomes the others.
A fault is the ruin of oneself;
a good quality sustains one's own welfare.
The Omniscient One possesses all good qualities,
and these never decline;
and faults, together with their habitual tendencies,
are absent in the one and only Protector.

Because his conduct is beyond reproach,
the Sage is established as flawless.
Unless they have cast aside the Dharma renowned for bringing happiness,
people of bright virtue do not revile him.
In the deeds of the Blessed One,
of his body, his speech and his mind,
not even someone filled with hatred
can find an opening for blame that accords with the Dharma.

If I do not take up, with certainty, words
that capture even a portion of his great qualities,
and do not praise the Teacher,
I shall be ruined as if born in a state without leisure.
Like a turtle whose neck slips
into the hole of a yoke adrift in the ocean,
I have obtained a human life
together with the great feast of the true Dharma;
much of it has already been cut away,
and at its end it remains prey to the flaws of the age.
If my tongue does not become purposeful,
then I shall have been cheated.
With this very tongue of mine,
which follows in the wake of impermanence
and stands in peril of the openings of karma,
why should I not extract its essence?

If one would not praise him
because one has not grasped the limit of his qualities,
then even Śāriputra and the others
could surely never praise him.
Thus, though I know the domain of the Sage's qualities
to be beyond all number,
out of regard for my own aim,
I shall gather my thoughts and speak of one part of it.

One who learns from another,
and one who, being lesser, has not trained well:
even when their faults are spent, they still are learners,
but you, in truth, have nothing left to learn.
You whose qualities know no end
in their number or their power,
you of wondrous and measureless deeds,
Self-Arisen One, to you I bow.

By the power of counting qualities
one can speak of a learner,
and one can measure what has a limit,
but not in the presence of the one beyond learning.
If there is no end to saying "this much,"
what could one say by declaring "it is like this"?
Yet, thinking it merit for the sake of your qualities,
boldness has arisen in me.

This is the first chapter, called "Introduction to the Praise," from the Praise of the Buddha, the Blessed One, called "Eloquence Born of Faith."

Those who are at ease and lack compassion
never weigh whether to act or not to act;
being in thrall to their own happiness,
they are bound fast to that alone.
You cast away the conceptual thought
that wonders, "Shall I act or not?"
and saying, "These beings are in misery,"
you yourself took on the task.

Having cast compassion aside,
others act when others entrust them;
some, intent on benefiting themselves,
set out for the sake of their own happiness.
You are a benefactor unasked;
you love as though you had no reason;
you are a friend to those you never knew,
a kinsman to those with no tie to you.

For the sake of the perfection of generosity,
having taken up beings with compassion,
your delight growing at those who begged,
your body hair bristling again and again,
you bestowed even your own flesh;
what need is there to speak of other things?
Benefactor, even your very life
you gave away to those who begged.

Having given up self-regard for yourself
and taken others as yourself,
like one trading grass for great and precious jewels,
with a delight that never waned,
with your own body and your life
you ransomed, hundreds of times over,
the bodies and the lives
of embodied beings seized by executioners.

Worldly people, whose thinking clings to a self,
have no mind of compassion;
from fear of suffering or desire for happiness
they take up moral discipline and guard it.
Not from dread of lower destinies,
nor to win some destiny you desired,
but solely from pure intention
did you cultivate moral discipline.

Since it is left incomplete by outer conditions
and lacks excellence in one's own application,
even with a thoroughly pure intention
the perfection of moral discipline does not become pure.
Always forsaking all crookedness,
always relying on the upright and steadfast,
through utterly pure deeds
you became the one foundation and support.

As for the cause of the perfection of patience:
though it should be honored through the power of compassion,
others practice it in dread of future suffering,
since harming others is harming themselves.
But you, with a joyful heart,
held out against the afflictions
of those tormented by many sufferings
and cherished afflicted people.
Even toward those who tormented you
you gave no thought to their wrongs;
wishing for the perfection of patience,
you looked only to the kindness done to you.

The delight you felt
in giving up body and life for the sake of others,
such delight did not arise
even in creatures spared from death.
To be pleased when benefactors honor one
I do not count a great wonder;
but the delight you had, Protector,
that I deem a wonder.

Not merely a few times
you were hacked apart, yet heedless of the pain,
Lord, you felt compassion
even for the beings who served as your executioners.
And when the executioner himself
cut you down with his sword,
seeing it as serving your quest for awakening,
you remained in loving-kindness.

Your precious mind
is the seed of perfect awakening;
Hero, you alone know its essence;
other people cannot even guess at it.

Even having become such yourself,
seeing that beings are always in suffering,
you examined one by one the qualities of a buddha
and how diligence should be applied;
thinking, "Without undertaking hardships,
the rank so hard to find cannot be won,"
and for that very reason paying no heed even to yourself,

you strengthened your diligence well.
Having found qualities of distinction
and wholly abandoned every fault,
though you shouldered the burden for the sake of beings,
your mind was never once forgetful;
in making yourself ever more excellent
you never slackened even once.
Therefore your distinctions
cannot be ranked in order, high and low.
How could the perfection of meditative concentration
fail to follow in the wake of such purity?
You, by whose wisdom views and pride
were cleared away so that they no longer appear,
because your mind is always
bound up with great compassion,
were not attached even to the supreme bliss
that comes as the fruit of samādhi.

Some non-Buddhists (tīrthikas)
hold that the highest good is reached through enjoyments.
You yourself did not, one-sidedly,
forbid enjoyments to others.
Yet any happiness not shared with others,
however lavish it may be,
for one of holy conduct like you
is harmful rather than of benefit.

Your wisdom is unequalled;
because your gnosis is utterly pure,
how could even the many animals
fail to follow after you?
From what was mixed, good sayings alone,
a faultless essence, however partial,
you accepted and kept in mind;
bad sayings you shunned entirely, like poison.
Great good sayings, even half a verse,
you sought at the cost of your life;
who, to make their wisdom grow,
would not follow after you?
You alone discerned it fully.
You who know the precious essence,
whenever you met with good sayings at the cost of your life,
in birth after birth
showed heroism for the sake of awakening.

Even for beings of exalted diligence
it takes countless eons;
it cannot be reached by people
who are lame and lacking in practice.
Therefore, for three countless eons,
as you strove in this way,
keeping diligence alone as your companion,
you attained the unsurpassed state.

This is the second chapter, called Praise by Way of the Causes, from Praise of the Buddha, the Blessed One, called "Eloquence Born of Faith."

You regard all your qualities as slight;
when called upon, you are never asleep;
what you have undertaken you hold firm,
and your mind is set upon excellence.
You feel no envy toward the high,
you do not lord it over the low,
and you do not compete with equals;
thus you have become the chief of the world.

You never falter in accomplishing the difficult.
Worldly things do not last long,
are mostly tainted by attachment,
and are very meager in their fruits.
You delight in the causes of good qualities,
not in their fruits.
By that very right accomplishment
you reached the far shore of good qualities.

"The state of Lord of Sages is won through hardship":
people who speak this way,
poor in compassion, give it no thought.
But you took up unwavering diligence,
and just as even the dust of your feet
became a source from which merit springs and spreads,
so through all that you made use of
you perfected yourself.

Through the noble path still within the world,
and through diligence and wisdom too,
even at the end of your former lives,
by what had truly arisen in you,
you weakened faults and tore them out by the root,
increased your qualities and refined them.
By that excellent way, therefore,
you attained the supreme accomplishment surpassingly.

Seeing that beings, always weary,
without protector, begging and without happiness,
are ruled by many afflictions
that never abate,
you destroyed faults in every way,
so thoroughly that in your own mindstream
not even a remnant of their habitual tendencies
was left behind.

Seeing as foremost the accomplishing
of the welfare of others,
and holding your own qualities to be lacking,
you, and no other, took on the work of diligence,
so that in others not even a likeness
of those qualities is to be found;
thus, gathering and gathering,
you brought good qualities to completion in yourself.

All the examples found in the world
are small and inferior, without sustaining power,
blameworthy and tainted by attachment,
unsatisfying, without a good end,
common and lacking in radiance,
obstructive and obscured,
temporary and partial,
and very easy to find.
Yours are many, best of the best,
nourishing all and cutting away stains,
satisfying, ending in happiness,
possessed of an uncommon radiance.
Your unsurpassed qualities
have no obstruction and no conceptual thought;
since they are stable and unbroken,
how could there be any example for them?

Your intent, which even the śrāvakas of supreme intellect
could not fathom through eons,
how could the ocean, whose depth the asuras have found,
be anything like it?
When one examines the expanse of your mind,
which has neither bottom nor far shore,
even the very depth of the ocean
looks plainly like the hoofprint of a cow.

Unattached to the extremes of desire,
it is faulted by no one at all;
your word, which is steadfast,
who could ever measure up to it?
When one contemplates your steadfastness,
unshaken by any phenomena whatever,
even the firmness of the earth
is as slight as the tip of a śirīṣa flower's filament.

Outsiders, wishing for happiness,
lie down in the wake of the sun;
but you are not like the sun;
such words speak only of a shared feature.
O Sage, when the light of your gnosis
destroys the cataract of ignorance,
even the sun, beside it,
does not serve so much as a firefly.

Through purity of application and of intention,
even in the past you had no faults;
since your conduct is beyond conception,
who could conceive of buddhahood itself?
Beside the purity of your speech, your mind and your deeds,
the purity of the autumn moon,
of water and of the sky
seems to be stained.

Who can measure by way of examples
good qualities that are immeasurable?
In setting them beside your distinctions,
there are many words, and words alone.
Whatever is excellent in the world,
even with all of it gathered there together,
the afflicted qualities of the world
cannot rival the qualities of a buddha.

Those who are like the rhinoceros horn,
and the disciples who follow you,
equal you in liberation alone,
not in your wondrous qualities.
The precious Dharma by attaining which
you became supreme,
were it compared with you, and you with it,
that would be mere comparison back and forth;
or else, even that is not your equal.
For that the two are equal
no master of valid cognition can declare,
and so no one at all will come to realize it.

Whatever little has been said
by comparing in mere generalities
is only the speaker's own delight,
a device, and nothing more than flattery.

This is the third chapter, called Praise of the Incomparable, from Praise of the Buddha, the Blessed One, called "Eloquence Born of Faith."

Beyond all beings, you are victorious over Māra;
you surpass whatever inference can measure.
Even your hearers (śrāvakas),
abiding in equipoise, prevail over Māra.

That you triumphed over Māra
and also bring all beings to faith:
reflecting on my own nature,
I have seen nothing else so great.

Though you do not rest on force,
you have the kind of power by which one ought to conquer;
and since by it other existences are conquered,
the servants and envoys of Māra,
who strove in hatred to lead you astray,
you brought low, many though they were.
For one who rests in that special state,
through the labor of patience, this is no great wonder.

Even when you met with conditions for distraction,
you relied on no one else;
throughout beginningless saṃsāra
you have found delight in yourself alone.

That on the very night
on which you subdued the māras
you abandoned the very great afflictions:
that itself is marvelous.

You who, though it is beyond conception,
have so supremely conquered the afflictions,
what need is there to mention your victory over the non-Buddhists (tīrthikas),
a thing you share with other people?

That the sun, with its thousand garlands of rays,
dispels the darkness, and that you, O hero,
have won victory over the non-Buddhists:
what is there to wonder at in that?

Possessed of attachment, aversion and delusion,
through these very things beings remain in existence;
but for those who have abandoned the latent tendencies,
when they engage in debate, it is with discernment.

Free of attachment, the attached,
free of aversion, the hateful,
free of delusion, the deluded:
with these three you have always tamed those three.

That one abides in the state of equanimity
is no wonder, nor is it joy.
Because of engaging and turning back,
this is what is called a wonder:
though you highly praise the true Dharma
and censure what is not the true Dharma,
toward the noble and toward the base
you have neither attachment nor anger.

Toward the arhats, who fulfill the hopes
that the mind has long and fervently desired,
and toward the non-Buddhists, who with unreasonable disputes
obstruct the nature of reality,
if your mind does not turn
to attachment or to anger,
what is there that could praise you?

Since, for the sake of what brings about growth,
you take no thought even for your life;
since what you see and what you hear
is to you as though not seen and not heard;
since you are not attached even to good qualities,
and have no craving even for those who possess them,
oh, the strength of your mind
is utterly and perfectly pure!

Because body and mind follow one another,
states of grief, joy and the like
are surely obscured as well by hope and fear.
But since your senses are utterly clear
and never fail at any time,
your mind, ever clear,
seems as if it were directly perceived.

Though, wanting very little,
you strive to conceal them,
since your faults are utterly and entirely exhausted,
since you are drawn forth by the aims of others,
and since your excellent nature is proclaimed
by your well-spoken words and your good conduct,
your intelligence and your intentions
are known to be pure even among the simple.

This is the fourth chapter, called "Praise of the Marvelous," from the Praise of the Buddha, the Blessed One, called "Eloquence Born of Faith."

Whoever, on seeing your body,
does not find the mind made clear with faith
has no eyes at all,
or else, though he has them, they serve no purpose.

Utterly peaceful, yet delightful to the mind;
blazing, yet doing no harm;
gentle, yet of great majesty:
whom does this body not bring to faith?

Whatever is pleasing to look at or calming,
even when such an object is found,
leaves all people free of attachment to it;
but this is supreme among all sights.

The one who has seen your body
a hundred times, and the one
who sees it for the first time:
it delights the eyes of both alike.

Even one whose mind is filled with hostility,
how could he fail to be gratified by the sight?
Even the hungry ghosts,
though they are not skilled in discerning distinctions,
because your body is beautiful,
of a nature one never tires of beholding,
in all who see it, again and again,
you bring joy to birth.

It is a wonder that you take no thought of weariness:
you act for aims in harmony with beings,
and when your activity becomes manifest,
you work your miracles without effort.

Because the qualities of the support
and the qualities of what is supported
befit one another, your body
and your qualities possess supreme perfection.

If the qualities did not dwell in you,
on what other could this body
of the Tathāgata rest?
It is adorned beyond all other men.

Apart from your body,
blazing with the excellent major and minor marks,
in what other could these qualities
of the Tathāgata abide so well?

The body surpassing all perfection,
unequaled by any other, that you attained,
through the distinction of what it supports
holds perfection all the more.

As if your body were saying
to the qualities it supports, "I am glorious,"
those qualities of yours seem to reply in turn,
"We are well arranged."

This is the fifth chapter, called "Praise of the Body," from the Praise of the Buddha, the Blessed One, called "Eloquence Born of Faith."

Slaughter, hunger and thirst, cold and heat,
the unruly, the revilers, and many more,
through three eons you bore without weariness,
and the sufferings of the lower realms as well.

All these beings, without distinction,
are bound by their afflictions.
You, to untie the afflictions of beings,
were bound for ages by compassion.

Because affliction is the cause of their bondage,
beings put up with their afflictions.
But that you, bound by compassion,
should endure it all is a wonder.

Shall I bow first to you,
or to that great compassion
by which you, knowing its faults,
remained so long in saṃsāra?

Thus, equanimous toward suffering,
you dwelt in solitary places;
yet to make all things perfectly clear,
you endured even that.

That you gave up the bliss of solitude
and dwelt among the multitude
was the doing of your compassion, long overdue,
as though it had found its opening at last.

Much of it resembles worldly conduct:
relying on future happiness,
with care for what is present now,
having examined sentient beings,

from within the lake of knowledge,
like a nāga, by your compassion
you were drawn from the wilds of calm abiding to the edges of towns
for the sake of those to be tamed.

With a mind attached to others,
there is nothing Queen Compassion will not do.
Or else, because you are attached to her,
you abandoned what accords with yourself,

and while abiding in supreme peace,
being possessed of compassion as your chief,
even the artistry of a dancer
is cast forth by your feet.

Before, not yet having found the ultimate,
your austerities were not like this.
But having attained buddhahood itself,
though you gave up your own calm abiding,

your miracles, your lion's roar,
and your proclaiming of your own qualities
are a display of your compassion,
which has no thought of self-gratification.

If this work for the sake of beings
never changes while beings remain,
and out of devotion never strays elsewhere,
how can it be so pitiless, so relentless?

Supremely good for the sake of others,
it is pitiless toward your own aims;
so toward you alone, Protector,
compassion showed no compassion.

Or else it is through it that you yourself
practiced hardships, your conduct never failing;
you looked to the aims of others
more than to your own.

Thus, as has at times been shown,
in order to accomplish the aims of others,
you were cast a hundred times to the directions
like a torma offering.

That which leads you into the unbefitting
crafts of a performer and the like,
and even into base deeds,
that very thing is not fitting.

Yet though it thus does you harm,
you do not count it as at fault,
so it seems that it acts
only in accord with your wishes.

Some do not let go, yet are not attached;
some are attached, yet do not know things as they are;
some, though they know things as they are,
are not in harmony.
But you let go, and yet do not let go;
you are attached, and yet,
knowing things as they are, you are in harmony.

The many fine deeds of that compassion,
who could ever tell them?

From the Praise of the Buddha, the Blessed One, called "Eloquence Born of Faith," this is the sixth chapter, called "Praise of Compassion."

Fine in its words and great in meaning,
true and pleasing to hear,
very clear, bearing meaning in both senses,
possessing both brevity and elaboration,
destroying afflictions and never harsh,
lucid and easily understood,
not confusing its varied meanings,
full of essence, and abundant too:
such is your speech.
Even if your enemies were to hear it,
who would not become convinced,
"He is the Omniscient One"?

Toward those attached to you, the indifferent,
and those who harm you out of hatred,
because you make them vomit up all their faults,
your mind is even toward them all.

Much speech is not pleasing as a whole;
a few contrived phrases are pleasing.
Because your speech accomplishes its aim,
all of it is nothing but well spoken.

Since, through differences of inclination,
disposition and faculty, intentions are diverse,
therefore, skilled in means,
you, the supreme tamer of persons, steer the reins.

When your speech is examined,
whatever in it is gentle or harsh,
and whatever has both,
all of it becomes of a single taste.

Developed over a long time
through perfect compassion and diligence,
your intent and true wisdom as well
are applied with effort toward all.

How wonderful! Your perfectly pure
deeds have fashioned it well;
by them this vessel
for precious speech has been made.

Because it is bound up with the faults of the vessel,
even such nectar for the ears
has its flavor stolen,
like the divine waters in the ocean.

Your face is most lovely to behold,
and the sweet words heard from it
are like nectar
dripping from the moon.

By teaching the unattractive
in its divisions, the bluish corpse and the rest,
and loving-kindness and compassion as well,
as antidotes matched to each temperament,
your speech is like a rain cloud
that wholly settles the dust of attachment;
and because it drives off the snake of aversion,
it is like the garuḍa.

Furthermore, by teaching
the many aspects of dependent origination,
and likewise that karma is one's own,
and selflessness, without distinction,
again and again it dispels
the dim sight of unknowing, and so is like the sun.
Because it shatters the mountain of pride,
it is like the weapon of Śakra (Indra).

Had you not spoken this,
who would come to understand your true words,
you, the Sugata, the Arhat, the Buddha,
thus perfect in realization?

Since you see the meaning, it does not deceive;
since you have no faults, it is consistent;
since it is well composed, it is easy to grasp:
your speech has three excellences.

It is profound and melodious,
easy to grasp and well composed,
in accord with those to be trained,
and, even toward the cruel, true and meaningful.

At first your speech
captivates the minds of those who listen;
then, when they reflect on it,
it also clears away the dust and the darkness.

You show that enjoyments are meager indeed,
you show birth and destruction too,
and, furthermore, to beings in others' power
you show the way of renunciation.

It brings relief to the destitute,
it makes the heedless afraid,
it makes the comfortable weary of the world:
thus your speech fits itself to all.

By the power of full ripening
it gives rise to wisdom whose nature is meditation;
and at the time of rising from that,
through wisdom whose nature is hearing and reflection,
it gladdens the learned,
enlarges the understanding of the middling,
and dispels the dim sight of the lowest:
thus this speech is healing for all people.

First it brings forth the truths,
the undefiled eye of wisdom;
then, by the distinctions of faculties,
it sets the paths of meditation and of no more learning
apart from that of seeing,
and leads beings close to nirvāṇa.
Your speech, which clears away faults,
also showers down a rain of good qualities.

Skilled in the means of training,
you tell the histories of former times;
yet even so, for the fortunate,
you never stray at all from the ultimate.

Your mind is unobstructed toward all,
and your intent is ever present;
therefore all your declarations
are without deceit toward anyone.

The words of your foremost śrāvakas
are veiled by not knowing
beings' lineage, ripeness and inclination;
but you, whose intent never fails,
do not engage at the wrong time, in the wrong place,
or with unfit vessels;
therefore your speech bears fruit,
like diligence well undertaken.

Even a fraction of the qualities of your speech
I am unable to praise,
the speech by which you lead all beings
most clearly to having few desires.

From the Praise of the Buddha, the Blessed One, called "Eloquence Born of Faith," this is the seventh chapter, called Praise of the Speech.

A single way to travel, easy in its means,
well connected and without fault,
virtuous in the beginning, the middle and the end:
there is no teaching like yours elsewhere.

The others do not aim at liberation;
they are won through hardship and are no easy road;
they are impermanent and contradict one another,
for such are the paths and fruits they teach.

If childish ones, destitute and clinging to views,
revile your system,
which alone is of this kind,
there is no enemy like views.

Having taken up the mind of awakening,
with a heart devoted to the welfare of others,
you dwelt through ten million eons as if a moment,
and likewise even in the hells.

Considering how many sufferings
you endured for the sake of beings,
should it come to dwelling even in evil states

Translator's notes

The verse beginning "What is a fault is what harms others" is terse and its second and third lines are difficult; the rendering given, contrasting faults that harm others and ruin oneself with qualities that overcome faults and sustain one's welfare, is a probable interpretation. The phrase translated "the Dharma renowned for bringing happiness" may alternatively mean "the Dharma proclaimed by the Sugata." In the verses on patience, the lines about others practicing patience from dread of future suffering are compressed, and the construal given is tentative. The verb rendered "ransomed" reads as "deceived" on its surface, but in context it is the verb "to ransom, redeem." "Those who are like the rhinoceros horn" refers to the pratyekabuddhas, the solitary realizers. In the list of the Buddha's qualities, the Tibetan "ocean everywhere" has been read as "nourishing all," in contrast to the worldly examples "without sustaining power"; this is a likely scribal slip. The phrase rendered "you never falter in accomplishing the difficult" reads "word" where "difficult" is plainly meant. In the verse "even with all of it gathered there together," the Tibetan wording is obscure and the rendering is tentative. The verses about animals and good sayings, ending "how could ... fail to follow after you," are rhetorical questions; the humble verbs suggest that the following is directed toward the Buddha. Several verses in this chapter are very terse in Tibetan, and their construal is uncertain, notably the verse on "conditions for distraction" (rendered as the Buddha finding delight in himself alone through beginningless saṃsāra) and the verse on objects "pleasing to look at or calming." In the closing title of chapter five the text reads "dang pa" where the established title has "dad pa" (faith); it has been translated as the same title. In the opening verse "three" is elliptical in the Tibetan; it has been taken as the three incalculable eons of the bodhisattva career. The verse beginning "If this work for the sake of beings" is obscure in the Tibetan; the rendering follows the sense set up by the next verse (compassion kind to all yet merciless to the Buddha himself). The image of the dancer's art "cast forth by your feet" is literal; it seems to mean that compassion makes the Buddha perform like a dancer for others' sake. "The divine waters in the ocean" most likely refers to the Ganges, whose sweet water loses its taste on entering the salt sea. The verse on the paths is terse: I have read it as the path of seeing arising first, then the paths of meditation and no more learning being set apart from it according to faculties. In the verse on the three wisdoms, the third term, rendered "revulsion," is doubtful in the Tibetan, and the correlation of the three terms with hearing, reflection and meditation is not made explicit. In chapter eight, "no easy road" could also be read as "not leading to the happy realms." In the first verse the text reads "aspired for beings" (smon); in context, and given the parallel phrase in the third verse, this is taken as the similar-looking "benefit" (sman). Several phrases in this piece are obscure and the renderings are tentative: "those who hold back" (rngan then bgyid) in the charioteer imagery of chapter ten; "are transformed" for a line saying literally that animals and others "become not other"; and "wait for the time" (gzhes par mdzad). Bharadvāja refers to the brahmin of that clan who came to the Buddha to find fault with him and was won over. "Charioteer who tames persons" renders the standard epithet puruṣadamyasārathi. In the third verse of chapter ten, the Tibetan reads "this is medicine and this is supreme"; this has been taken as a scribal slip for the similar-looking word meaning "lowly," which the contrast with "supreme" requires. "The one swollen with pride" in chapter twelve probably refers to the proud brahmin known in the Sanskrit tradition as Mānastabdha. Since the Tibetan here does not clearly present it as a name, it has been translated descriptively. The word rendered "thickly strewn grass" in the same chapter is unclear in the Tibetan. The sense of a humble bed set against beds of gold is clear. In "The conduct of others is one thing, their words another," the Tibetan reads "eye" (mig), which makes no sense here; I have followed the evident reading "words" (tshig), which fits the contrast between speech and conduct.

The phrase rendered "the watchman who keeps watch" (zhugs tse ba) is obscure, and the rendering is inferred from context.

In the stanza beginning "Protector, you have held beings in mind," the list (number, noble one, teacher, limb, kin) is terse. "As your own number" is a tentative rendering.

In "for this there is no one who can act in return" and in "there is no improper turning away," the Tibetan is compressed and the renderings are interpretive.

Mdzes bzang has been taken as Subhadra, the Buddha's last personally ordained disciple, as the context requires.

In "entrusting the whole of the dharmakāya to your foremost sons," the word plays on the body of the teaching handed on to the disciples.

In "homage to you, the inconceivable," the Tibetan reads "bas min," which gives no sense; I have read it as "bsam min" (inconceivable), in line with the closing stanza. The third stanza is obscure. The verb rendered "holds itself back" is uncommon here. It seems to say that, fearing to fall short of inexhaustible qualities, the poet restrains himself and stops his praise. In the fourth-from-last stanza, "the Venerable One" refers to Mātṛceṭa, author of the root praise into which Dignāga interwove his own verses.
