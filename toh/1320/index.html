About this text

The Means of Accomplishing the Swift Lady in Her Thirteenfold Form, whose Sanskrit title is given as the Trayodaśātmaka Kurukullā Sādhana and whose Tibetan title is Nyur Dzé Mé Drubtab Chu Sumpé Dakñi, is a brief tantric ritual manual devoted to Kurukullā, the red goddess of magnetizing and subjugation associated with Tārā. It explains the preliminary approach recitation, the painting of a square maṇḍala with Kurukullā at its center as an eight-armed red goddess, surrounded by Tārā forms in the directions and four fierce goddesses at the gates, together making thirteen deities. It then describes the meditation in which the practitioner arises as the goddess from a red syllable hrīḥ, the hand gesture of victory over the three worlds, her mantra, and the recitations and fire offerings said to bring beings, ministers and kings under one's power. The work is Toh 1320 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the tantra commentaries section among the many sādhanas, or practice texts, for meditation deities.

Translation

In the language of India: trayodśa ātma kurukulle sādhanaṃ. In the language of Tibet: The Means of Accomplishing the Swift Lady in Her Thirteenfold Form.

Homage to the goddess Kurukullā.

I shall explain the rite of Kurukullā. First of all, if one has first performed the approach, reciting the mantra a hundred thousand times, then accomplishment will come.

I shall fully teach the rite for the painted image. It is square, with four doors, adorned with four archways (tvarana). In its center one should paint Kurukullā. She is red in color and eight-armed. With her first two hands she binds the mudrā of victory over the three realms. In her right hands she holds a hook, draws an arrow, and makes the gesture of supreme giving; in her left hands she holds a noose, a bow, and an utpala. Paint her thus.

In the east paint the Tārā of Supplication. In the south paint Niṣpanna Tārā. In the north paint Karṇa Tārā. In the direction of Agni, the southeast, paint Aparājitā Tārā; in the direction of the rākṣasas, the southwest, Pradīpa Tārā; in the direction of Vāyu, the northwest, Vīra Tārā; and in the direction of Īśāna, the northeast, the Urging Lady. These goddesses are red in color, their heads adorned with the five Tathāgatas, adorned with every ornament, and four-armed. Their right hands make the gesture of supreme giving and draw an arrow; in their left hands they hold an utpala and a bow.

At the eastern gate paint Vajravetālī, with a great belly and a terrifying face, her tongue lolling, her head adorned with Akṣobhya. In her right hands she holds the threatening gesture and a hook, and in her left a vajra bell and a skull cup. Her right leg is extended.

At the southern gate is Vajravārāhī, with a great belly, her hair bristling upward, a terrifying face, and her tongue lolling. She is red in color, her head adorned with Ratnasambhava. In her right hands are a vajra and a hook, in her left a bell and a noose. Her right leg is extended.

At the western gate paint Ekajaṭī, her hair bound up in a topknot, with a great belly, her lower lip pressed by her teeth, her head adorned with Amitābha. In her right hands are a vajra and a hook; in her left she holds a vajra, a bell, and a noose. Her right leg is extended.

At the northern gate is the Vajra Songstress, her head adorned with Amoghasiddhi, with a terrifying face and a great belly. In her right hands are a sword and a hook, in her left a vajra bell and a noose. One should paint her form with the right leg extended.

They should be consecrated in their proper order.

The stages of meditating on these are taught just as for the form of the Blessed Lady. There, she arises in full from a red syllable hrīḥ resting on a sun disk. One should visualize the person to be won over, red in color and filled with devotion, sitting at her feet. With one's own rays one should fill the three worlds. If one places the person to be won over upon a wind maṇḍala shaped like a bow and adorned with a banner, which has arisen from a blue syllable yaṃ, one brings that person under one's power. Even the three worlds come under one's power.

As for the mudrā of victory over the three worlds: join the backs of the two palms, bind the two ring fingers in a knot, touch together the tips of the little fingers, the middle fingers, and the thumbs, and make the index fingers like hooks. Placing this at the forehead is the mudrā of victory over the three worlds.

The secret mantra to recite while meditating in this way is: oṃ kurukulle hūṃ hrīḥ svāhā. If one recites it ten thousand times, all beings come under one's power. With a vast number of recitations, mantra practitioners do. With a hundred thousand, the king comes under one's power.

If one makes a fire offering with utpala flowers and arka wood, one obtains the aim one desires. If one kindles a fire of bheta wood and makes a fire offering a hundred thousand times with handfuls of madhuka and rice, even the king's ministers come under one's power. With a thousand utpalas, even the great minister comes under one's power. By offering the goddess a thousand lamps of sesame oil, even the king comes under one's power.

The goddess Kurukullā in her thirteenfold form is complete.

Translator's notes

The Swift Lady of the Tibetan title is Kurukullā herself, and the thirteen are the central goddess with her eight surrounding Tārās and four gate goddesses. The text names only seven of the surrounding goddesses, and none for the west; this is translated as it stands. The eastern Tārā, the Tārā of Supplication, and the northeastern Urging Lady could not be identified with confidence and are translated literally; so also the northern gatekeeper, the Vajra Songstress. The count given for mantra practitioners is a Tibetan word for an immense number, which sits oddly between ten thousand and a hundred thousand; it is rendered generally. In the description of Ekajaṭī, གསུམ་ཁྱིམ is read as གསུས་ཁྱིམ, "belly," and in the last line རྒྱལ་པ as རྒྱལ་པོ, "king." The offering substance མ་དུ་བ is taken as madhuka.
