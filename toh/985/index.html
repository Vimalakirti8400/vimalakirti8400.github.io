About this text

The Noble Great Queen of Vidyā-Mantras, She of the White Parasol Who Arose from the Uṣṇīṣa of All the Tathāgatas, Invincible by Others, She Who Turns Back, known in Sanskrit as the Ārya Sarvatathāgatoṣṇīṣasitātapatrā Aparājitā Pratyaṅgirā Mahāvidyārājñī, is a scripture centered on the protective goddess Sitātapatrā, the White Parasol. In Tibetan it is known as a text on the great queen of vidyā-mantras, the white parasol that arose from the crown protuberance of all the tathāgatas. The discourse opens in the Heaven of the Thirty-Three, in the gods' assembly hall Sudharmā. There the Buddha, surrounded by monks, bodhisattvas, and Śakra, lord of the gods, enters a meditative absorption called Gazing upon the Uṣṇīṣa, from which this powerful mantra and its protective goddess emerge to repel harm and turn back hostile forces. The work is Toh 985 in the Kangyur, where it belongs to the collection of dhāraṇī texts, the incantations prized throughout the Tibetan tradition for protection and blessing.

Thus did I hear at one time. The Blessed One was dwelling in the realm of the gods, in the abode of the Heaven of the Thirty-Three, at Sudharmā, the assembly hall of the gods. With him were a great saṅgha of monks, a great saṅgha of bodhisattvas, and Śakra (Indra), lord of the gods. Then the Blessed One, seated on the seat that had been prepared, entered into the meditative absorption called Gazing upon the Uṣṇīṣa. Translator's notes

Two buddha names could not be identified with confidence and are translated by meaning: "Vajradhara, Conqueror of the Ocean" and "King of the Crest of the Fragrance of Blooming Lotuses and Utpalas."

The Tibetan for "together with the Lord of Umā" follows the Sanskrit umāpati-sahita. This is usually understood as an epithet of Rudra himself, Umā's lord, accompanied by his retinue.

In the Mahākāla homage the Tibetan says only "together with." The words "his retinue" supply the implied companions.

The phrase on the vidyādhara sages literally reads "able to benefit by curses." Parallel Sanskrit versions read this as "able to curse and to bless."

The closing verb (rab tu brjod par bya'o) is a gerundive, "is to be proclaimed." It is rendered as an instruction to the reciter, not as a first-person statement. Many of the goddess epithets in the verse and several graha names in the list are rendered by their meaning, since a Sanskrit form could not be identified with confidence. These include "Vajra Voice" for rdo rje sgros, "she who stretches forth" for rnam bsgyings ma, the graha that "draws out mats" for re lde 'don pa, and "discharges" for nom bu. Lus Chagma (lus chags ma) is left in phonetics as an unidentified graha name. "The oppressing grahas" renders gnon po as it stands; it may be a corruption, and the Sanskrit parallels have a different name here. The first-person "I cut" and "I pin" follow the sense of the parallel Sanskrit, where the forms are chindayāmi and kīlayāmi; the Tibetan leaves the subject unstated. Several names in this litany are identified from the parallel Sanskrit of the Sitātapatrā dhāraṇī rather than read directly from the Tibetan. The least certain are "Meteor-Face" as Ulkāmukha, "Accomplisher" as Siddhikara, and "Youth" as Kumāra (Skanda).

The verbs "I sever" and "I pin down" render the future or intentional forms of the Tibetan. These correspond to the first-person verbs of the Sanskrit original.

"Together with their companions" renders lhan cig pa, which corresponds to Sanskrit sahāya. Several graha names in the prose list are uncertain in their Indian identification and are translated by meaning: "the oppressor grahas," "the grahas that cause trembling all over," "the grahas that draw out thorns," "the jar-like grahas," and "the body ḍākinī grahas." The item rendered "eaters of discharges" is uncertain, because the Tibetan word is obscure. The petition to protect continues into the next piece, where the list of fevers closes. Several disease names in the lists are uncertain in precise identification; "rashes" for the obscure term bas bldags is a tentative rendering. The large number qualifying the vajra-family deities combines koṭi with khrag khrig, a very large numerical unit, here rendered loosely as "trillions." The phrase rendered "over every calamity" is uncertain. As written, the Tibetan reads "all that is ruined" or "all that has disintegrated." It may refer to every kind of misfortune, or it may reflect a scribal variant.
