About this text

Toh 89 in the Kangyur is a Mahāyāna sūtra known in Sanskrit as the Ārya-Akṣayamati-paripṛcchā and in Tibetan as "Phags pa blo gros mi zad pas zhus pa," rendered in English as "The Noble Sūtra of the Questions of Inexhaustible Intelligence." The text records teachings given by the Buddha on Vulture Peak Mountain at Rājagṛha, framed as a dialogue initiated by the bodhisattva Inexhaustible Intelligence, and belongs to a tradition of inquiry-centered Mahāyāna scriptures in which a bodhisattva's questions draw forth profound doctrinal exposition. It forms the forty-fifth chapter of the vast collection known as the Great Heap of Jewels, the Ratnakūṭa, a compendium of one hundred thousand chapters gathering numerous independent sūtras under a single organizing title. The translation into Tibetan was carried out and finalized by the great translator-monk Yeshé Dé, one of the most prolific and revered translators of the early period of Buddhist textual transmission in Tibet.

Homage to all buddhas and bodhisattvas.

Thus did I hear. At one time, the Blessed One was dwelling at Rājagṛha, on Vulture Peak Mountain, together with a great Saṅgha of one thousand two hundred and fifty monks, and with a thousand bodhisattvas — namely, the great bodhisattva Jñānaketu, the great bodhisattva Dharmaketu, the great bodhisattva Candraketu, the great bodhisattva Sūryaketu, the great bodhisattva Anantaketu, and Bhadrapāla and the other sixteen holy persons; the sixty incomparable bodhisattvas headed by the great bodhisattva Mañjuśrī; and all the great bodhisattvas of the Fortunate Aeon headed by Maitreya — all present together in that assembly.

The great bodhisattva Inexhaustible Intelligence was also there, seated in that very assembly, surrounded and attended upon in front by sixty million bodhisattvas.

Then the great bodhisattva Inexhaustible Intelligence rose from his seat, arranged his upper robe over one shoulder, placed his right knee on the ground, and, with palms pressed together turned toward the Blessed One, offered to the Blessed One a canopy of flowers of various precious gems and a shower of flowers, and spoke these words to the Blessed One:

"Blessed One, we speak of 'the awakening mind, the awakening mind' — but, Blessed One, by what measure does a bodhisattva truly possess the awakening mind? Blessed One, what is the awakening mind? Blessed One, with respect to awakening, no mind or act of thinking is apprehended — it has never been apprehended, and it will never come to be apprehended. Nor is the awakening mind apprehended anywhere apart from awakening. Awakening is inexpressible. Mind has no form, cannot be shown, has no obstruction, has no cognitive manifestation, and no act of thinking is apprehended in it either. Blessed One, how then are we to understand the meaning of these phenomena?"

The Blessed One declared: "Son of good family, with respect to awakening, no sign, no designation, and no conventional label is apprehended; and the awakening mind is likewise not apprehended anywhere apart from awakening. Awakening is inexpressible. Mind has no form, cannot be shown, has no obstruction, has no cognitive manifestation — and no act of thinking is apprehended in it. Why is this so? Awakening is inexpressible; and just as awakening is, so too is mind. Just as mind is, so too is awakening. Just as both awakening and mind are, so too are all those who think. And just as those who think are, so too are all phenomena of the past, future, and present.

"Son of good family, it is in this way that a great bodhisattva acquires the name 'bodhisattva.' The mind that follows along with all phenomena — that is called the awakening mind. Awakening is neither past, nor future, nor present; and mind is just the same; and thinking is just the same. Because not a single phenomenon is apprehended, nothing whatsoever is found here: there is no awakening, no designation of awakening, no sentient being, no designation of sentient being, no śrāvaka —"

no designation of śrāvaka, no pratyekabuddha, no designation of pratyekabuddha, no bodhisattva, no designation of bodhisattva, no buddha, no designation of buddha, no conditioned phenomenon (saṃskṛta), no designation of a conditioned phenomenon, no unconditioned phenomenon (asaṃskṛta), no designation of an unconditioned phenomenon — none of these is apprehended, nor can they be apprehended, nor will they be apprehended.

And yet, son of good family, by way of example: just as the first arising of the mind is perfectly exalted by all wholesome roots in the manner of Mount Meru, so too its cause is the perfection of generosity.

The second arousal of mind regards all phenomena as the ground of activity, viewing them in the manner of the earth; its cause is the perfection of moral discipline.

The third arousal of mind is like the prowess of the maned lion, the king of wild beasts, in that it is free from fear, free from apprehension, and free from terror; its cause is the perfection of patience.

The fourth arousal of mind, in the manner of one who is irreversible, is regarded as being like the power, strength, force, diligence, and swiftness of Indra's son (Jayanta); its cause is the perfection of diligence.

The fifth arousal of mind, in the manner of one who is never satisfied by merit, is regarded as one who is perfectly exalted by manifold virtues; its cause is the perfection of meditative concentration.

The sixth arousal of mind, dispelling great dense darkness, is regarded as being like the brilliant orb of the sun; its cause is the perfection of wisdom.

The seventh arousal of mind, liberating beings from the wilderness of saṃsāra in the manner of a great caravan leader richly endowed, is regarded as gloriously adorned by the complete fulfillment of all intentions; its cause is the perfection of skillful means.

The eighth arousal of mind is regarded as complete in the thorough purification of all obscurations, by way of the analogy of the disk of the nearby moon, perfectly pure; its cause is the perfection of aspiration.

The ninth arousal of mind is regarded as fulfilling all intentions in the manner of the maṇḍala of all the arranged adornments of virtue of a perfectly pure buddha-field; its cause is the perfection of power.

The tenth arousal of mind, having received empowerment that grants sovereignty over all qualities, wisdom, and phenomena, is regarded as equal to space, by way of the analogy of the marks of a wheel-turning sovereign; its cause is the perfection of gnosis.

Son of good family, these are the ten arousals of mind of bodhisattvas. By being endowed with these, one is called a bodhisattva. In ultimate truth, however, there is no awakening here, no bodhisattva, and no awakening mind.

Translator's notes

The passage breaks off mid-sentence at the end of the Tibetan input, ending with "no śrāvaka." This is not an error in translation but a genuine textual truncation at the source.

The names Jñānaketu, Dharmaketu, Candraketu, Sūryaketu, and Anantaketu are given here in Sanskrit reconstruction from the Tibetan: Ye shes tog, Chos kyi tog, Zla ba'i tog, Nyi ma'i tog, and Mtha' yas tog respectively, meaning "Crest of Wisdom," "Crest of Dharma," "Crest of the Moon," "Crest of the Sun," and "Crest of the Infinite." Bhadrapāla is reconstructed for Bzang skyong. These reconstructions are standard and uncontroversial within the tradition. The fourth arousal of mind compares the bodhisattva's irreversibility to "Indra's son" — the Tibetan reads Sred med kyi bu, literally "son of Nairriti" or possibly "son of one without craving," which in some traditions identifies the deity Jayanta, son of Indra. The precise referent is not entirely certain; it may instead point to a minor deity associated with irreversible energy. This is noted as the one genuinely uncertain identification in this passage.
