About this text

Toh 326 in the Kangyur is a canonical Buddhist scripture known in Sanskrit as the Udānavarga and in Tibetan as the Cheddu Jöpé Tsom, meaning The Collection of Purposeful Utterances. It is a collection of inspired or solemn verses attributed to the Buddha, organized into topical chapters, and represents an expanded Indic compilation related to the Pali Udāna and Dhammapada traditions, gathering pithy sayings and verse teachings spoken by the Buddha on various occasions. The text opens with homage to the Omniscient One and moves swiftly into verses on impermanence, the urgency of spiritual practice, and the nature of suffering, setting a tone of earnest moral and contemplative instruction aimed at motivating practitioners. As a verse anthology of the Buddha's direct speech, it belongs to the broader class of sūtra literature within the Kangyur.

Translation

In the language of India: Udānavarga. In the language of Tibet: The Collection of Purposeful Utterances. The First Fascicle.

Homage to the Omniscient One. May all be auspicious.

The purposeful utterances spoken by the Victor
I shall now set forth with care.
Dispel your drowsiness and dullness,
gladden your mind, and listen well.

The Omniscient One, the Protector,
the Sage who cared for beings with compassion,
holding his last bodily existence,
the Blessed One spoke these words:

Alas — all formations are impermanent,
they have the nature of arising and perishing.
Having arisen, they fall into ruin;
their stilling is bliss.

When everything is ablaze,
what cause is there for laughter, what for song?
You who dwell enshrouded in darkness —
why do you not seek a lamp?

These things that are cast away,
white as bleached bone,
scattered in every direction —
seeing this, what is there to rejoice in?

The person who enters the womb
in the course of a single night
will not remain but must pass on,
and once gone, does not return.

In the morning one may see many people;
by evening some will be seen no more.
In the evening one may see many people;
by morning some will be seen no more.

Men, women in great number,
even those in the prime of life will die —
who, then, would say of any person, "this one is young,"
and take pride in the warmth of being alive?

Some will die within the womb,
some likewise on the ground where they are born,
some when they have just learned to crawl,
some when they can run about.
Some are old, some young,
some are people in the prime of life —
one by one they pass away
like ripe fruit falling.

Just as ripened fruit
ever lives in fear of falling,
so too a person who has been born
forever lives in fear of death.

Just as a potter who fashions vessels
from clay —
every single one ends in ruin at last —
so too is the life of human beings.

Just as a loom being stretched,
as thread after thread is drawn through,
each thread pressing against the one behind,
so too is the life of human beings.

Just as those who are condemned to death,
with every step they take,
draw ever closer to the place of execution,
so too is the life of human beings.

Just as the current of a swift flowing river
never turns back,
so the passing of a human life
can never be reversed.

Difficult, brief,
and saturated with suffering —
it perishes swiftly,
like a line drawn on water.

Just as a herdsman bearing a staff
drives the cattle home to their place,
so illness and old age
drive human beings into the presence of the lord of death.

Like the water of a waterfall,
as days and nights flow past,
the life of human beings is exhausted,
and vitality draws close to ruin.

A firefly makes the night seem long;
a road makes smoke-signals seem far.
For the childish who do not know the true Dharma,
saṃsāra is long.

"I have a son; likewise I have wealth" —
by such thoughts the fool is ruined.
When there is no self, within or without,
whose child is it, and what, indeed, is wealth?

Clans of men and women,
hundreds and thousands and many more,
even those who have properly secured their prosperity —
all will fall into the power of the lord of death.

Everything accumulated is finally exhausted;
what is raised up will finally topple.
What has met will finally part;
the end of living is death.

Since the end of life is death,
all sentient beings will die,
and following their virtue and their evil
toward their respective fruits,
they go in accordance with their karma exactly as it is:
through evil deeds, to hell;
through merit, to the happy destinies;
and others, by cultivating the path,
will attain undefiled nirvāṇa.

Whether buddhas, pratyekabuddhas,
or even the śrāvakas of the buddhas —
if they too relinquish this bodily form,
what need is there to speak of ordinary people?

There is no place anywhere to dwell
that is beyond the reach of death:
not in the sky above, not in the midst of the ocean,
not even by entering into the heart of the mountains.

All that has come to be and all that will come to be —
every one of them departs, leaving this body behind.
Let the wise understand that all of this is subject to destruction,
and abide in the Dharma, living the holy life (brahmacarya).

Seeing old age, seeing the suffering of sickness,
and seeing death that severs one from mind and breath,
with resolution one should abandon the household, which is like a prison,
though for ordinary worldly people the renunciation of desire is rare.

Even the king's chariot, so brilliantly adorned, will perish.
In just the same way, this body too draws ever nearer to decay.
But this Dharma of the noble ones is itself noble:
the supreme among human beings realizes it, and it does not grow old.

Old age, you are foolish and ungainly.
You are one who acts without propriety.
Even this pleasing form
has been utterly crushed by you.

One who lives for a full hundred years
is still carried away by death itself.
All these beings age,
fall ill, and are vanquished by death.

They go on, never turning back,
disintegrating utterly through day and night,
endowed with the suffering of birth and death —
like fish being boiled in a pot of hot water.

This life, by day and by night,
whether going or staying,
passes like the current of a great river,
never to return.

Like a fish in a shallow stream —
one for whom each passing day and night
makes this life grow ever shorter —
what reason is there to rejoice?

Because the end of living is death,
this body ages completely,
this nest of disease swiftly falls apart,
and even this mass of flesh will perish.

Alas, this body, without support,
empty and severed from consciousness,
will topple to the ground
like a discarded log thrown in a charnel ground.

Constantly tormented by illness,
always leaking impurity,
worn down and diminished by old age and death —
why would one not wish to abandon this body?

With this body so swiftly crumbling, full of pus and disease,
and with no certainty whatsoever of accomplishment or happiness,
strive instead for the sake of the supreme peace.

Thinking, "I shall do this in winter, in spring, in summer" —
so the childish one deliberates,
and the obstacles are not seen.

The mind of a person is attached
to children, wealth, and livestock.
The lord of death takes him and goes,
as a flood sweeps away a sleeping village.

When the time of death has come,
one's children will not be a refuge,
nor one's father, nor one's relatives —
there is none who can become your refuge.

"This task is already done; now I will do that.
Having done those things, I shall then do this" —
while a person thus deliberates on and on,
old age and illness strike, and death overwhelms him directly.

Therefore, always take delight in meditative absorption and rest in equipoise.
Generate diligence; do not wait for the end of birth and old age.
Overwhelm Māra and all his hosts completely.
Monks, strive to go beyond the far shore of birth and death.

This concludes the first chapter, on impermanence.

Desire arises from conceptual thought (vikalpa),
so know this to be the very root of desire.
Do not engage in conceptual thought,
and thereby desire will not arise in you.

From desires, sorrow is born;
from desires, fear arises.
For one who is entirely free of desires,
there is no sorrow and no fear.

From joys, sorrow is born;
from joys, fear arises.
For one who is entirely free of joys,
there is no sorrow and no fear.

The ripening of sensual pleasures is suffering:
sweet at first, yet the fruit is bitter.
Like a child who will not let go of a burning torch,
in the end it burns the one who holds it.

Look at those who cling with utter attachment
to children and daughters, to jeweled earrings —
the noble ones declare that the bonds made of iron
and of rope and wood are not the tightest bonds of all.

The noble ones declare that the bond of desire,
however slack it may appear, is hard to fully loosen.
Those who are steadfast, not looking to the pleasures of desire,
sever these bonds and swiftly move onward.

The many-formed things of the world are not the desire;
it is the person's conceptual thought and attachment that is desire.
The many-formed things of the world remain just as they are,
yet those who are steadfast subdue their longing toward them.

People possess no lasting pleasure,
for whatever desired thing they are attached to is impermanent.
Therefore, since there is no further existence to be had,
relinquish it thoroughly in your mind,
and it is taught: know how to make of death a place that does not change.

Is it not so that longing arises and then exhausts itself?
That which is blameless in mind, together with mindfulness,
and non-attachment, each in its own way, to desires —
that, it is taught, is what moves one higher.

Just as a smith refines silver,
so one who possesses wisdom, gradually,
moment by moment, little by little,
should remove one's own impurities.

Just as a cobbler thoroughly softens leather
and then fashions shoes from it,
so each and every desire that is abandoned
yields a happiness that is complete and excellent.

If you wish for happiness in its entirety,
abandon all desires entirely.
By abandoning all desires completely,
you will attain the highest happiness.

For as long as one follows after desire,
for that long one will never be satisfied.
Those who turn away from it by means of wisdom —
they attain contentment.

Since one cannot find satisfaction through desires,
find satisfaction happily through wisdom.
A person who is satisfied through wisdom
will not fall under the power of existence.

Those men who are attached to desires —
alas, they delight in what is contrary to Dharma.
While they dwell in the briefest flash of life,
they will not come to see the obstacles before them.

Those of poor understanding, overcome by wealth,
do not seek the farther shore;
with minds scattered and clinging to desire through craving,
they bring ruin to themselves and to others as well.

Even were a rain of gold coins to fall,
those bound by desires would never be satisfied.
The wise have understood within themselves
that desires bring small pleasure and many faults.

Even toward the pleasures of the gods
one should feel no special delight.
Fully awakened buddhas and śrāvakas
rejoice through the exhaustion of craving.

Even if there were a mountain of gold
equal in size to the Himālayas,
know that not even one person would be satisfied by such wealth —
understand this and conduct yourself well.

Those who know the cause of suffering
will never delight in desire.
Knowing desire to be the cause of pain in the world,
they study the teaching in order to tame it.

This concludes the second chapter, on desires.

Embodied beings, utterly overwhelmed by conceptual thought,
looking to impurity as clean and pure out of unbearable desire —
desire increases and spreads still further,
and by that very bond it draws itself tighter.

Those who delight in the quieting of conceptual thought
and who continuously cultivate mindfulness of the unattractive —
by thoroughly abandoning craving in regard to that,
they cause those bonds of theirs to decay and crumble.

Shrouded in the darkness that is desire,
covered by the canopy of craving,
bound by the bonds of heedlessness,
they are like fish that have fallen into the net of the fisherman.

A person who lives in utter heedlessness —
craving grows in that one like a climbing vine;
one drifts after aging and death,
like a calf that desires its mother's milk.

Those who produce recollection, who produce attachment,
who entertain thoughts toward what is mentally pleasant —
through these they wander again and again in cyclic existence,
like a monkey in a forest who desires its fruit.

A person who constantly pursues pleasure
draws ever nearer to birth and aging.
Those driven by the craving of sentient beings push one forward —
like a rabbit that races in circles through a snare.

Sentient beings bound by craving,
their minds attached to existence and non-existence alike,
those childish ones who delight in the junctions and pleasures of clinging —
they meet with suffering again and again.

People are separated from success and happiness;
possessed of the bonds of Māra,
they follow after aging and death —
like a calf that desires its mother's milk.

But the one here who abandons craving such as this,
free from craving for existence and non-existence,
that monk who overcomes existence —
passes fully into nirvāṇa, without remainder.

The one who is driven by craving's woman,
that craving so hard to abandon in the world —
that one's sorrow will grow and grow,
like grass upon which rain has fallen.

The one who abandons craving's woman,
that craving so hard to abandon in the world —
that one's sorrow will be turned back,
like water drops from a lotus.

Therefore, to all of you who have assembled here,
I speak for your well-being:
as usira grass is pulled out by its roots,
so too pull out craving, root and all.

When a person keeps company with craving,
one wanders for a very long time.
So pull craving out by the root —
then there is no sorrow and no fear.

Taking up and grasping again and again,
entering the womb again and again,
through transitions and transformations,
sentient beings go and come.

Therefore abandon that craving so hard to abandon here,
cut the seed —
then in that one craving will not arise,
and that one will not circle in saṃsāra again.

Without squandering the leisure and endowment one has obtained,
strive against the craving for objects
that binds one and sets one among gods and humans with worldly aims.
Even if a sentient being is born in hell,
one who has squandered leisure and endowment will come to regret it.

Because craving for objects, the basis of results,
is the cause of wandering the ocean of existence,
covering one with a net of creepers and ensnaring,
burning and unsubdued —
this suffering will not be reversed in any respect, again and again.

For example, if a tree is not uprooted,
even though cut down it simply grows back again.
Likewise, if the subtle tendril of craving is not pulled out,
this suffering will not be reversed again and again.

For example, just as a weapon fashioned by oneself
slays the maker by a thief's power,
so too craving, arising from within one's own mind,
slays the one who has a body.

Knowing in this way the faults by which
suffering arises from craving,
the monk who is endowed with mindfulness
should abandon craving, eliminate grasping,
and travel through everything.

This is the third chapter, on craving.

Heedfulness is the ground of the deathless.
Heedlessness is the ground of death.
The heedful do not die.
Those who are heedless are as if always dead.

Knowing well this distinction,
the wise are endowed with heedfulness.
Those who rejoice greatly in heedfulness —
they rejoice in the domain of noble ones.

The one who continuously cultivates heedfulness,
steadfast and ever conquering the other shore —
through that steadfastness one attains the unsurpassed ease of completion
and touches nirvāṇa.

When the wise one, through heedfulness,
has overcome heedlessness —
ascending the palace of wisdom,
sorrowless, one looks down upon
those in sorrow, the steadfast upon mountain peaks
looking down upon those who are children on the ground.

Through diligence, heedfulness,
perfect restraint and discipline,
the wise one, having made an island,
turns back the great flood.

The one who is diligent and endowed with mindfulness,
who acts with purity and with careful examination,
who lives by right restraint and by the Dharma —
the renown of such heedful ones openly increases.

Be heedful with respect to higher mind,
and train in the rules of restraint laid down by the Sage.
One who guards with mindfulness, abiding in constant peace —
that guarding is nirvāṇa.

Do not rely on lowly dharmas,
do not resort to heedlessness.
Do not incline toward wrong views,
and do not let the world proliferate.

Whoever possesses, in the highest degree,
the right view that is worldly —
even across a thousand lifetimes
that one will not go to lower destinies.

One who gives himself over to heedlessness
is a foolish person whose thinking goes to ruin.
As a merchant guards his wealth,
so let the wise one be endowed with heedfulness.

Not giving oneself over to heedlessness,
not pursuing delight in desires,
and ever holding the mind in heedfulness —
such a one will attain the exhaustion of defilements.

Even if the exhaustion of defilements has not yet been attained,
do not at this time indulge in heedlessness.
Like a lion, not a deer of the wilds,
the heedless ones will be slain.

The man who, driven by heedlessness, resorts to another's wife —
four circumstances arise to settle in his mind:
he fails to gain merit, he does not sleep at ease;
third, he is reviled; and fourth, he falls into hell.

He who gains no merit goes the way of evil destinies,
and with fear and shame beside him, his happiness is small.
He is cut down by the king's severe punishment,
and this body is scorched in the hell-realms.

Therefore, whoever desires to see their own good
should act in accordance with what was said above.
Do not let diligence become weak with constancy,
for it goes the way of a child's cart.

For example, just as children's carts
abandon the great good road
and go along bad roads,
bringing ruin like an eye that is broken —
so likewise those who abandon the Dharma
and follow what is not the Dharma,
those foolish ones go under the power of the lord of death
and, like the broken eye, meet with destruction.

Those who abandon what should be done
and do what should not be done,
and who conduct themselves with arrogant heedlessness —
for all such ones, the defilements multiply.
And for those whose defilements multiply,
the undefiled is far, far away.

Those whose mindfulness is constantly applied to the body,
who steadily apply themselves to what is good,
who do not do what should not be done
and continually do what should be done,
and who are endowed with mindfulness and vigilance —
for them, the defilements will be exhausted.
And for those whose defilements are exhausted,
they will attain the undefiled.

However much one may speak,
if one does not hold to the true Dharma,
those who here have heard but little
yet who have realized the teachings through their body —
those who are heedful in the Dharma,
they are the ones who hold the true Dharma.

Even if one speaks at length with reasoned argument,
heedless persons will not act accordingly.
Just as a cowherd counts the cattle of another,
they have not obtained the share of the ascetic life.

But even if one speaks only a little with reasoned argument,
yet walks in the Dharma in accordance with the Dharma,
and abandons attachment, aversion, and delusion —
such ones have obtained the share of the ascetic life.

He who proclaims the praise of heedfulness
and ever reproaches heedlessness —
heedfulness, which was praised even by a hundredfold sacrifice,
has become foremost among the gods.

Whether a deed is done or left undone,
the wise one proclaims the praise of heedfulness.
The wise one who is equipped with heedfulness
takes up both benefits fully:
what the benefit of this life is,
and what the benefit of the next life is —
by grasping these steadily and with discernment,
one is accordingly called wise.

The monk who delights in heedfulness
and looks upon heedlessness with fear
pulls himself free from lower destinies,
like a great elephant from a swamp.

The monk who delights in heedfulness
and looks upon heedlessness with fear
shakes off unwholesome dharmas
like the wind shakes the leaves of a tree.

The monk who delights in heedfulness
and looks upon heedlessness with fear
causes all bonds, subtle and coarse,
to be consumed as if by fire.

The monk who delights in heedfulness
and looks upon heedlessness with fear
gradually, through cultivation,
exhausts every one of his bonds.

The monk who delights in heedfulness
and looks upon heedlessness with fear
individually realizes the peaceful stage
of the stilling and cessation of formations.

The monk who delights in heedfulness
and looks upon heedlessness with fear —

and looks upon heedlessness with fear —
because of his nearness to nirvāṇa,
he cannot fall away at all.

The monk who is endowed with diligence, possessed of heedfulness,
and who practices the Dharma and practices it well
will attain happiness in this world and the next
through his practice of the Dharma.

For the sake of peace, take refuge in the training;
arouse effort and generate diligence.
Lack of mindfulness, heedlessness,
little diligence, absence of restraint,
drowsiness, torpor, and distraction —
these are obstacles to the training.
Therefore, having understood that limb,
do not let mindfulness be squandered and wasted.

The monk who is good in moral discipline
and who delights in heedfulness —
let him set all conceptual thought at rest
and guard his own mind accordingly.
He should rouse himself and lift himself up,
and enter into the teaching of the Buddha.
Like a great elephant against a house of clay,
let him crush the army of the Lord of Death.

Whoever practices with heedfulness
in this very Dharma of discipline,
having fully abandoned the wandering of birth,
will make an end of suffering.

This is the fourth chapter: on the island of heedfulness.

Grief arises from those one holds dear;
fear arises from those one holds dear.
When one is fully free from those held dear,
there is no grief, and there is no fear.

From those one loves, grief arises;
from love comes suffering; from love comes fear.
When those one loves are transformed by change,
one is stricken with bewilderment and distress.

However many forms of suffering in the world,
however many laments and cries of sorrow —
all of these arise in dependence on those held dear.
When attachment to the dear is abandoned, they cease to exist.

For those who have nothing dear anywhere in the world
are those who dwell in happiness and are free from grief.
Therefore one who seeks to be without anguish and without stain
should never make anything a beloved object.

From not seeing those held dear comes suffering;
and from seeing those not dear it is the same.
Therefore never meet with what is dear,
and never meet with what is not dear.

From separation from what is dear
and from encounter with what is not dear,
the unbearable grief arises
that makes impure beings grow old.

When one who is dear has died,
many relatives and close friends
grieve for a long time
and suffer through separation from the beloved.

One in whom there is neither the dear nor the not-dear
has no binding knot.
Therefore do not make an evil one
into a beloved.

Looking only at the beloved and abandoning one's purpose,
not engaging in the things that lead to happiness,
while those who are themselves bound to unhappiness
yearn after the things of happiness —

Those who are fettered by the pleasure of beautiful form,
across the various classes of gods and humans,
commit evil and fall to ruin,
and come under the power of aging and death.

But whoever, through day and through night,
practices heedfulness and abandons beautiful form,
uproots from the very base
the food of Māra that is so hard to abandon, and the root of evil.

Through appearing as what is good when it is not good,
through appearing as what is dear when it is not dear,
and through appearing as happiness when it is suffering,
the heedless are utterly overcome.

If one wishes to do good to oneself,
since happiness cannot be obtained with ease
through the one who acts harmfully,
one should not lead this self into what is beloved.

If one wishes to do good to oneself,
since happiness will be obtained with ease
through the one who acts rightly,
one should not lead this self into evil.

Just as the cities of the wilderness
are surrounded by firm ramparts —
if one wishes to do good to oneself,
guard this self, and guard it well.

If one wishes to do good to oneself,
then in whichever of the three periods it may be,
with the skill of one who fans a flame,
hide this self, and hide it well.

Just as the cities of the wilderness
are guarded within and without by sentinels —
so that the endowment of leisure and opportunity is not wasted and consumed,
in just that way should the self be guarded.

Were one to be born in the hells,
one would come to regret the wasting of leisure and opportunity.

Though with the mind one searches in every direction,
one finds no one anywhere more dear than oneself.

In just the same way, each being holds itself most dear.
Therefore one who is self-cherishing should not harm others.
Life is dear to all beings,
and all tremble before the fear of being cut off.
Measuring by one's own experience,
one should neither beat nor kill.

A man who has been exiled to a distant land,
when he returns after a long time without news from home,
is greeted by his kinsmen, friends, and loved ones
and met with joyful cries of delight.

In the same way, a person who has done merit,
when he has passed from this world to the beyond,
is met by his merits, bright as the light of day,
and is filled with manifest joy.

Therefore, for the sake of the world beyond,
accumulate merit.
Merit becomes the support and dwelling place
of those who bear a body in the next world.

One who does what is good and noble in conduct —
the gods praise their virtue.
Even here they will not be despised,
and in the higher realms they will find joy.

Abiding in the Dharma, perfect in moral discipline,
knowing shame, and speaking truth —
in doing their own proper work,
they bring joy to all other people.
In pursuing their own good thus,
others too become glad,
and in the Dharma that is visible they are praised,
and in the beyond they go to a happy destiny.

When counseled, they speak words of assent,
and in turning others away from improper conduct,
the noble ones rejoice.
Those who are not noble will not rejoice.

Therefore, between the noble and the ignoble,
when the separation of death has come,
the ignoble go to hell,
while the noble go to the higher realms.

This is the fifth chapter, on what is dear.

Those who seek the threefold good —
to be praised, to obtain wealth,
and to find joy in a happy destiny beyond —
let the wise guard moral discipline.

To be noble, to have the fullness of vision,
to become a support for the peace of the world —
seeing these further states,
let the wise guard moral discipline.

Through moral discipline, one attains happiness,
free of all torment in the body.
At night one sleeps in peace,
and upon waking one finds joy as well.

Through the merit made by the wise
in generosity and moral discipline,
both in this life and in the next
one comes always to happiness.

Virtuous in moral discipline until old age,
well established in faith — this is excellent.
Wisdom is the precious jewel of the human being,
and it is difficult for even the destitute in merit to destroy.

A monk, abiding in moral discipline,
restraining the sense faculties,
knowing the measure in food,
and striving in non-sleep —

dwelling thus with diligence,
not given to torpor by day or night —
because of closeness to nirvāṇa,
decline and deterioration cannot occur.

A person, established in moral discipline,
diligently training in the cultivation of mind and wisdom,
the monk who abides with certainty
will attain the exhaustion of suffering.

Therefore always guard moral discipline,
follow and protect meditative absorption,
train in insight,
and be mindful and vigilant, each in its own way.

By that, having exhausted all binding connections,
one becomes free of mind, leaving nothing behind.
The one possessed of wisdom who has shed the body
attains the immeasurable nirvāṇa.

One who has trained well in these three —
moral discipline, meditative absorption, and wisdom —
having reached the utter end, perfectly stainless,
free of torment, with becoming exhausted,
fully released from the bonds of attachment,
having shed the body, yet possessed of wisdom,
having gone beyond the domain of Māra,
blazes forth like the sun upon the path.

A monk who is careless and arrogant
with regard to outer and inner conduct —
his moral discipline, meditative absorption, and wisdom
will not be brought to perfect completion.

From cloud-cover rain falls,
while from a clear sky rain does not fall.
Therefore dispel the obscuring cover,
and in this way the rain will indeed fall.

Thus it is that the monk who always sees clearly
and is restrained by the vow of moral discipline —

is purified swiftly on the path leading to nirvāṇa.

The fragrance of flowers does not travel against the wind,
nor does sandal, nor costus, nor jasmine.
But the fragrance of the noble ones travels against the wind —
the fragrance of the excellent person pervades every direction.

Among all these kinds of fragrance —
costus, sandalwood,
blue lotus, and jasmine —
the fragrance of moral discipline is supreme.

The fragrance of costus and sandalwood,
however it may be, is slight.
But the fragrance of one endowed with moral discipline
pervades this world and the heavens alike.

Therefore dwell in heedfulness
and purify your moral discipline completely.
When one is liberated through perfect understanding,
the path of Māra will not be found.

This is the path to happiness,
and on this path of purification,
having entered and trained with diligence,
abandon the bonds of Māra.

This is the sixth chapter on moral discipline.

Having abandoned the wrong conduct of body

if one guards against the great wrongs of body,
one will have achieved restraint of body.

Having abandoned the wrong conduct of speech
and practised the right conduct of speech,
if one guards against the great wrongs of speech,
one will have achieved restraint of speech.

Having abandoned the wrong conduct of mind
and practised the right conduct of mind,
if one guards against the great wrongs of mind,
one will have achieved restraint of mind.

Abandon wrong conduct of body,
abandon wrong conduct of speech,
abandon wrong conduct of mind,
and abandon whatever other faults one may have.

Perform the virtuous acts of body,
perform the many virtuous acts of speech,
perform the virtuous acts of mind,
and be endowed with the four immeasurables.

When one performs the virtuous acts of body
and acts likewise in speech and mind,
in this life and in the next as well
one will always attain happiness.

Because body is always restrained,
the Sage is one who does no harm.
He goes toward the abode of the deathless,
and wherever he goes there is no sorrow.

Because speech is always restrained,
the Sage is one who does no harm.
He goes toward the abode of the deathless,
and wherever he goes there is no sorrow.

Because mind is always restrained,
the Sage is one who does no harm.
He goes toward the abode of the deathless,
and wherever he goes there is no sorrow.

Those who are steadfast restrain the body,
those who are steadfast restrain speech as well,
those who are steadfast restrain the mind —
if by steadfastness all is restrained,
they go toward the abode of the deathless,
and wherever they go there is no sorrow.

Restraint of body is excellent.
Restraint of speech is excellent.
Restraint of mind is also excellent.
To be restrained in every respect is excellent.
The monk who is restrained in all things
is fully liberated from all suffering.

Guard your speech, restrain your mind well,
and let the body commit no non-virtue.
If these three pathways of action are fully purified,
you will attain the path proclaimed by the sages.

Translator's notes

The opening verse identifies this text as the Udānavarga, the collection of inspired or purposeful utterances attributed to the Buddha and his circle, compiled in Sanskrit by Dharmatrāta and translated into Tibetan. The phrase rendered "holding his last bodily existence" refers doctrinally to the Buddha's final rebirth, the last life before parinirvāṇa, and signals that these are among his culminating teachings. The verse on the loom is a traditional Indian simile: as each new weft thread is inserted, it pushes the prior threads toward the end of the cloth, just as each moment of life presses one closer to death. The phrase "lord of death" renders the implied presence of Yama rather than any Tibetan term appearing explicitly here. The final verse's structure — evil deeds leading to hell, merit to happy rebirths, and the path to nirvāṇa — is a standard threefold soteriological summary. The phrase rendered as "living the holy life" translates a term equivalent to the Sanskrit brahmacarya, meaning the celibate, spiritually disciplined life of a renunciant; the Tibetan here is tshangs par spyad pa, a standard equivalent.

The line "what reason is there to rejoice" reflects a rhetorical question directed at those who take pleasure in worldly existence despite life's brevity.

The closing distinction between "desires" and "joys" in the final verses draws on a traditional doctrinal point: worldly pleasures and attachments, however pleasant, are themselves sources of suffering and fear, because what is loved can be lost. This is a standard teaching found in the Dhammapada and parallel canonical sources. The phrase rendered "like fish that have fallen into the net of the fisherman" translates ཚེ་དམར་ཆུད་པའི་ཉ་དང་འདྲ, which literally reads "like fish that have entered into the red of life" or "fallen into the living trap." The image is of fish caught in a weir or trap, a standard simile for beings ensnared in saṃsāra; the rendering reflects the doctrinal intent rather than a strictly literal gloss.

The verse on the gold-coin rain alludes to a well-known canonical utterance of the Buddha found in the Pali Dhammapada and parallel sources, affirming that even a shower of gold could not satisfy human craving — a point the text here applies within its broader anti-desire argument. The phrase rendered "craving's woman" translates the Tibetan literally, treating craving as a female figure personified, which is consistent with the Dhammapada tradition where taṇhā appears as a feminine noun and is occasionally anthropomorphized. Some translators render this more freely as "this craving, the woman," understanding it as an appositive rather than a genitive.

The expression "joints of clinging" or "junctions and pleasures" renders a term related to the accumulations or conjunctions of sensory delight; the reading here follows the standard commentarial understanding of worldly sense pleasures as objects of grasping.

The final verse of the craving chapter and the opening of the heedfulness chapter correspond closely to Dhammapada verses 21 and following, and the imagery throughout is consistent with that canonical context. The phrase rendered "heedfulness praised even by a hundredfold sacrifice" reflects a passage comparing the praise that Indra, king of the gods, received through great sacrificial rites; the Tibetan "brtsan sbyin brgya pa" alludes to Śakra's ancient epithet as "lord of a hundred sacrifices." The translation renders the doctrinal sense for a listening audience without embedding an obscure mythological reference in the flow.

The closing line of the passage breaks off mid-stanza in the source text; the translation faithfully preserves that incompleteness. The verse "Through appearing as what is good when it is not good" renders a triplet describing how heedless people are deceived by appearances — the not-good appears good, the not-dear appears dear, suffering appears as happiness. The Tibetan is compressed; the translation expands slightly for clarity in the spoken register.

The phrase rendered "the food of Māra that is so hard to abandon" translates a compound meaning literally "Māra's food, difficult to abandon"; in Buddhist doctrinal context this refers to sensory attachment, which sustains Māra's dominion over beings.

The three periods in "whichever of the three periods" refers to the three watches of the day or night — morning, midday, and evening — a standard division for practice in Vinaya and ethical literature. The passage is drawn from a Tibetan verse collection that closely parallels the Pāli Dhammapada and related Āgama material. The verse "life is dear to all / all tremble before being cut off" directly echoes Dhammapada 129–130 (Skt. Udānavarga 5.18–19), and the chapter colophon confirms this is the fifth chapter on what is dear (the chapter on "Priya" or the beloved). The final lines about the monk and moral discipline appear to be the opening of the following chapter and are left untranslated only at the point where the Tibetan text itself breaks off mid-sentence, which is reflected in the translation ending at the line break. The final verse is incomplete in the source text, breaking off mid-sentence. The translation reproduces this as it stands.
