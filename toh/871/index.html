About this text

The Noble Dhāraṇī Called "The Stainless" (Sanskrit: Ārya Vimala Nāma Dhāraṇī; Tibetan: 'Phags pa dri ma med pa zhes bya ba'i gzungs) is a short scriptural discourse centered on a dhāraṇī, a protective formula of the kind that is recited for spiritual benefit. It is set at Rājagṛha, on Vulture Peak, where the Buddha addresses the bodhisattva Viśuddhamati, explaining that the Tathāgata is completely pure because each of his qualities, among them moral discipline, meditative absorption, patience, and loving-kindness, is itself completely pure. The text thus joins a reflection on the perfected purity of a buddha with the practice of reciting a dhāraṇī named for that stainlessness. It is Toh 871 in the Kangyur, the Tibetan canon of translated words of the Buddha, and belongs to the class of dhāraṇī texts found among the tantra section.

Translator's notes

The Tibetan name of the bodhisattva literally means "Completely Pure Intelligence." It is given here in its Sanskrit form, Viśuddhamati. The series of verbs of veneration (honoring, revering, esteeming, worshipping, venerating) translates a stock sequence of terms for showing respect. In "because it abides in unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening," the Tibetan leaves the subject unstated. It could also be read as the practitioner who abides in, or is set toward, awakening. The interlocutor's name, Blo gros rnam par dag pa, is given as Viśuddhamati. Apart from the well-established names Akṣobhya and Amitābha, the buddha names in the list are rendered as English translations of the Tibetan, since their Sanskrit originals cannot be reconstructed with confidence. The mantra syllables are transliterated as the Tibetan gives them, including the irregular forms "amitā mate" and "saumanasya mupanaya" (perhaps for saumanasyam upanaya, "bring joy"). The mantras are given in a reconstructed Sanskrit romanization. The second mantra contains several syllables of uncertain form, such as kucchalo, kotthālo and ramṛti, which are transcribed as they stand in the Tibetan. "The three times" in the verse is taken to mean the three times of day, as it usually does in recitation instructions.
