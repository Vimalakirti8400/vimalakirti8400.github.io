About this text

The Stages of the Great Drop, in Sanskrit Mahātilakakrama and in Tibetan Tiklé Chenpö Rimpa, is a short Indian verse treatise of completion-stage instruction in the Hevajra tradition, attributed in its colophon to the Indian preceptor Vilāsavajra. In four chapters it moves from the stage of abiding by nature, where the aggregates, elements and sacred sites are shown to be the deities and places of the Hevajra maṇḍala within the body, through entry into great primordial wisdom by way of the channels, wheels and drops of the subtle body, to the great primordial wisdom of self-experience gained through the practice with a consort and the four joys, and finally to the ultimate stage in which innate primordial wisdom, beyond all activity and description, is realized as mahāmudrā. Written in a dense and often cryptic esoteric style, it is Toh 1290 in the Tengyur, where it is placed in the section of tantra commentaries among the works connected with the Hevajra cycle.

Translation

In the language of India: mahātilakakrama nāma. In the language of Tibet: The Stages of the Great Drop.

Homage to glorious Hevajra.

Intrinsic nature free of duality, like gold, the ground from which qualities are drawn forth;
the very continuum to be entered, which makes itself manifest;
self-experience, hero of heroes, the self of the instant of the still and the moving;
abiding at the limit, sky free of concepts, every task without exception complete;
by the words "ocean" and "weapon," primordially passed beyond sorrow:
to that one who rules by the force of self-experience I pay homage and bow.

Not following the three and the two, beginning with analysis, by means of method,
since a pointing finger does not of itself draw out the meaning,
who among the many fortunate here has faith in an uncertain introduction?
The light of the sun illuminates; therefore, the instruction on method.
Therefore, for the Mother Tantra, a burning fire and poured water,
for persons without fortune bound to it by their own connection,
a small collection of terms illustrated by the river
I shall set out for the sake of the dull like me.

The intrinsic nature of the first treatise
is pure from the beginning, like gold in ore.
Because intrinsic nature is emptiness,
by nature it is unborn, and yet it is not empty either.
As desire is satisfied in a dream,
so through delusion one accomplishes one's own aim.
Appearance without basis, like a golden tree,
is not blocked; its meaning should be known.

The aggregate of form is Vajrā;
feeling itself is called Gaurī;
perception is the Water Yoginī;
formations are Vajraḍākinī;
since consciousness has the essence of Nairātmyā,
know it as nirvāṇa by nature.
Form is Gaurī, sound is Caurī,
smell is Vetālī, and Ghasmarī;
for touch, Khecarī and Bhūcarī.
Because they always abide of themselves as dharmas,
the nirvāṇa of enjoyment is complete.
Akṣobhya is water, and that is Vairocana;
Ratnasambhava of the sun, Amitābha, mind;
with Amoghasiddhi, these are the five lords.
Delusion and the rest, the five faculties,
by nature primordially uncreated,
pure in their own nature, are without bondage.

Then the natural vow
and the inner ten grounds should be known in sequence:
pīṭha and upapīṭha, and likewise kṣetra,
charnel ground, chandoha, melāpaka and the rest.
The body of the Victor, the great self,
is to be known as outer, likewise as inner,
and likewise as secret.
The moving of the feet, in brief, is known as warmth;
the gathering is always shown as water;
in emptiness, by shape, know it as fourfold.
The wheel abides likewise as bliss;
it is the bhaga, which is space.
The anus is the fierce great charnel ground;
the secret place is likewise the one with shape;
the ears are called the wild one and the bell;
the mouth appears as the blazing thicket;
the unbearable host of hells is shown as the eyes.
It is the substance of the śiri tree,
with flowers, fruit and faculties,
having one pillar and nine doors.

Nairātmyā and Heruka are bliss:
method and wisdom primordially joined.
Sun and moon are the seats of right and left,
joined as nondual, always subtle.
In the host of mothers, Vajraḍākinī
is the indivisible, subtle Vajrā;
Gaurī is the left, the loving one;
the short one, the tortoise, know as water;
the power of meditation is shown as Vajraḍākinī.
The faulty one, the tail, is Pukkasī;
Śavarī is the mother, the sign;
in heat and cool, Caṇḍālī;
Ḍombī, the one who inclines and brings contentment;
Gaurī, the door guardian, is form in general itself;
Caurī, giver of the cause, free of union;
Vetālī, the one who gathers and accomplishes;
Ghasmarī, the good-minded one, the measure;
at the threefold door, through the desirous one, the Wanderer;
Khecarī, the householder, is shown as Caṇḍālī.
The maṇḍala of the ḍākinīs is uncreated:
the body and the mudrā, the host of consorts,
the fruit, bliss, pure in an instant;
one should always make it into its very nature.

Beginning from four and two, up to sixty,
these and the rest are seventy-two thousand;
more extensively, they cannot be told.
The Victor taught them as natural emanations.
The secret should likewise be known.
The elements, in their sequence, abide in the bhaga.
Through the gathering of the openings it is earth itself;
through abundant bliss, know it as butter.
The three bodies are possessed by nature from the beginning.
In a world pure by nature
there is no bondage whatsoever.
Whatever the lesser ones seek to eliminate
is just so, by its very nature.

From the Glorious Stages of the Great Drop, the stage of abiding by nature: the first.

What sets things in place should be known as generation;
being with method, it serves the aim of the lesser.
The deities arise from sound and meaning;
the true vow should also be known there.
Purifying on the wheels,
since one consumes oneself through one's own bliss,
freed from conceptual thought, one purifies the ten grounds;
know them as the nine vajra holders.

It is not spoken of conventionally:
at the navel, wisdom, exhausted at the limit;
method, know it at the tip of the anther.
At the root are sixty-four petals;
in eight groups, the ends are empty,
having the essence of the unborn;
there, know the central seat.
Examined as blazing and moving, as action,
it is to be known as the first of action.
The center of the ground is shown as dharma;
before it there is one eighth part.
The four channels are the four inner mātṛkās;
the others are taught definitively in the tantra.
The essence is explained as the arising of the aggregates;
this is always shown as dharma;
know the essence as the dharmakāya.
That itself is the essence of primordial wisdom.
This wheel is the element of space,
the great one pervading all things.
That itself is enjoyed in emptiness;
since the elements in the four intervals are empty,
it has the essence of the five primordial wisdoms.
Through attachment one always binds oneself;
with wrong views it is always hard to be freed;
with going out and coming in unobstructed, one becomes free.
The lotus endowed with marks, great bliss,
always indestructible, bounded by the expanse:
in fully knowing the nature as dharmakāya,
know that it has six doors.
Through primordial wisdom made familiar by samādhi,
in every ground and place there is great bliss.
There is no fixed rule for the arising of primordial wisdom.
The maṇḍala with its limbs is the nirmāṇakāya;
that itself, made blissful by bliss,
is the saṃbhogakāya, Hevajra.
On the path of action one enters as others do.

Having opened the door of the vital point, oṃ tra,
the primordial wisdom of the directions is made by oneself;
entering the first door of the blazing holder,
in the middle of the path where nectar drips,
by the strength of samādhi one makes it straight.
Having found it, there is enjoyment and nirvāṇa.
The three bodies, and the four as well, are not apprehended.
Whoever abides in the bliss of the mind itself,
through familiarity does not apprehend even light;
and if not even a hundredth part of the tip of a hair
is apprehended at the end,
bliss is not experienced by the mind,
and one abides in no extreme at all:
know that the conduct is enjoyment there.
The two bodies too, which seek the welfare of beings,
are unobstructed, in the manner of sound.
The primordial wisdom that is natural appearance,
since it is not two, appearance and emptiness,
is always called the great drop.

From the Glorious Stages of the Great Drop, the stage of entering great primordial wisdom: the second.

For the intelligent one who wishes to abide in self-experience,
the object that is hard to know
bears signs that change with her own family:
a hundred, ten, and twice six,
half white and black, or neither white nor black;
black hair, long eyes, a deep navel,
avoiding both thin and stout, both tall and short;
a straight nose, slender waist and broad hips,
large petals and deep within;
firm breasts, little illness, much camphor,
richly endowed with virtuous conduct;
inside and out her body's fluids and breath are fragrant;
gentle, composed and unwavering.
Her divine family too should be examined.
Having found her, give her the treatise in stages;
this is called the dharma of the king's daughter.
The stages of samaya, of food and the rest:
having known these without conceptual thought,
and having properly given the essential fluid,
one should also know the four kinds of wind.
By thoroughly arousing the organ and the one the organ delights,
bliss is produced in the palace.

The places and the stages are to be known:
emanation, dharma, and great bliss;
know that joy and bliss abide there.
Joy, supreme joy, the joy of cessation, and the innate:
know these as the essence of the treatise.
The instant, likewise the moment and the interval,
are distinguished by the action of wind.
Variety, and that which is free of day and night,
are said to be the very stages of action.
Appearance, increase, and attainment
arise from the true, stainless light.
Between day and night, and neither of the two:
these are names for the meeting of two kinds of things.
The class, the mudrā, the mother from whom they arise,
and the supreme and the rest, are also named by occasion.
Lalanā, rasanā and avadhūtī,
where the three families abide, are also the path of bliss.
By sun and moon, method and wisdom,
one's own aims too are taken up by that.
The conventional also appears as two.
Well known, within the life force, is that very self;
it is within, with the character of a knot;
the Teacher also taught joy in its portions.
The tip is a line of twelve finger widths;
know its foot as enjoyment.
Eight by eight, one joins the stages to the essence;
that is taught as the meaning of the nature.
Bliss through appearance is joy;
outer touch is said to be the second;
bliss through meeting is cessation;
in its place the innate arises.
Know that as the common stage.
On the stages of the wheels, abiding in sequence,
that outer meaning is also inner.
Within the chamber are three and two grains;
at the tip, without interval, likewise in stages.
Having known that within the jewel,
these are the sixteen stages of the anther;
my drop abides at the limit.
Having known it as the domain of the mind,
know the inner grounds, the pīṭha and the rest.
The secret stages of the grounds end in joy;
method and wisdom are the ground of a buddha;
at the perineum, at completion, is Vajradhara,
wholly beyond the two appearances.

Having thus known the stages from the grounds:
just as a body burned by fire
is to be treated with heat by fire itself,
so one burned by the fire of desire
is thoroughly cleansed by passion itself.
Whatever deeds of fearsome people
bind them, one by one,
if joined with method, by those very deeds
one is freed from the bonds of existence.
Those of wrong view, śrāvakas and the unfortunate denigrate this;
therefore liberation is said to be far from them.

Through the thorough union of vajra and lotus,
by solidity, emanation is explained as earth;
moisture, the dharma, is Māmakī;
by movement, Pāṇḍaravāsinī, enjoyment perfected;
drawing in, Tārā, great bliss;
hūṃ phaṭ: by the power of one's own wind
one should examine from the limit of the supreme.
What is known through the meeting of things
is neither union nor separation.
The thing joined with the minds of sun and moon
is called the innate thing.
What mingles without any falling,
know all of it as bliss arising from method.
That nature is called the innate;
because it is the joy of suchness,
it is called the great delight.
Because it binds all dharmas into one,
the vow too is called the great drop.
For whoever abides in the great vow
there is no guarding and no transgressing.
Since method and wisdom do not exist,
there is no desire and no freedom from desire.
Even the Middle Way does not become an object;
so it is explained in the Yoga Saṃpuṭa.
To clarify the reasons in detail
is only one's own mind's conceptual thought.
It comes to be known through the guru, the right time, method, the mudrā,
and one's own merit.

Having found that primordial wisdom from before,
the body of karma itself no longer appears,
and the things of method and wisdom become mingled:
in a single instant, perfect awakening.
Beyond this there are no stages.
Even if one does not become pure,
through the focus of anuyoga
one is always endowed with entry into self-experience.
If one need not exhaust the observance,
like a man who has found a lamp,
even if one gives up the fourfold mudrā,
bliss will never decline.
It is accomplished at completion, without any action.
Its qualities, set out in full, could never be told.

From the Glorious Stages of the Great Drop, the stage of the great primordial wisdom of self-experience: the third.

The yogin whose primordial wisdom is perfected,
having found that natural primordial wisdom,
is by nature free of the extremes of many and one:
a body beyond existence, nonexistence and the middle.
There is no self and no other, and there no knowing.
The lesser, the middling and the supreme,
and whatever others there may be, these too
are known in oneself as one, of equal taste.
Apart from innate primordial wisdom
no other nature of the still and the moving is seen.
However it is done, that is just what it is.
By meditating on color, shape and emptiness
this meaning can never be found,
because those are taught in the stage of action.
Mahāmudrā is free of any cause.
The primordial wisdom that is self-aware clarity
is in no way empty;
therefore, whatever kinds of things to be accomplished are found
are said to be spontaneously present by nature.
Like the qualities of a precious jewel,
it never takes delight in anything at all.
Since it does not appear as an object,
it is the supreme primordial wisdom, unceasing through the three times.
Freed from activities such as fire offerings and instruction,
it is found abiding in self-experiencing awareness.
That very point was also taught by the Teacher:

"The knowledge being, the accomplished vajra holder, has abandoned all things and abides in all things. He has abandoned all activities, yet obstructs no activity whatsoever. He is nothing at all. He has no equal in any quality. He goes nowhere and abides nowhere. He is wholly free of beginning, end and the rest. He appears as the three limbs of approach. He has gone everywhere, knows everything, is everything, is the aim of all, and abides in the minds of all. He is wholly freed from all the lower realms, adorned with all qualities, possessed of every color and shape, of benefit to all, of unequaled virtue, arising not only once, a protector and savior, free of all conceptual thought, and free of every color and shape. Because he is profound, he is not the domain of ordinary beings. Because he is lord of all, he is king. Because he is hard to split, he is vajra. Because he is freed from the burning torment of the afflictions, he is the moon. Because he illuminates everything, he is the sun. Because he is without fear, he is a lion. Because his depth is hard to fathom, he is a great mass of water. Because he is the ground of all, he is all of space. Because he is spontaneously present, he is the body of essential nature." This and more he taught at length.

From the Glorious Stages of the Great Drop, the stage of abiding in the ultimate meaning: the fourth.

From the great ocean tantra of Hevajra,
nature, entry, experience and fruit, like a ripened harvest:
working earnestly, like a victor, for the sake of the borderlands,
by the garland of the swan's four waters, may it be brought to completion.

The instruction on the completion stage called the Glorious Stages of the Great Drop, composed by the Indian preceptor, the guru Vilāsavajra, is complete.

Translator's notes

This is a highly condensed esoteric verse text, and many lines, especially the correspondences between the goddesses, the sacred sites and parts of the body, are cryptic; they have been rendered literally. In the opening verse, "ocean" and "weapon" may be code words for numbers (four and five) rather than plain nouns. In the list of the ten sacred sites, ཚད་ནྡོ is read as a partial transliteration of chandoha. The line rendered "at the threefold door, through the desirous one, the Wanderer" (སུམ་སྒོར་འདོད་མས་སྤྱོད་མ) seems to be missing a syllable and may have named Bhūcarī. The phrase rendered "know it as butter" (མར) is uncertain. This piece describes the consort to be sought for the practice of karmamudrā and the stages of bliss in esoteric terms; many lines are cryptic and have been rendered literally. "A hundred, ten, and twice six" is unclear in reference. The word rendered "virtuous conduct" (སི་ལ) is taken as a transliteration of śīla; "much camphor" may be a coded term for a bodily substance. The phrase rendered "perineum" (པ་ཤང) is uncertain. The line rendered "it never takes delight in anything at all" (དགའ་བ་མེད) may be a slip for དགག་པ་མེད, "it obstructs nothing," which would better suit the comparison with a wish-fulfilling jewel. The third line of the closing verse is obscure and has been rendered literally.
