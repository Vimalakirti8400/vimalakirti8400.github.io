About this text

The Commentary on the Praise of the Three Jewels, known in Sanskrit as the Triratnastotravṛtti and in Tibetan as Dkon mchog gsum la bstod pa'i 'grel pa, is a short explanatory treatise on a hymn of praise to the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Saṅgha. Opening with homage to Mañjuśrī in youthful form, it explains that honoring the Three Jewels, the gurus of the threefold existence, is the cause of every result beings wish for. It then works through the root verses phrase by phrase, beginning with the praise of the Buddha as chief. It unpacks the meaning of the word "buddha" in two ways: as one fully awakened from the sleep of both kinds of ignorance, and as one whose intelligence has blossomed like a lotus to encompass all that can be known. It is Toh 1145 in the Tengyur, where it belongs among the collection of praises and their commentaries.

Translation

In the language of India: triratna stotra bṛbhi. In the language of Tibet: Commentary on the Praise of the Three Jewels.

Homage to the Blessed One, Mañjuśrī in youthful form.

Paying homage to the Three Jewels, the gurus of the threefold existence, and similar acts are the cause that produces every result that beings wish for. Therefore I pay homage to them.

The text says, "Homage to the Buddha, the chief," and so on. To have abandoned without remainder both kinds of ignorance, the unmixed and the associated, is to be a buddha, an "awakened one," like someone fully awakened from sleep. Or else, because he has abandoned non-afflicted unknowing, his intelligence has opened out to all that can be known, and so he has "blossomed," like a lotus in full bloom. Read this way, he is praised for his excellence of abandonment and his excellence of wisdom, which is the excellence of his own aim.

With regard to his excellence in the aim of others, the word "chief" is used: he is chief because he has become the great lord of the three realms. He is weighty because he outweighs all of them without exception, just as something heavy in form presses down the end of a balance. This shows that he overpowers all and tames them. Or else, he is the guru because he bestows the jewels of unerring qualities. "Homage to him" means bowing down deeply with the three doors.

As for "Homage to the Dharma that protects": it is called "protecting" because it protects from the suffering of the lower destinies and of existence, dispels suffering, and gives happiness. For that very reason it is Dharma, because it holds one back from falling. That is so because whoever realizes the ultimate Dharma does not fall again into the lower realms or into saṃsāra. The indirect cause of realizing it is the Dharma of the teaching, and the direct cause is the Dharma of the path, so these too are figuratively called "holding." Or else, the word "protecting" shows its excellence in benefiting others, while the word "Dharma" shows its excellence in holding its own pure characteristic. "Homage to it" is as before.

As for "Homage to the great Saṅgha": these are the Saṅgha because not even the māras who are sons of gods can separate them from the Teacher or from the view. They cannot be separated from the Teacher because they have attained faith through knowing. They cannot be separated from the view because they have attained the pure wisdom of selflessness. Here, whether they are śrāvakas, pratyekabuddhas, or bodhisattvas, the noble ones who have abandoned the three fetters, whether still in training or beyond training, are the Saṅgha. The perfectly complete Buddha, however, has abandoned all the obscurations to the knowable and has completed the entire accumulation of merit, so he is not to be counted within the Saṅgha. Because he is worthy of worship by all the excellent beings of the three realms, both those in training and those beyond training, he is called the great arhat.

Such inseparability is the attainment of their own aim, and the word "great" is used because they bring about the aim of others. These are great because they greatly purify the offerings of others: through the two acts of offering and of making use of what is offered, the supreme merit arising from the goods of beings increases. Moreover, those in training and those beyond training, each as is fitting, still have remaining portions of craving to be abandoned by meditation, the subtle desire that longs for food, clothing, and the like; and because they bear the five aggregates, the result of past maturation, they still need to be sustained by food and so forth. So they make use of offerings, whereas the Buddha and the Dharma are not like that. "Homage to them" is the same as before.

The meaning of "Always I bow to these three" is easy to understand.

The maturation result of paying homage in this way is to attain the crown protuberance whose top cannot be seen, chief of all the excellent marks of a buddha. Among results corresponding to the cause, the result corresponding in experience is that when one is born in saṃsāra one's head becomes like a parasol, and that one comes to delight in paying homage to the Three Jewels. The dominant result is that pleasing external objects appear to one's cognition as though bowing down, and do not appear as though turning away haughtily or impossible to obtain.

Thus, in order to show clearly the praise of the Three Jewels that was spoken in brief, so that excellent beings might have even greater faith, and in order to gather his own stainless accumulation, the master Mātṛceṭa spoke a praise, saying:

The two great accumulations completed, the four knowledges and three bodies accomplished,

and so on. These are "accumulations" because through them completion is brought about: they are the six perfections beginning with generosity. They are "great" because they are immeasurable in nature, and are gathered over immeasurable eons and toward immeasurable objects. "Two" refers to merit and wisdom. Generosity, moral discipline, and patience are merit; meditative concentration and wisdom are wisdom; and diligence belongs to both. So it is taught in the noble Sūtra Unraveling the Intent.

Their completion is this: in the first incalculable great eon one trains in the path of preparation; in the second one trains up to the seventh ground; and in the third one trains and purifies up to the tenth ground, and so they become complete and reach their culmination.

Having thus shown the gathering of the special cause, he then speaks of "the four knowledges" and so on in order to show the result. The four knowledges are the four wisdoms beginning with mirror-like wisdom. Mirror-like wisdom is the transformed foundation consciousness, the wisdom that knows all knowables without obstruction. The wisdom of equality is the transformed afflicted mind, the wisdom that knows selflessness as selfless. Discriminating wisdom is the transformed mental consciousness, which knows all conventional things without exception, without mixing them, like the petals of a lotus. All-accomplishing wisdom is the transformed consciousness of the five doors, which knows how to carry out, without exception, whatever activity there is for taming beings.

Their summary is shown by the words "three bodies accomplished." The result of the great accumulation of merit is the accomplishment of the two form bodies, and the result of the great accumulation of wisdom is the accomplishment of the dharma body. Here, mirror-like wisdom is the dharma body, the wisdom that is free from grasped and grasper, mere natural luminosity.

Its qualities, the ten powers, the four fearlessnesses and so forth, are many distinct wisdoms gathered together, and so it is a collection of qualities. It is called the dharma body because the word kāya, "body," is used for a collection. In actual fact, that wisdom is the nondual wisdom of the mind of the enjoyment body.

Discriminating wisdom and the wisdom of equality are the enjoyment body. That is, the inner wisdom of that body, freed from dependence on distinct sense bases, knows the many conventional things, and by showing itself as a body it tames whoever is fit to be tamed.

So here, the basis of all the bodies of the buddhas is the maturation body. From its nonconceptual wisdom and from the radiance of the light rays of the wisdom of the five doors, the dharma body and the emanation body are posited, and that very body, together with the wisdom that supports those two, is called the enjoyment body.

Alternatively, the special body brought about by the infinite accumulation of merit, and its light rays appearing as special forms, are the two form bodies, since whatever becomes an object of the eye is form. The wisdom that appears as natural luminosity is the principal one, and the knowing of variety and the knowing of selflessness should also be said to arise from the accumulation of wisdom.

Mere selflessness, which is other than that, is posited as the ultimate Dharma Jewel. It is not posited as the Buddha, for it is the object of the wisdom of the Victors, and it should be posited as the result to be attained. Therefore the bodies that have their own defining characteristics should be stated as three only.

In order to show clearly the nature of those three bodies through their activity, their example and their characteristics, the passage beginning

"Without conceptuality, you know anything at all vividly,"

was spoken. Because it is free from the three or four kinds of conceptuality, it is without conceptuality: it is the great wisdom that has uprooted every form of dualistic grasping. Therefore the negation in the word "without" is an implicative negation, for those kinds of conceptuality do not exist, while the wisdom devoid of them, naturally luminous, is present.

Therefore, by it, "anything at all," that is, any phenomenon whatsoever, from the most subtle particle up to Mount Meru, is known "vividly," that is, with utter clarity. If one asks how it knows them: it knows them free from the superimposition and denial of conceptuality that works through verbal generalities, without their obscuring one another, without obstruction, without darkness, in an undivided aspect that is clear and lucid like crystal. That itself is the activity of that wisdom.

Its example is shown by the words "the dharma body is like space." Just as unconditioned space is unobstructed and does not obscure, so mirror-like wisdom too is unobstructed and unobscured with regard to all knowable things, and appears without its objects and aspects being cut off. That is the meaning.

Here, the remaining three wisdoms are wisdoms with distinct, determined objects, because they know forms and so on, the mere selflessness of persons, and the dispositions, latent tendencies and so forth of the beings to be tamed together with the means of taming them. This wisdom, however, knows everything, both the subjects, which are those very wisdoms together with their objects, and their nature, natural luminosity, which is the general characteristic. It knows all of them, subjects together with their nature.

"The form body, possessing a beautiful body" refers to the enjoyment body and the emanation body, blazing with the major and minor marks. The meaning of "buddha" is as explained before.

"Like a wish-fulfilling tree" is the example for all three bodies. Because they completely fulfill the hopes of beings just as those beings have conceived and wished for in their minds, they are like the four great wish-fulfilling trees of the gods. It is as in the praise that says:

"You, wish-fulfilling tree of merit, from where have you come today?"

Moreover, since all three of these bodies abide in the nature of holding the countless seeds of former aspirations, the benefit of those to be tamed now comes about effortlessly, just as the aspirations were made before. In particular, the form bodies, with unobstructed power, also engage directly in bringing to maturity bodhisattvas, individuals of limited scope, and beings.

Some say: "'The form body' is the general statement, 'possessing a beautiful body' is the enjoyment body, 'like a wish-fulfilling tree' is the emanation body, and 'buddha' refers to all three bodies."

To those three bodies of such a kind, emanating in devotion bodies as numerous as the atoms of the realms, I bow down: that is what is meant, and the meaning of the words is as before.

"The twelve branches of scripture, concordant cause of the nature of reality, and" praises the Dharma of the teachings. As for "the nature of reality": the nature of reality is emptiness, devoid of the two kinds of self. It is the ultimate essence, the general characteristic, natural luminosity, which bears the characteristic of being selfless and free of self.

Its concordant cause is the Dharma of the teachings, the scriptures that teach selflessness. Here, "concordant cause" means a cause that accords with it: it is fit to generate and able to bring about attainment. Fit to generate means that this cause generates the unerring consummate wisdom. Able to bring about attainment means that, once that wisdom is born, one is able to attain the unchanging consummate, suchness. Because these are supremely excellent speech, they are called "excellent speech." The words of non-Buddhists lack the fitness and ability just described, and so they are not excellent.

The twelve are the discourses, the sung verses, the expositions, the verses, the inspired utterances, the introductions, the narratives, the accounts of what happened, the birth stories, the extensive teachings, the marvels, and the established instructions. First they resounded from the mouth of the Teacher. After that they dwelt in the minds of individuals. After that they were written down in volumes. Moreover, first, by focusing on them, the wisdom arising from hearing and reflection is born, and then, by meditating, one attains that nature of reality. Therefore it is certain that they are a concordant cause only indirectly.

Therefore the master Vasubandhu said in the Principles of Exegesis (Vyākhyāyukti):

"Come from the Teacher's mouth, it soothes the cataract that clouds people's eyes; this source of all that is auspicious, set it in your mind! For it is nectar to the ears of embodied beings, and it clears away the inner depths of the afflictions; therefore reflect rightly on its meaning. For these reasons it is fitting always to listen with reverence to the words of the Buddha."

Next come the lines:

"The Dharma, without arising or ceasing, isolated from elaboration,
by focusing on which all qualities are accomplished."

These praise the ultimate Dharma. Dharma here means the sacred Dharma. In it, ultimately, there is no arising and no ceasing. Moreover, it is isolated from elaborations, that is, from the elaborations of permanence and annihilation, of coming and going, of oneness and difference.

The sacred Dharma has selflessness as its nature. Those elaborations are nothing but imputations made under the power of grasping at a self. So the moment self-grasping is reversed, they too cease. In the state of selflessness they never existed from the very beginning; they are only adventitious imputations.

Selflessness is thus free of elaboration. When one focuses on it as selfless by means of the three kinds of wisdom, then immediately, as self-grasping and the rest come to an end, all qualities are accomplished. These are the common supramundane qualities, the many kinds of fruits of the noble ones beginning with stream-entry, and the uncommon qualities such as the powers and the fearlessnesses. This accords with the saying, "The noble persons come forth from the unconditioned."

The words "To the sacred Dharma, cause of the good" praise the Dharma of the path. Here "the good" is the definitive good, the bliss of peace. Its "cause" is what produces it, because the path gives rise to the wisdom of selflessness, and through that wisdom the definitive good is attained. In this sense, the vajra-like samādhi that arises just before the knowledge of exhaustion and non-arising is the ultimate cause of the good. The wisdoms from the path of seeing up to the point that this samādhi brings to completion should also be understood as causes of the good. This same Dharma is "sacred," because it is experienced by the noble ones and because it holds one back from downfall. That is why it is called the sacred Dharma. The words "I bow to it" are to be understood as before.

The passage beginning:

"The obscurations of afflictions and the obscurations to the knowable
are removed in stages by the antidote, and they enter the grounds"

praises the noble Saṅgha. Afflictions are so called because they thoroughly afflict the mind; they are the six, beginning with attachment. These are themselves obscurations, because they obscure the wisdom that knows exhaustion and non-arising and obstruct its seeing. The knowable is the all-pervading dharmadhātu, luminous by nature. The obscuration to it is the grasping that takes the verbal universal, the general idea a word expresses, to be something real. In fact it is every kind of conceptual thought of grasped object and grasping subject. Both of these obscurations have many subdivisions, so the word for them is in the plural.

"By the antidote" means by the wisdom that knows the two kinds of selflessness. "Removed in stages" means that they are not abandoned all at once. First what is to be abandoned by seeing is abandoned, and after that what is to be abandoned by meditation.

The result of this is taught by the words "they enter the grounds." The grounds are the supramundane grounds: the ground of the eighth, of seeing, of thinning, of freedom from attachment, of having done what was to be done, and entry into the ground of the pratyekabuddhas; and again, entry into the grounds from the Very Joyful and the Stainless up to the Cloud of Dharma.

Here, by the sixteen moments of wisdom that attend to the four truths in their sixteen aspects, the eighty-eight latent tendencies are abandoned. On the path of seeing this is the ground of the eighth, or the ground of the Very Joyful. After that, by the same method, the ten to be abandoned by meditation are abandoned, and on this basis the remaining grounds of the śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas are set out. When the desire realm's afflictions have been abandoned up to the third degree, up to the sixth, and up to the ninth, and when the ninth degree of the Peak of Existence has been exhausted, there are the grounds of seeing, of thinning, of freedom from attachment, and of having done what was to be done. Abandoning everything at once and completing the path is the ground of the rhinoceros-like.

The remaining bodhisattva grounds should be understood as set out according to the completion of the perfections. For bodhisattvas, the afflictive obscurations to be abandoned by meditation are abandoned all at once by the vajra-like samādhi. The obscurations to the knowable, however, are abandoned gradually, ground by ground. Some say that the afflictive obscurations too are abandoned gradually, ground by ground, and that what abandons them is the wisdom of the selflessness of phenomena.

Having thus explained abiding in the accumulation of wisdom, the text now shows abiding in the accumulation of merit, and so it says:

"Accomplishing the aims of beings and purifying buddha fields."

The aims of sentient beings are their needs, namely their happiness and their benefit. Accomplishing these means doing so as far as one is able, through the means of attraction, the immeasurables, and the perfections. The buddha fields are the environment and its inhabitants. Purifying them is as taught in the Mother of the Victors: "When one sees beings poorly clothed, one should make the aspiration, 'May the beings in my buddha field be adorned with clothing and ornaments.' When one offers flowers, one should make the aspiration, 'May flowers of every season bloom in profusion in my buddha field,'" and so on. In brief, it should be understood that buddha fields are purified through the power of aspiration.

On the words "To the great noble Saṅgha": they are noble because they have gone far from evil and unwholesome things. Saṅgha is as explained before. They are great in number and great in qualities, because they are limitless in number and possess great qualities such as having entered well.

"I bow" means bowing deeply with every limb of the body, together with praise.

Having thus bowed to the Three Jewels, in which all qualities are gathered, if one goes to them for refuge, one will not be born in the lower realms but will be born in the abodes of the gods. As it is said: "Going for refuge to the three is a door of the light of Dharma; it is the complete passing beyond the bad destinies." And it is also said: "If, with a deeply clear mind and an upright heart, with a lucid mind not distracted elsewhere, one goes for refuge to the Buddha, the Dharma, or the Saṅgha, then when one dies, by the cause of that deeply clear mind and that going for refuge, one will be born in the happy destiny, in the heavenly world called Dwelling at the Edge."

Born there, his body will be endowed with splendor, adorned with its own light, and he will dwell in happiness." So it is said.

If that virtue of paying homage and going for refuge is joined with dedication, it should be understood to become a cause of liberation, in accordance with how it was dedicated.

Thus, the Three Jewels have abandoned every fault
and become a treasury of boundless qualities.
By explaining a little the words of their praise,
whatever gathering of virtue I have gained,
through that, may all beings
come to abide on the ground of buddhahood.

This completes the Commentary on the Praise of the Three Jewels, composed by the master Jinaputra. It was translated, edited and finalized by the Indian preceptor Jñānaśānti and the Tibetan translator, the monk Palgyi Lhünpo Dé.

Translator's notes

The passage on "weighty" plays on the Sanskrit guru, meaning both "heavy" and "teacher." I read སྲོང་གི་མགོ as སྲང་གི་མགོ, "the end of a balance," and the verb འདོན་པ as "outweighs." Near the end, ཚོགས་པོ་པོའི་འབྲས་བ is evidently a slip for ཚོགས་ཆེན་པོའི་འབྲས་བུ, "the result of the great accumulation," and is translated that way. The passage on results names only the result corresponding in experience but lists two items under it. It is translated as written. The commentator glosses the root-verse term usually understood as "outflow" or "concordant result" (niṣyanda) as "concordant cause," and the translation follows his gloss. The "unerring consummate" and "unchanging consummate" are the two kinds of consummate nature (pariniṣpanna) in Yogācāra. The phrase rendered "clear and lucid like crystal" may intend two separate similes; the sense is unaffected. In the explanation of "by the antidote," the Tibetan reads "by both" in the lemma and "selflessness antidote" in the gloss. The two words appear to have been swapped, so I have translated the evident reading: "'By the antidote' means by the wisdom that knows the two kinds of selflessness."

In the list of śrāvaka grounds, the text reads "higher than" where "seeing" fits the series that follows (seeing, thinning, freedom from attachment, having done what was to be done). I have translated it as "seeing." The pairing of each degree of abandonment with a ground is implied by the order of the list rather than stated outright.

"Dwelling at the Edge" is a literal rendering of the name of a heavenly world that I cannot confidently identify with a Sanskrit name.

The saying "The noble persons come forth from the unconditioned" echoes the Diamond Sūtra's statement that noble persons are distinguished by the unconditioned. The ground of buddhahood renders the stage, or bhūmi, of a buddha.
