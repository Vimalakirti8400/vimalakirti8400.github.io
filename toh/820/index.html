About this text

Toh 820 in the Kangyur is a short liturgical text known in Tibetan as the Auspicious Verses of the Thirty-Seven Deities, belonging to the class of Yoga Tantra. The text consists of a series of benedictory verses invoking the auspiciousness of the thirty-seven deities of the maṇḍala of the Yoga Tantra, organized around the five buddha families — Vairocana, Akṣobhya, Ratnasambhava, Amitābha, and Amoghasiddhi — along with their consorts, bodhisattvas, and retinue figures including Vajrasattva. Each verse calls upon the merit and blessings of a particular deity or group of deities and dedicates that auspiciousness to all beings in the present moment. Framed as verses requested by a divine interlocutor, the text functions as a maṅgala or auspiciousness prayer and is placed within the tantra section of the Kangyur.

Translation

Whoever is a wise one who offers praise —
upon that one may good fortune always come.

Here ends the auspicious verses requested by the god.

Homage to the Three Jewels.

Homage to glorious Vajrasattva. The auspicious verses of the thirty-seven deities — the deities of the maṇḍala of the glorious Yoga Tantra, the five families of Tathāgatas together with their retinues.

Whatever auspiciousness belongs to the Blessed Ones — Vairocana, the Victor Akṣobhya, the Jewel-Born, Amitābha, and Amoghasiddhi — the Sugatas of the five families: by that supreme auspiciousness, may there be auspiciousness here and now.

Whatever auspiciousness belongs to the supreme Mother Blessed Ones — Vajradhātvīśvarī, Māmakī, Dharmadhātvīśvarī, and Karma­dhātvīśvarī: by that supreme auspiciousness, may there be auspiciousness here and now.

Whatever auspiciousness belongs to the retinue of the Blessed One Akṣobhya — Vajrasattva, Vajrarāja, Vajrarāga, and Vajrasādhu: by that supreme auspiciousness, may there be auspiciousness here and now.

Whatever auspiciousness belongs to the retinue of the Blessed One Ratnasambhava — Vajraratna, Vajratejas, Vajradhvaja, and Vajrahāsa: by that supreme auspiciousness, may there be auspiciousness here and now.

Whatever auspiciousness belongs to the retinue of the Blessed One Amitābha — Vajradharma, Vajratīkṣṇa, Vajracakra, and Vajrabhāṣa: by that supreme auspiciousness, may there be auspiciousness here and now.

Whatever auspiciousness belongs to the retinue of the Blessed One Amoghasiddhi — Vajrakarma, Vajrarakṣa, Vajrayakṣa, and Vajramuṣṭi: by that supreme auspiciousness, may there be auspiciousness here and now.

Whatever auspiciousness belongs to the goddess-deities of the inner offerings — the goddess of grace, the goddess of garlands, the goddess of song, and the goddess of dance: by that supreme auspiciousness, may there be auspiciousness here and now.

Whatever auspiciousness belongs to the goddess-deities of the outer offerings — the goddess of incense, the goddess of flowers, the goddess of lamps, and the goddess of perfume: by that supreme auspiciousness, may there be auspiciousness here and now.

Whatever auspiciousness belongs to the Blessed Ones who guard the gates — the hook-bearer, the lasso-bearer, the chain-bearer, and the bell-bearer: by that supreme auspiciousness, may there be auspiciousness here and now.

Here ends the auspicious verses of the thirty-seven deities — the deities of the maṇḍala of the glorious Yoga Tantra, the five families of Tathāgatas together with their retinues.

Translator's notes

The four consort-deities named as "sems ma" in the first goddess stanza are the four female buddhas associated with the five-family maṇḍala system of the Yoga Tantras: Vajradhātvīśvarī (consort of Vairocana), Māmakī (consort of Akṣobhya), Pāṇḍaravāsinī (here rendered as Dharmadhātvīśvarī following the Dharma-family association), and Samayatārā (here as Karma consort). The Tibetan gives functional epithets rather than full Sanskrit names, so the Sanskrit renderings supplied are those standardly assigned to these positions in the thirty-seven-deity maṇḍala of the Sarvatathāgatatattvasaṃgraha tradition.

The four gate-guardian goddesses are rendered by function rather than by the conventional Sanskrit names (Aṅkuśā, Pāśā, Sphoṭā, Ghaṇṭā) since the Tibetan itself uses functional epithets; those Sanskrit names correspond exactly.
