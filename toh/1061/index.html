About this text

The Great Sūtra Called the Sūtra in Accord with Those Who Roam and Those Who Do Not Roam, known in Sanskrit as the Āṭānāṭīya Sūtra, is a canonical discourse set at Śrāvastī, in the Jeta Grove, the park of Anāthapiṇḍada. It opens as the Great King Vaiśravaṇa, guardian of the north, arrives with a vast retinue of yakṣas whose radiance lights up the whole grove, and comes before the Buddha. The text belongs to the tradition of protective scriptures, in which powerful spirits pledge to guard the Buddha's followers, and it offers safeguards against harmful beings for those who recite it. It is Toh 1061 in the Kangyur, where it is counted among the great sūtras, a class of texts valued for their protective power and closely associated with the collection of dhāraṇīs.

In the language of Tibet: The Great Sūtra Called the Sūtra in Accord with Those Who Roam and Those Who Do Not Roam.

Homage to the Three Jewels.

Thus did I hear at one time. The Blessed One was dwelling at Śrāvastī, in the Jeta Grove, the park of Anāthapiṇḍada. Then the Great King Vaiśravaṇa, of surpassing complexion, came to where the Blessed One was. With him came his retinue of many yakṣas, many hundreds of yakṣas, many thousands of yakṣas, and many hundreds of thousands of yakṣas. He bowed his head at the Blessed One's feet and sat down to one side. At that time, through the power of the Great King Vaiśravaṇa's radiance, the whole of the Jeta Grove was lit up with a great light. Seated to one side, the Great King Vaiśravaṇa then offered these verses:

Perfect Buddha, supreme among those with two feet,
O wise one, what you know
the gods do not know.
Great hero, I pay homage to you.
To all the buddhas who have passed,
who are present now, and who have not yet come,
I pay homage,
and in you too I take refuge.

"Venerable One, there are monks, nuns, laymen and laywomen, śrāvakas of the Blessed One, who dwell in lodgings in the wilderness, at the edges of forests, and in remote borderlands. There too dwell yakṣas and malevolent nonhumans who are native to those places. Some of them have faith in the Blessed One's teaching, and some have no faith in it. Those yakṣas and malevolent native nonhumans who have faith in the Blessed One's teaching are very few, while those yakṣas and malevolent native nonhumans who have no faith in the Blessed One's teaching are far more numerous. Venerable One, so that the Blessed One's śrāvakas, the monks, nuns, laymen and laywomen, may in this way guard and protect themselves and dwell in ease, and so that the yakṣas and malevolent native nonhumans who lack faith may come to have faith, and those who have faith may grow in it still further, it would be excellent, excellent, if this very Sūtra in Accord with Those Who Roam and Those Who Do Not Roam, this vidyā-mantra and protection, through which homage comes to be paid with hands and feet, were taken up in full and thoroughly mastered."

The Blessed One consented to the Great King Vaiśravaṇa by remaining silent. Translator's notes

The phrase rendered "through which homage comes to be paid with hands and feet" is uncertain. The Tibetan "sug pa rkang pa" is obscure, and "sug pa" has been taken as "hand." The Tibetan title's "those who roam and those who do not roam" reflects a Tibetan interpretation of the Sanskrit title Āṭānāṭīya. In the arrival scene the Tibetan has a stray "rnam thos pa dang" ("and with Vaiśravaṇa") after the list of the retinue; it is probably a scribal duplication and has not been translated. "Jeta Grove" renders the Tibetan "Prince Jeta's Grove." The lines "Pay homage…" render "phyag 'tshol cig," which may also be read as questions: "Do you pay homage…?" Compare the Pali "vandatha." The names of the cities, the beings living beyond Uśira and the assembly halls are translated by meaning. This includes Ever-Moving, Sheathed, Golden, Clothed, Body-Abode, Verandaed and Bull-Possessing, as well as the king called Hero. The Tibetan forms cannot be matched with confidence to particular Sanskrit names, though they correspond loosely to the city list of the Pali Āṭānāṭiya Sutta. "Ever-Moving" and "Not Ever-Moving" echo the Tibetan rendering of the sūtra's own title (Āṭānāṭīya), so they may represent Āṭānāṭa. "Hero" may stand for Sanskrit Vīra.

In the city list, the closing "bdag lags so" is read as "dag lags so" ("these are"). "Lotus-Filled," with whom Kubera sports, probably refers to his lotus pool (compare the Pali naḷinī). The phrase "na li ke ra" is taken as nālikera, coconut, which suggests cooking in coconut shells, but this is uncertain. "Chunlak" is an unidentified bird name, left in phonetic form. Many of the gandharva and kumbhāṇḍa names in the two lists cannot be confidently identified with Sanskrit originals. They are translated by meaning, for example "Intoxicated with Desire," "Joy-Maker," "Five-Holder," "Cuckoo-Voice," "Gold-Goer," "Golden One," "Great Flank," "Left Side," "Accomplisher of Existence," "Concealer of Deeds," and "Possessors of Fine Blankets." The division of some lines into separate names is also uncertain, for instance "Left Side, the Black One and Red One." The Tibetan "gser spyod bu mo gser can" could also mean "Golden One, the daughter of Gold-Goer." Pa ba sangs and Phur bu are the Tibetan names of the planets Venus and Jupiter, and are given as Śukra and Bṛhaspati. Byin and Nye byin are given as Datta and Upadatta. The phrase rendered "makes them bow down hand and foot" is literal, and its precise sense is uncertain. The royal palace "lcang lo can" is Alakāvatī, Vaiśravaṇa's city. Many of the nāga and yakṣa names could not be identified with confidence and are translated by meaning.
