About this text

The Hundred and Eight Names of Jambhala (Sanskrit: Āryajambhalanāmāṣṭaśataka; Tibetan: 'Phags pa gnod 'dzin gyi mtshan brgya rtsa brgyad pa) is a short litany of praise dedicated to Jambhala, the wealth-bestowing deity who is a yakṣa lord and a guardian of prosperity. The text opens with a yakṣa lord of the wilds, splendid with gold and jewels, entering the Grove of Willows, bowing to Vajradhara, the Lord of Secrets, and then questioning the Blessed One, Jambhala, who is hailed as sovereign among Victors. What follows is a sequence of honorific epithets, in the manner of a name-offering or stotra, celebrating his bodhisattva qualities, his power over secret mantras, and his work of taming the unruly for the benefit of beings. It appears as Toh 771 in the Kangyur, among the tantra texts, and the colophon indicates it was translated into Tibetan by the translators.

Translation

Translated (into Tibetan) by the translators.

In the language of India: Ārya-jambhala-nāma-aṣṭaśataka. In the language of Tibet: The Noble One's Hundred and Eight Names of Jambhala. Homage to the Noble Jambhala.

Then the yakṣa, the lord of the wilds, finely adorned with gold, radiant with jewels, blazing like fire, entered the Grove of Willows. There he bowed fully at the two feet of the Lord of Secrets, Vajradhara, and, in deep meditative equipoise, questioned the Blessed One, Jambhala, sovereign among Victors:

You are the lord of all the hosts of secret mantras, honored by all the holders of knowledge-mantras, to whom gods and asuras bow, who strive to tame the unruly. You possess the qualities of a bodhisattva, protector, working for the benefit of all beings. You hold the secret of all the buddhas.

Wishing to benefit beings, with a mind overwhelmed by compassion, I too have generated the awakening mind for the sake of perfect and complete awakening. Seeing people poor in wealth, slight in merit, and weak in power, I am tormented by great suffering, O Lord of Secrets.

Then Jambhala spoke, overwhelmed with compassion, to the Blessed One, the Lord of Secrets:

Listen well, Secret One. The protector Jambhala, endowed with glory, who works for the benefit of all beings and dispels the afflictions of poverty, the sovereign of the whole treasury of wealth: his hundred and eight names, rich in merit, remove the faults and misdeeds of poverty. They were declared long ago by the supreme Victors, as numerous as the sands of the Ganges. In the realm called Hundred Rays, when the buddha whose face is like the sun was teaching the Dharma, I too heard this myself in former times. It dispels poverty and is rich in merit. With my own wisdom, striving for the benefit of all beings, I shall teach it well to you. Listen with deep concentration.

He is noble, supreme among gods and asuras, wise, reverent toward the Dharma, with a compassionate nature, patient and steadfast, with calmed and subdued faculties, who by virtue destroys misdeeds, who is mindful and cautious. He is one who accomplishes all aims, harmless, undeceiving, glorious, lord of the treasury, unconquerable by others, with the Three Jewels as his sole refuge, seen by all the buddhas, guardian of treasures, majestic, who ever gives and increases wealth, source of jewels, who tames beings. He is lord of waters, bringer of rain, a yakṣa whose nature is to enjoy the waters, who reveals the hidden treasures, the golden mountain, Meru, who liberates from the afflictions of poverty. He is the powerful lord, protector of beings, the lord of waters, cloud-colored, the eye and the supreme guide of the world, steady like the sun and moon.

He is the great king of great wealth, holding a mongoose in his hand, a youth with a lion's crest, a great yakṣa of vast miraculous power, smiling like the lord of speech, sweet-voiced in his speech, the brahmin, the iron hook of desire, honored by the greatly accomplished. He holds knowledge-mantras, is a wheel-turner, rides Kubera and a man. He has a pot-belly and wide eyes, is fragrant with divine scent, swift as thought, the all-pervading lord, source of bliss, son of Nor-bzang, who delights in protecting beings. He is a mind-god who changes his form to fulfill every wish, wearing a crest, delighting in his lineage, on the left holding a crest-jewel and a staff, hundred-handed, of great strength, lord of the eight grounds, a god of the heavenly realm, with a face the color of the full moon, leader of caravans, lord of the assembly, an elder, a merchant of good fortune, a wish-fulfilling jewel, an excellent vase, a wishing tree, lord of men, the supreme one who is worthy of the highest aim.

Giver of the supreme, the swift pervader, worthy refuge, protector of auspiciousness. Adorned with garlands of jewels, he sits upon a divine lion throne. A bodhisattva, lord of splendor, flourishing, with inexhaustible wealth, possessed of the power and prowess of a lion, a teacher victorious over the three worlds. Chief of merchants, supreme among lineages, the lotus center, the invincible victor, an ocean vast in pure conduct, a great-natured subduer of harm: this recitation of one hundred and eight names, with its explanation, destroys all evil deeds and removes the afflictions of poverty. It gives rise to prosperity and good fortune, and increases elephants, horses, and wealth. It dispels evil planets and constellations, bad dreams, and evil omens.

Whether in a forest, in a village, in a charnel ground, or on a mountain, wherever a wise person recites it, battle and enemies, fear, snakes, fire, water, thieves, tigers and bears, the great dangers, and the hardships of crossing the ocean, all these grow utterly peaceful for that person.

Indra, the gods of fire and water, Yama, the lord of the rākṣasas, the god of wind, Vaiśravaṇa, the glorious Kubera, the jewel-like Maṇibhadra with his five sons, the Playful One, the lord of the pastures, and other gods and yakṣas as well, without negligence, day and night, protect with great joy. Wherever this name is recited, elephants, horses, cattle, and crops of every kind increase, along with fruits and flowers. Human illness, the illness of livestock, and all harms from any source turn everywhere to well-being. Above all, children are blessed with good fortune. Hungry ghosts, nāgas, the mothers, fierce spirits, ḍākinīs, and non-humans and rākṣasas will not harm that person. One is freed from every disease. In the same way one becomes happy and wealthy, and increases through children, grandchildren, splendor, and generosity. One is born into a noble lineage as a victor, born amid great enjoyments, endowed with long life and splendor, free of sickness and epidemic. In all lives one remembers one's births, grasps what is heard with swift insight, possesses the qualities of a bodhisattva, and becomes truly fortunate.

I pay homage, I pay homage to the lord of yakṣas. Lord of secrets, I pay homage to you, who strive to subdue the unruly. Holder of the vajra in your hand, I bow to you. You are pleased with beings who have faith. This renown of the son of Nor-bzang, which pacifies the sickness of poverty, has been well taught, excellently expounded. I too offer here the secret mantra that accomplishes all aims. By merely remembering it well, a person is freed from poverty, cannot be overcome by others, becomes protected, is not plundered by enemies, and becomes fortunate and endowed with good fortune. One will live even a hundred years. One will surely and swiftly gain a divine jewel elephant, victory, and the kingship for which one is fit, and there is no doubt about the kingship.

The mantra:

Namo ratnatrayāya. Namo maṇibhadrāya. Mahāyakṣasenāpataye. Oṃ hrīṃ hraṃ kṣā jrāṃ hrī hraṃ. Dīpya ratnoga pari śana

Translator's notes

The passage breaks off partway through the list of names. Several epithets in the list are obscure and are rendered tentatively, including "lord of the eight grounds," "son of Nor-bzang," and "rides Kubera and a man." The place name rendered as the Grove of Willows (ལྕང་ལོ་ཅན) is given by its literal meaning. The realm "Hundred Rays" is likewise literal. The opening line, "Translated by the translators," is the closing colophon of the preceding text and is retained only as it appears. The passage ends mid-mantra, so the final syllables are left as transliterated and unfinished. The Tibetan transliteration of the mantra has some probable corruption, so I have not forced the Sanskrit into more regular forms. In the opening, the epithet translated "swift pervader" is a tentative reading of "short-bodied one pervading." The "Playful One" and "lord of the pastures" are tentatively taken as epithets of yakṣa figures. The "mothers" refers to the mātṛkās, a class of spirits.
