About this text

The Means of Accomplishment of Glorious Hevajra as the Solitary Hero, in Sanskrit the Śrī Hevajra Ekavṛtti Vīra Sādhana and in Tibetan Dpal dgyes pa rdo rje dpa' bo gcig pa'i sgrub pa'i thabs, is Toh 1252 in the Tengyur, among the commentaries on the tantras. It is a concise prose sādhana, or manual of deity practice, attributed in its colophon to the great master Kṛṣṇa, which guides the practitioner through a full day of Hevajra yoga. Beginning at dawn with protection and the consecration of a ritual maṇḍala, it moves through offerings to the eight goddesses, the four immeasurables and meditation on emptiness, and then the visualization of oneself as the eight-faced, sixteen-armed Hevajra in union with Nairātmyā, followed by empowerment, mantra recitation and torma offerings. It closes with instructions for bringing the practice into meals, sexual yoga and sleep, and its colophon records that it was translated into Tibetan by the Indian preceptor Kṛṣṇa together with the Tibetan translator Gö Lhetsé.

Translation

In Sanskrit: śrī hevajra eka bṛtti bīra sādhanaṃ. In Tibetan: The Means of Accomplishment of Glorious Hevajra as the Solitary Hero.

Homage to the noble Avalokiteśvara, lord of great compassion.

I bow to Heruka, who draws forth the qualities
That utterly cast off this bondage of existence
Merely through tasting the flavor
Of becoming familiar with him.

At dawn, rising from one's bed, one visualizes at the heart a sun disc arising from raṃ, and with its light makes oneself free of stains. Reciting oṃ rakṣa rakṣa hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā three times, one protects oneself, the place and the yoga. Then one enters the meditation room, places one's hand on the maṇḍala there with oṃ vajra rekhe hūṃ, and consecrates it with oṃ vajra medini vajri bha va banbha hūṃ. Reciting oṃ āḥ hūṃ, one scatters flowers in the center. In the east one scatters flowers with gaṃ gaurī namaḥ; in the south, caṃ caurī namaḥ; in the west, baṃ bettāli namaḥ; in the north, ghaṃ ghasmarī namaḥ; in the northeast, the quarter of Īśāna, paṃ pukkasī nāmaḥ; in the southeast, the quarter of Agni the Fire-Eater, śaṃ śabarī nāmaḥ; in the southwest, the quarter of Nairṛti, laṃ caṇḍalī nāmaḥ; and in the northwest, the quarter of Vāyu, ḍaṃ ḍombinī nāmaḥ. With these one scatters flowers.

Then again one worships the Blessed One with oṃ deva picu vajra hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā, and worships the Blessed One with oṃ a ā i īṃ u ū ṛ ṝ ḷ lī e ai o au aṃ aḥ svāhā. Then one worships him also with the eight lines: oṃ aṣṭānanāya namaḥ; oṃ piṃgordhākeśavartmane namaḥ; oṃ caturbiṃśatinetrāya namaḥ; oṃ ṣoḍaśabhujāya namaḥ; oṃ kṛṣṇajīmūtavapuṣe namaḥ; oṃ kapālamālāne kadhariṇe namaḥ; oṃ adhmatakrūracittāya namaḥ; oṃ ardhendudaṃṣṭhaṇe namaḥ. Then again, having worshipped him perfectly with consecrated flowers and the rest, by means of oṃ vajra puṣpe ā hūṃ, oṃ vajra dhūṣpe āḥ hūṃ, oṃ vajra dīpe āḥ hūṃ, oṃ vajra gandhe ā hūṃ and oṃ vajra naiviti āḥ hūṃ, one recites three times the sevenfold practice that begins with going for refuge to the Three Jewels. Then one prostrates, offers the torma, and requests him to depart with oṃ vajra muḥ.

Then, after briefly cultivating the four immeasurables, loving-kindness and the rest, one takes all phenomena as the object of the intellect, analyzes them one by one, and meditates that they are without intrinsic nature and are luminosity. One consecrates this with oṃ śūnyatā jñāna vajra svabhāva ātmako'haṃ. At the end of that, one visualizes a and hūṃ on a sun disc arisen from raṃ. With their light one establishes in awakening the sentient beings who dwell in the world systems out to the furthest limits of infinity; then, merging them and drawing them back, one absorbs them into the two seeds, and in a single instant one meditates on oneself as the Blessed One together with the Blessed Lady.

He has eight faces and sixteen arms. With his four legs he dances upon the four māras, in a posture with legs half crossed. He wears the five mudrās and a garland of human heads. The color of his body blazes like the fire at the end of the eon; he is wrathful, and his tawny hair bristles upward. He has twenty-four eyes and sixteen arms. In the eight skull cups of his right hands are an elephant, a horse, a donkey, an ox, a camel, a lion, a human and a cat. In the eight skull cups of his left hands are Earth, Water, Fire, Wind, Moon, Sun, Yama and Dhanada (Kubera). He bears a crossed vajra on his crown and steals away the suffering of saṃsāra. One meditates on him embraced by the goddess Nairātmyā, who holds a curved knife and a skull cup.

After that, one visualizes hūṃ on a sun at one's own heart, and aṃ on a moon at the heart of Nairātmyā. One also sets oṃ at the head, āḥ at the throat and hūṃ at the heart, and places Mohavajrī on the eyes, Dveṣavajrī on the ears, Mātsaryavajrī on the nose, Rāgavajrī on the mouth, Īrṣyāvajrī on the body, and Nairātmyayoginī on the mind. With the rays of light from the seed at the heart one invites the buddhas, and requests empowerment with abhiṣiñcatu māṃ sarva tathāgata. One then meditates that the buddhas rain down flowers and bathe one, and that the eight goddesses praise and make offerings.

When one tires of meditation, one should recite the mantra: oṃ deva picu vajra hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā. On rising, one gathers the goddesses into the seed at one's own heart and should go about in the form of the deity.

At the time of meditation, one should offer the torma at the beginning and at the end. For this, one meditates on a fire maṇḍala arisen from raṃ above a wind maṇḍala arisen from yaṃ. There, inside a skull cup arisen from a, one should consecrate with oṃ the substances arisen from the seeds go ku da ha na and bi mu ma ra śu. Above it one meditates on a vajra arisen from hūṃ, and meditates on it as a blazing fire in the form of the union of vajra and lotus. With its light one beholds all the Tathāgatas, draws nectar from the seeds at the hearts of all the Tathāgatas, and absorbs it there. Consecrating it with oṃ āḥ hūṃ: this is the rite for purifying the torma, food and the like.

Then, in a single instant, one imagines the maṇḍala of the Blessed One at the center of an eight-petaled lotus contained within a source of phenomena that comes from the sky, and visualizes the Solitary Hero. On the petals one meditates on the guardians of the directions, blue in color, holding vajra and bell, embraced by their respective consorts, seated with the right leg extended, their heads adorned with Akṣobhya. In the same way, above that one imagines the gods, Brahmā and the rest, white in color, with Vairocana as their crown ornament. Below, one meditates on the beings who move beneath the earth, red in color, with Amitābha as their crown ornament. Imagining a white hūṃ on the tongues of all of them, one should satisfy them by having the goddesses Gaurī and the rest, arisen from the seed at one's own heart, offer them nectar. The mantra is this: oṃ inda ja ma ja la ja kṣa bhū ta ba hni ba u ra kkha canda sujja mā da pa ppa ta lā pā ta la aṣṭa sappa svāhā i daṃ ba liṃ bhūṃ ja jīṃ gha phu lla dhu pa māṃ sa piṃ gha a mbha sappa kajja satva kha nti khu ṇi phe ṭa gā da oṃ a kā ro mu khaṃ sarva dha rmā ṇāṃ ā dya nu tpa nna tvā ta oṃ āḥ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā. One recites this three times and offers water for rinsing the mouth.

The mantra is oṃ hrīḥ biśuddhanaya sarva bikalbānaya hūṃ. Then one should worship with offerings produced by the mind, and should request departure with oṃ vajra muḥs.

At mealtime, having purified the food as before, one imagines a white vajra on one's own tongue. Having absorbed all the Tathāgatas into one's heart, in the manner of the Blessed One, one should kindle the fire with the seed of the wisdom being.

At the time of engaging in passion, one meditates on the wisdom consort as Nairātmyā, and meditates on oṃ at her head, āḥ at her throat, hūṃ at her heart, and a on the anthers of the lotus. One consecrates the lotus with this: oṃ padma sukhadhara mahāraga sukhaṃdatda caturānanda bhaga biśva hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ karyaṃ kuruṣva me. Having generated the vajra from hūṃ and consecrated the jewel with oṃ, one consecrates it with oṃ vajra mahādveṣa caturānanda dayaka khagamukha ekaraso nātha hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ karyaṃ kuruṣva me. One should begin the joy with siṃ siṃ siṃ and ha ha ha. Having identified its moments through the guru's instructions, one draws up its nectar and should satisfy oneself.

At the time of sleeping, one should think of all phenomena as having the nature of the innate, and then sleep.

The Means of Accomplishment of Glorious Hevajra called the Solitary Hero, composed by the great master Kṛṣṇa, is complete. It was translated, revised and finalized by the Indian preceptor Kṛṣṇa and the Tibetan translator Gö Lhetsé.

Translator's notes

The buddhas "rain down flowers and bathe one" reads bkug ("draw in, summon") as the similar bkrus ("bathe"), which fits the empowerment; as written it would mean that they rain down flowers and draw one in. The seed syllables go ku da ha na and bi mu ma ra śu stand for the five meats and the five nectars of the inner offering.
