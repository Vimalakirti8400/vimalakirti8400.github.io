About this text

Toh 53 in the Kangyur is a Mahāyāna sūtra known in English as "The Noble Ten Dharmas," in Sanskrit as the Ārya-daśadharma-nāma-mahāyānasūtra, and in Tibetan as Phags pa chos bcu pa. It forms the ninth chapter of the vast collection known as the Great Ratnakūṭa, a compendium of one hundred thousand chapters organized around the theme of the "Heap of Jewels," and it belongs to the Ratnakūṭa section of the Kangyur. Set on Vulture Peak at Rājagṛha, the text opens with the Buddha surrounded by a great assembly of monks and bodhisattvas, and centers on a bodhisattva named Pure Virtue, Stainless and Immaculate, King of Jewel Moonlight, suggesting that its teaching will concern a set of ten dharmas or spiritual qualities of particular importance for the bodhisattva path.

Translation

From the noble great Mahāratnakūṭa Dharma-door of a Hundred Thousand Chapters, the Ninth Chapter: the sūtra called "The Noble Ten Dharmas," First Fascicle.

In the language of India: Ārya-daśadharma-nāma-mahāyānasūtra.
In the Tibetan language: the sūtra of the Great Vehicle called "The Noble Ten Dharmas."

Homage to all buddhas and bodhisattvas.

Thus did I hear. At one time the Blessed One was dwelling on Vulture Peak Mountain at Rājagṛha, together with a great Saṅgha of monks numbering around five hundred, and with a very great many bodhisattvas, great beings. At that time the bodhisattva, great being named Pure Virtue, Stainless and Immaculate, King of Jewel Moonlight had gathered in that very assembly and was seated there.

Then the bodhisattva, great being Pure Virtue, Stainless and Immaculate, King of Jewel Moonlight rose from his seat, arranged his outer robe over one shoulder, placed his right knee on the ground at the heart of a lotus, and with palms pressed together bowed in the direction of the Blessed One. He then addressed the Blessed One with these words: "If the Blessed One were to grant me the opportunity to ask a question and receive a prophetic declaration in reply, I would venture to put to the Blessed One, the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly and fully awakened buddha, a matter or two from my own side."

When he had spoken thus, the Blessed One addressed the bodhisattva, great being Pure Virtue, Stainless and Immaculate, King of Jewel Moonlight with these words: "Son of good family, the Tathāgata always grants you the opportunity. Ask whatever you wish, and I shall satisfy your mind by prophetically declaring the very answers to whatever you ask."

When the Blessed One had spoken thus, the bodhisattva, great being Pure Virtue, Stainless and Immaculate, King of Jewel Moonlight addressed the Blessed One as follows: "Blessed One, as to one who is said to be 'a monk who has genuinely entered the Great Vehicle' — 'a monk who has genuinely entered the Great Vehicle' — Blessed One, by what measure is a monk one who has genuinely entered the Great Vehicle? How does the Tathāgata designate someone as having genuinely entered the Great Vehicle? And for what reason is this vehicle called 'the Great Vehicle'?"

When he had spoken thus, the Blessed One gave his approval — "Excellent!" — to the bodhisattva, great being Pure Virtue, Stainless and Immaculate, King of Jewel Moonlight, and said: "Son of good family, your intense eagerness is good. Your thorough inquiry is also good. Your confidence in asking in order to receive a prophetic declaration is also good. Son of good family, you are one who has undertaken this for the benefit of the many, for the happiness of the many, out of compassion for the world, for the welfare and happiness of gods and humans. It is excellent, excellent, that you intend to fully inquire of the Tathāgata about such a matter. Son of good family, therefore listen well and hold it in mind — I shall explain."

The bodhisattva, great being Pure Virtue, Stainless and Immaculate, King of Jewel Moonlight said "Excellent!" to the Blessed One and listened attentively. The Blessed One then spoke these words:

"Son of good family, if a bodhisattva is endowed with ten qualities, he has genuinely entered the Great Vehicle. What are the ten? They are as follows: he possesses faith; he applies himself earnestly at the core; he is of excellent lineage; he desires awakening mind (bodhicitta); he desires the Dharma; he engages in reflection close to the Dharma; he has entered into conduct harmonious with the Dharma; he is free from pride and..."

...free from arrogance and conceit; and he is skilled in understanding the intended meaning of what has been taught. He does not wish to pass beyond sorrow through the vehicle of the śrāvakas or the pratyekabuddhas.

Then King of Jewel Moonlight asked: "Blessed One, in what way does a bodhisattva possess faith?"

The Blessed One declared: "Son of good family, a great bodhisattva is one who is by nature without wavering and without deceit, who is straightforward and whose very nature is uprightness. Such a one has confident trust in the awakening of the Tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly and completely awakened buddhas. He has confident trust in the wisdom of the Tathāgatas, which in a single instant encompasses the three times. He has confident trust in the buddha-nature (tathāgatagarbha), which is without aging, without death, without exhaustion, without cessation, and without annihilation. He has confident trust in the ultimate limit of reality, in the dharmadhātu, in the knowledge of all things, in omniscience, in the powers, in the fearlessnesses, and in the unshared qualities of a buddha. He has confident trust in the fact that the crown protrusion atop the heads of the Tathāgatas cannot be seen even by looking. He has confident trust in the Tathāgatas being adorned with the thirty-two major marks of a great being, endowed with the eighty excellent minor marks, and encircled by a fathom-wide halo of light. He has confident trust in what has been well expounded, whether spoken by a śrāvaka, spoken by a pratyekabuddha, spoken by a bodhisattva, or spoken by the great sages beyond these. He has confident trust in this world and in the world beyond. He has confident trust in those who are śramaṇas and brahmins who have gone rightly forth and entered rightly upon the path. He has confident trust in the fruit that is the ripening of virtuous karma, desirable and pleasing and delightful, namely becoming a lord among gods within the ranks of the joyful and agreeable deities, or becoming a lord among humans within the ranks of humankind. And he has confident trust that the fruit of the ripening of non-virtuous karma — to be born among hell-beings, animals, or hungry ghosts — is unwanted, unpleasant, joyless, disagreeable, unbearable, fierce, rough, and scorching.

Through this confident trust in all these things, he abandons three qualities. What are the three? They are: hypocrisy, duplicity, and doubt. Son of good family, this is called a bodhisattva who follows faith. Son of good family, this is how a bodhisattva possesses faith."

Thereupon, at that time, the Blessed One spoke these verses:

Faith is the supreme vehicle —
by it the guides have surely gone forth.
Therefore a person of intelligence
should take up the following of faith.

In all worlds faith is supreme;
one possessed of faith suffers no decline.
Thus should a person of wisdom
take up the following of faith.

In one without faith
the white wholesome qualities do not arise —
just as a green sprout does not grow
from a seed scorched by fire.

"Son of good family, in what way does a bodhisattva apply himself earnestly at the core? Son of good family, here a bodhisattva, having shaved head and beard, dressed in saffron-colored robes, goes forth from home into homelessness in the excellent way, with faith. Having gone forth, he trains in the trainings, the procedures, the conduct, and the comportment of the śrāvakas; and he also trains in the trainings, the procedures, the conduct, and the comportment of the pratyekabuddhas. Through training in this way, his body and..."

...speech, and mind — all the unwholesome habitual bases belonging to body, speech, and mind are abandoned in every way, in every aspect, and in every respect.

What then are the unwholesome habitual bases belonging to the body? They are as follows: taking life, taking what is not given, sexual misconduct, harming others by means of clubs, weapons, clods of earth, or one's own hands; flailing the arms, flailing the legs, running, making sudden dashes, leaping, walking in haste, and swimming. These are called the unwholesome habitual bases belonging to the body.

What are the unwholesome habitual bases belonging to speech? They are as follows: speaking falsely, divisive speech, harsh speech, idle chatter, speaking so as to slander others, abandoning the profound sūtras, and speaking disagreeably to those who are like preceptors, teachers, and masters, as well as to those who live the holy celibate life. These are called the unwholesome habitual bases belonging to speech.

What are the unwholesome habitual bases belonging to mind? They are as follows: covetousness, malice, and wrong views; envy and miserliness; craving for gain, craving for fame, and craving for the profit of knowledge; pride in one's lineage, pride in one's form, pride in one's youth, pride in one's health, pride in one's vitality, pride in one's learning, and pride in one's meditative attainments; thoughts of desire, thoughts of malice, thoughts of harm, thoughts of relatives, thoughts of one's homeland; thoughts about monastic robes, alms-food, bedding, seats, medicines for the sick, and requisites; excessive attachment to one's dwelling, excessive attachment to one's conveyance, excessive attachment to one's bedding, excessive attachment to drink, excessive attachment to food, excessive attachment to one's sense of greatness; excessive attachment to wife, sons, daughters, trade and farming, male and female servants, laborers, those who live on sour milk, friends and companions, counselors, relatives, and those connected through blood ties; excessive attachment to wealth, grain, storehouses, and granaries; and excessive attachment to all manner of livelihood and its various provisions.

Through such excessive attachment as this, whenever any of the things enumerated is lost or destroyed, grief arises, and lamentation arises, and suffering arises, and mental distress arises, and agitation arises. In this way, the mind being thoroughly drenched in the moisture of craving, one comes to take rebirth in continued existence. Son of good family, in brief, propelled by the mechanism of mental karma, one wanders thoroughly on the wheel of saṃsāra. This is called the unwholesome habitual basis belonging to mind.

Now, being free from these unwholesome habitual bases of body, speech, and mind, the practitioner gives rise to the perception of a preceptor toward the instructor, gives rise to the perception of a preceptor toward the teacher, and becomes one who acts with affection and reverence toward those senior, middling, and junior in the celibate life. Then, dwelling in solitude alone, he thinks to himself: "Since I am striving to liberate all sentient beings, striving to free all sentient beings, and striving to establish all sentient beings in taming and peace, yet I myself remain untamed, unpeaceful, unguarded, and undisciplined — that is not my part at all. How then could I, merely by my being seen, cause sentient beings to become tamed?

"I shall act in accordance with the words of the Tathāgatas, and shall conduct myself so that gods, nāgas, yakṣas, gandharvas, and the like are gladdened. Thus shall I act, thus shall I accomplish, thus shall I exert diligence." Thinking in this way, that son of good family is a bodhisattva who knows shame.

He also thinks to himself: "I shall not associate in conversation with anyone around me — whether laypersons or renunciants — who habitually practices with impaired moral discipline, who habitually practices with impaired view, who habitually practices with impaired livelihood, who habitually practices with impaired ritual conduct, who habitually practices with impaired deportment, or who habitually practices any form of impairment whatsoever." Thinking thus, being endowed with such a sense of shame, three times by day and three times by night he examines himself thoroughly with respect to moral discipline. And by the complete purity of his moral discipline, being free from remorse, free from obscuration, and without interruption in the continuity of the path, he draws close to and abides within the Tathāgata's Dharma of the Vinaya. That son of good family is called a bodhisattva who possesses a sense of shame. Sons of good family, in this way a bodhisattva makes diligence his very essence.

Then at that moment the Blessed One declared these verses:

The perfectly awakened buddhas and the buddhas'
śrāvakas make diligence their very essence.
Therefore, in order to attain diligence,
let the one endowed with wisdom strive always.

The bodhisattva of great wisdom, fearless in diligence,
who relies upon what all the buddhas have relied upon,
will come to realize an awakening utterly free of dust.

Sons of good family, in what way is a bodhisattva one of excellent spiritual lineage? Sons of good family, in this regard a bodhisattva is by nature one in whom attachment, aversion, and delusion are slight; one who is free of envy, free of miserliness, free of deceitfulness, and free of guile. He is not coarse, not excitable, not agitated, and not proud. He is not harsh and does not speak harshly. He is straightforward and gentle. He is agreeable and pleasant to be with. By nature he is one who is inclined toward great works of offering and service, who has wealth and the requisites of life, and who is disposed to sharing them generously with others. His hands, feet, eyes, and the excellent limbs of his body are exceedingly attractive and radiant. When he sees a Tathāgata or the śrāvakas of a Tathāgata, at the very moment of seeing he gives rise to faith and clarity, and through that faith and clarity he attains excellence of spiritual lineage. Sons of good family, in this way a bodhisattva is one of excellent spiritual lineage.

Then at that moment the Blessed One declared these verses:

Just as fire is known from smoke,
and water is known from a water bird,
so the lineage of an intelligent bodhisattva
is known from its marks.

Not coarse, not excitable,
having fully abandoned deceit and guile,
completely pure of heart toward all sentient beings —
such a one is a bodhisattva.

Sons of good family, in what way is a bodhisattva one who desires the awakening mind? Sons of good family, in this regard, when a bodhisattva dwells in his lineage and gives rise to the mind of awakening, if a Tathāgata or a śrāvaka of a Tathāgata properly urges him, properly generates renunciation in him, properly brings him to take it up, and causes him to generate the mind aimed at unsurpassed, perfect, and complete awakening — that is the first cause for desiring the awakening mind.

Furthermore, if upon hearing praises spoken of awakening and of the awakening mind, one generates the mind aimed at unsurpassed, perfect, and complete awakening — that is the second cause for desiring the awakening mind.

Furthermore, if upon seeing sentient beings who have no protector, no refuge, no dwelling place, no ally, and no island of safety, one establishes a mind of compassion, thinking, "May I myself become the protector, the refuge, the dwelling place, the ally, and the island of safety for these beings," and by that cause, that condition, and that basis generates the mind aimed at unsurpassed, perfect, and complete awakening — that is the third cause for desiring the awakening mind.

Furthermore, if upon seeing the body of a Tathāgata perfectly complete in all its aspects one gives rise to joy, gives rise to supreme joy, gives rise to great mental pliancy, and gives rise to mental enthusiasm, and by that cause, that condition, and that basis generates the mind aimed at unsurpassed, perfect, and complete awakening — that is the fourth cause for desiring the awakening mind.

Furthermore, for the sole benefit and happiness of sentient beings, one practises giving, guards moral discipline, cultivates patience, undertakes diligence, enters into meditative concentration, and cultivates wisdom. Now, how does one practise giving? To those who desire food, one gives food; to those who desire drink, one gives drink; to those who desire conveyances, one gives conveyances; to those who desire bedding, one gives bedding; to those who desire clothing, one gives clothing; and to those who desire ornaments, one gives bracelets, armlets, earrings, ear-pendants, and adornments. Sons of good family, in brief, one even benefits sentient beings with one's own flesh. That is how one practises giving. And that giving one fully dedicates to unsurpassed, perfect, and complete awakening, and through that giving one does not give rise to conceit.

Now, how does one guard moral discipline? One keeps body well-restrained, keeps speech well-restrained, and keeps mind well-restrained; one completely and in every way abandons all misconduct of body in every respect, completely and in every way abandons all misconduct of speech and all misconduct of mind in every respect; and one guards the discipline so that it is unimpaired, unbroken, unmixed, and unsullied. That is how one guards moral discipline. And that moral discipline one fully dedicates to unsurpassed, perfect, and complete awakening, and through that moral discipline one does not give rise to conceit.

Now, how does one cultivate patience? Whether abused, scolded, struck, smeared with filth, bound, thrown into a pit, expelled, or tormented by householders or by those who have gone forth, one endures it and accepts it gladly. That is how one cultivates patience. And that patience one fully dedicates to unsurpassed, perfect, and complete awakening, and through that patience one does not give rise to conceit.

Now, how does one undertake diligence? One thinks to oneself as follows: just as the expanse of space is boundless and without limit, so too the expanse of sentient beings is boundless and without limit — and I alone, non-dually, shall establish them all in the realm of nirvāṇa without remainder.

With that cause, that condition, and that basis, one undertakes diligence, and one attends closely to bodily feelings through mindfulness that remains present with the body. While diligently attending closely to those feelings, one also applies diligence to observing the mind by following the mind, and to observing phenomena by following phenomena. Thus, with mindfulness firmly and closely established in this way, one generates the aspiration, makes the effort, undertakes diligence, holds the mind firmly, and places it rightly — for the sake of not giving rise to wicked and unwholesome phenomena that have not yet arisen. One generates the aspiration, makes the effort, undertakes diligence, holds the mind firmly, and places it rightly — for the sake of abandoning wicked and unwholesome phenomena that have already arisen. One generates the aspiration, makes the effort, undertakes diligence, holds the mind firmly, and places it rightly — for the sake of giving rise to virtuous phenomena that have not yet arisen. One generates the aspiration, makes the effort, undertakes diligence, holds the mind firmly, and places it rightly — for the sake of sustaining, expanding, not allowing the decline of, not forgetting, and bringing to full completion those virtuous phenomena that have already arisen.

Having thus undertaken diligence in this way, one cultivates the first base of miraculous power (Skt. ṛddhipāda). Likewise one cultivates the second, the third, and the fourth base of miraculous power. Having completely accomplished the four bases of miraculous power in this way, one remains for an eon or longer, and for an eon or longer one brings the perfection of diligence to complete fulfillment. That is how one undertakes diligence. And that diligence one fully dedicates to unsurpassed, perfect, and complete awakening, and through that diligence one does not give rise to conceit.

Now, how does one enter into meditative concentration? By not abiding in the state of freedom from attachment, not abiding in cessation, not dedicating entirely to renunciation, not abiding within, not abiding without, not abiding in form, not abiding in feeling, not abiding in perception, not abiding in formations, not abiding in consciousness, not abiding in the desire realm, not abiding in the form realm, not abiding in the formless realm, not abiding in emptiness, not abiding in the signless, not abiding in the wishless, not abiding in this world, not abiding in the world beyond, not abiding in form, not abiding in sound, not abiding in smell, not abiding in taste, not abiding in touch, not abiding in phenomena, not abiding in generosity, not abiding in moral discipline, not abiding in patience, not abiding in diligence, not abiding in meditative concentration, and not abiding in wisdom. In short, son of good family, having abandoned all attachment, one meditates without abiding anywhere at all. That is how one practices meditative concentration. And that meditative concentration one fully dedicates to unsurpassed, perfect, and complete awakening, and through that meditative concentration one does not give rise to conceit.

Now, son of good family, how does one cultivate wisdom? One thinks to oneself as follows: even though I lead the boundless expanse of sentient beings to nirvāṇa, not a single sentient being has actually been led to nirvāṇa. And why is that? Because the Blessed One has taught that all phenomena are without self, that all phenomena are without a sentient being —

because the Blessed One has taught that all phenomena are without a person. That is how one cultivates wisdom. And that wisdom one fully dedicates to unsurpassed, perfect, and complete awakening, and through that wisdom one does not give rise to conceit.

Son of good family, that is how a bodhisattva aspires to the awakening mind. Then, at that moment, the Blessed One spoke these verses:

A precious jewel, by its very nature,
blazes with light from within its source;
yet when it is polished and refined,
it shines more beautifully still with twofold splendor.

Just so, one who possesses the noble lineage
seeks out the awakening mind,
and in the domain where both extremes are fully purified,
Māra shall not come.

Son of good family, how then does a bodhisattva aspire to the Dharma? Son of good family, here the bodhisattva by nature seeks out the meaning of the Dharma, desires the Dharma, rejoices in the Dharma, and strives in pursuit of delight in the Dharma. When such a one sees a monk or a brahmin or anyone else afflicted by hunger and in want of food, one gives them whatever one has — whether cooked food, raw grains, or any provisions at hand. And the necessities of life and sustenance — roots, fruits, grains, and whatever else there may be — one gathers and gathers again, and gives to sentient beings.

Then one goes to those who hold the sacred Dharma and, with reverence, speaks respectfully to them; one bows down, one rises to greet them, one joins one's palms together, and having performed all acts of respectful conduct, one then asks questions in the proper manner, with full deference and respect. And in response, that holder of the sacred Dharma answers each question just as it was asked. One regards that holder of the sacred Dharma as the Teacher, as a spiritual friend, as one who frees beings from the ocean of saṃsāra, as a preceptor, as an instructor. One thinks: "This one seeks me out — I who for so long have wandered in the wilderness of saṃsāra," and cultivates just such a perception. One thinks: "This one awakens me — I who for so long have been veiled by delusion and sunk in sleep," and cultivates just such a perception. One thinks: "This one draws me out from the mire of the ocean of saṃsāra," and cultivates just such a perception. One thinks: "I have entered a bad path, and this one shows me the good path," and cultivates just such a perception. One thinks: "I am bound in the prison of saṃsāra, and this one releases me," and cultivates just such a perception. One thinks: "For so long I have been sick and this one is a supreme physician," and cultivates just such a perception. One thinks: "I am consumed by the fire of attachment, the fire of aversion, and the fire of delusion — and this one extinguishes it all; this one is a great rain-cloud," and cultivates just such a perception.

And for the sake of the Dharma, for the ground of the Dharma, for the purpose of the Dharma, one disregards heat, and cold, and wind, and sun, and gnats, and mosquitoes, and the touch of serpents; one disregards hunger and thirst. And when one sees others enjoying pleasures, one does not give rise to a mind of longing. Instead one thinks to oneself:

"The appearance of hearing the sacred Dharma will arise for me, and therefore I am one who is happy in this world." With this thought in mind, one does not grieve over that cause, that condition, and that ground; one does not lament or give rise to a mind of suffering and discontent. And free from grief, lamentation, suffering, and discontent, one gives rise to a thought such as this: "For the sake of even a single word of the Dharma spoken by the Tathāgata, I would gladly dwell in the great hell (naraka) of Avīci for a kalpa — or even longer than a kalpa." Such a thought arises, and because one's mind is utterly without weariness, one accomplishes omniscience itself — the knowledge of all aspects — without need of forcing, and one also comes to obtain all the qualities of a buddha not yet obtained. Therefore, son of good family, this is how a bodhisattva desires the Dharma.

Thereupon the Blessed One uttered these verses:

The sons of the Victor, endowed with great wisdom,
who have come to desire the Dharma —
when they seek out the sacred Dharma in full,
they do not give rise to weariness.

Those born from the heart of the Victor,
who seek the sacred Dharma out of reverence —
they hold it with their minds
and accomplish it with earnest application.

Son of good family, how then does a bodhisattva contemplate the Dharma in a thorough and close manner? Son of good family, a bodhisattva contemplates in the following way.

All phenomena are like an illusion, because they utterly bewilder childish ordinary beings.
All phenomena are like a dream, because they do not truly exist.
All phenomena are like the reflection of the moon in water, because they are without substantiality.
All phenomena are like a visual aberration, because they are devoid of sentient beings.
All phenomena are like an echo, because they are entirely imputed and not real.
All phenomena are like a resounding echo from a cliff, because they arise and then disintegrate and are consumed.
All phenomena arise through dependent origination, and therefore they arise and disintegrate and are consumed.
All phenomena are unborn, because they resolve perfectly into suchness.
All phenomena are unceased, because they are unborn.
All phenomena are not conditioned things, because they have no agent.
All phenomena are like space, because they are unstained.
All phenomena are at peace, because they are not intrinsically and utterly aflame.
All phenomena are primordially and utterly pure, because they are not intrinsically and altogether afflicted.
All phenomena are without stain, because they are free from all defilements.
All phenomena are nirvāṇa from the very beginning, because they are free from affliction.
All phenomena are without form, because they do not appear with certainty.
All phenomena truly transcend the domain of consciousness, because they are without body.
All phenomena are without abiding, because their very locus has been thoroughly demolished.
All phenomena are not objects of consciousness, because attachment and anger have been abandoned.
All phenomena are without location, because they truly transcend the sphere of the afflictions' activity.
All phenomena are like a venomous serpent, because they are devoid of the knowledge of skillful means.
All phenomena are like the trunk of a plantain tree, because they are without essence.
All phenomena are like a bubble on water, because they are by nature feeble.

Thus does one contemplate in this thorough and close manner. Son of good family, this is how a bodhisattva contemplates the Dharma.

Then at that time the Blessed One spoke these verses:

All phenomena are like illusions,
confounding the minds of the childish.
They are not real, but like a dream.
Look upon them in this way, O monks.

Phenomena are like the reflection of the moon in water,
stirred up entirely by a shimmer of the eye.
Phenomena are like a mirror's image —
what wise person would take them as real?

Son of good family, how, then, does a bodhisattva enter into the Dharma that accords with the Dharma? Son of good family, here a bodhisattva, correctly viewing form as impermanent, and because of being free from attachment to form does not directly realize the dharmadhātu, yet through genuine wisdom thoroughly understands all the phenomena within the sphere of the dharmadhātu, and thoroughly apprehends their characteristic marks, their well-spoken definitions, and their well-applied designations. Having thoroughly apprehended those characteristic marks, well-spoken definitions, and well-applied designations, he enters, and enters only, into the effortless path of that dharmadhātu. In the same way, correctly viewing feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness as impermanent, and because of being free from attachment to consciousness does not directly realize the dharmadhātu, yet through genuine wisdom thoroughly understands all the phenomena within the sphere of the dharmadhātu, and thoroughly apprehends their characteristic marks, their well-spoken definitions, and their well-applied designations. Having thoroughly apprehended those characteristic marks, well-spoken definitions, and well-applied designations, he enters, and enters only, into the effortless path of that dharmadhātu.

Just as with impermanence, so too with suffering, emptiness, and selflessness — he proceeds in exactly the same manner. Correctly viewing form as selfless, and correctly so viewing it, he is neither frightened nor terrified by form, nor does he become afraid. Why? Because form is aroused by the thorough conceptualization of what is not real, and he knows it exactly as it truly is. Likewise, correctly viewing feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness as selfless, and correctly viewing consciousness as selfless, he is neither frightened nor terrified by consciousness, nor does he become afraid. Why? Because consciousness is aroused by the thorough conceptualization of what is not real, and he knows it exactly as it truly is.

Son of good family, consider this example.
