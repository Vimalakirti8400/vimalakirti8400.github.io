About this text

Toh 636 in the Kangyur is a dhāraṇī text known in Sanskrit as the Ārya Kha Garbha Aṣṭottaraśataka Nāma Dhāraṇī Mantra Sahita, and in Tibetan as the One Hundred and Eight Names of the Noble Ākāśagarbha Together with Dhāraṇī and Mantra — Ākāśagarbha meaning "Essence of Space," one of the great bodhisattvas of the Mahāyāna tradition. The text consists of one hundred and eight names of this bodhisattva, accompanied by dhāraṇī and mantra, and is dedicated to the purpose of dispelling the suffering of beings and protecting them from evil destinies and lower rebirths. It belongs to the dhāraṇī class of canonical scriptures, a category of texts in which sacred names, syllables, and incantations are held to carry powerful spiritual efficacy when recited. Notably, the colophon as preserved associates the text with Maitreya rather than Ākāśagarbha, suggesting some ambiguity in transmission, though the body of the text is consistently devoted to Ākāśagarbha and opens with homage to that bodhisattva.

The one hundred and eight names of the noble Maitreya (Skt. Maitrī), together with dhāraṇī and mantra, for the purpose of dispelling the suffering of beings, are here completely set forth and concluded.

In the Indian language: Ārya Kha Garbha Aṣṭottaraśataka Nāma Dhāraṇī Mantra Sahita. In the Tibetan language: The One Hundred and Eight Names of the Noble Ākāśagarbha (the Essence of Space), together with Dhāraṇī and Mantra.

Homage to the noble Ākāśagarbha, whose face is broad and vast, whose eyes radiate light, and who makes the midst of space fragrant and sweet.

Homage to the one whose domain is space, who illuminates all the maṇḍalas of the earth.

Oṃ svastika mālikṣi vipula saṃbhava dharmādhātu gocara svāhā.

The moment this dhāraṇī is recited, all evil destinies and all lower rebirths are completely pacified. The earth shakes. The sound of great drums resounds. And all buddhas bestow their "Excellent!" upon it.

Then the Blessed One Vairocana declared: "O sentient beings gathered in the surrounding maṇḍala, listen! This, which is the supreme dhāraṇī of the svāhā of the manifest maṇḍala of the very essence of Ākāśagarbha, is exceedingly difficult to encounter. It is of great merit. It burns away all sins and mental afflictions. It is the mother of all bodhisattvas. It has become a mass of jewels. Hear it now, and upon hearing it one will become stainless, as though perfectly bathed and thoroughly purified.

One will become like a lotus: unstained by the mud of saṃsāra. One will become like the sun: illuminating all sentient beings. One will become like the moon: bringing deep satisfaction to all sentient beings. One will become like the sky: untouched by all the sufferings of the lower destinies. One will become like the earth: without high or low. One will become like Mount Meru: impossible for any sentient being to move. One will become like liberation: unstained by pleasure and pain.

And thus one will come to attain countless immeasurable qualities numbering in the hundreds of thousands, and so on. By merely hearing, holding, reading, reciting, teaching, and even just chanting this, whatever one may wish — all one's desires, aspirations, and hopes will be fulfilled.

Moreover, by merely chanting it, one will be fully protected from excessive rain, from drought, from disturbances caused by enemies, from human disease, from bad dreams, from harm, from times of ruin, from death, from thieves, from desolation, from warfare, from wild beasts, and from the suffering of famine. And all happiness, abundance, whatever one desires and whatever one delights in will come to be accomplished."

Then all those members of the assembly cried out "Ha, ha!" with great rejoicing and jubilation, exclaiming "Excellent!" They stirred, waving their robes and various ornaments, and from their hearts they bowed in homage. The phrase rendered "who makes the midst of space fragrant and sweet" reads literally "who makes the interior of space fragrant"; this is a characteristic epithet of Ākāśagarbha in his literature.

The list "knowing the supreme and the not-supreme of conduct" and "the supreme of what is held and what is not held" involves terse Tibetan formulaic verse; the renderings follow the grammatical structure faithfully, though the theological nuance of "held and not held" (འཛིན་དང་འཛིན་མིན) may refer to grasping versus non-grasping, which is doctrinally plausible in context. The deity described — blue-bodied, bearing a sword, wielding the vajra, sovereign of the three realms, and surrounded by blazing light — is consistent with a form of Vajrapāṇi or a related wrathful vajra deity.

The phrase rendered "knowing the three" for གསུམ་རིག་རབ refers most likely to the three knowledges (of past, present, and future lives, and so forth), consistent with traditional buddha-epithets; this is clarified in the later line "having attained the power of the three knowledges."

The figure referred to throughout the section beginning "The Great God, the maker of bliss, radiant, holding in hand, three-eyed" is Śiva described in his characteristic iconographic form: three-eyed, fire-dispelling, holding weapons, of golden hue. This passage is part of a longer litany of epithets for Ākāśagarbha that draws on divine imagery including Śaiva iconography to praise the bodhisattva. The line "born from the three removers of evil" is somewhat opaque; it likely refers to three purifying or destructive qualities traditionally associated with the deity's power to remove the three fires of affliction.
