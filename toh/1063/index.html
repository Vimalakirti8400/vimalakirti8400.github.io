About this text

The Noble Great Cloud, known in Sanskrit as Āryamahāmegha and in Tibetan as Pakpa Trinchenpo, is a Buddhist scripture set in the palace of the nāga kings Nanda and Upananda, where the Buddha teaches before a vast assembly of monks, bodhisattvas, and a long roll of serpent kings who are named one by one. Because the nāgas are traditionally associated with water and weather, the work belongs to a family of texts concerned with bringing timely rain, relieving drought, and ensuring the welfare of the land and its people, and it offers protective formulas and ritual instructions for that purpose. It is Toh 1063 in the Kangyur, where it is placed among the dhāraṇī texts, the collection of incantations and protective formulas associated with the tantra section of the canon.

In the language of India: ārya mahā megha. In the language of Tibet: The Noble Great Cloud.

Homage to all the buddhas and bodhisattvas.

Thus did I hear at one time. The Blessed One was dwelling in the abode of the nāga kings Nanda and Upananda, in the palace of the Great Cloud Maṇḍala, the Essence of the Glory of Precious Jewels. He was together with a great Saṅgha of monks, a great Saṅgha of bodhisattvas, and a great assembly of nāga kings. These were the nāga kings Nanda, Upananda, Sāgara, Anavatapta, Manasvin, Varuṇa, Takṣaka, Dhṛtarāṣṭra, and Vāsuki; the nāga king Mucilinda, Airāvaṇa, Peacock Eye, Glorious Splendor, Excellent Glory, Garland of Lightning, Jewel Crest, Bearer of the Gem Crest, Radiant Topknot, and Rainbow Topknot; These great nāga kings and others, eighty-four hundred thousand koṭis of niyutas of nāgas in all, had gathered and were seated there with him.

At that time those nāga kings, together with their retinues, rose from their seats, draped their upper robes over one shoulder, and placed their right knees on the ground. Bowing toward the Blessed One with joined palms, with reverence for the teacher and with great devotion, they offered to the Blessed One countless, immeasurable, supremely beautiful offerings of every kind: flowers, incense, perfumes, garlands, ointments, powders, garments, parasols, victory banners, pennants, silken tassels, the sound of music, cymbals, clapping, songs, jewel flowers, jewel tassels, strings of pearls, nāga flowers, and nets of pearls. Having offered these, they circumambulated him and sat down to one side.

Having sat down to one side, they made these aspirations. They thought: "We shall create bodies as numerous as the atoms in all the oceans of world systems. In all the oceans of maṇḍalas of the assemblies of buddhas and bodhisattvas, in all the vast oceans of world systems, among the atoms of all earth, water, fire, and wind, and on every single atom of all that appears as form, we shall create oceans of clouds of bodies. They will pass beyond every ocean of counting, beyond the countless, the immeasurable, the inconceivable, the incomparable, the unfathomable, and the inexpressible. On each of these bodies we shall create immeasurable, countless oceans of clouds of hands, reaching out in streams toward every direction. And from every part of every atom, with distinct oceans of clouds of every kind of offering, filling the ground of all directions far and wide, we shall make offerings to all the buddhas and bodhisattvas."

Having made such aspirations, those nāga kings again circumambulated the Blessed One three times and bowed at his feet, and, with the Blessed One's permission, each sat down on his own seat.

At that time the great lord of nāgas of the great trichiliocosm, called Parasol-Like King of the Splendid Maṇḍala of Ocean-Cloud Arrays with an Endless Retinue, who possessed the power of an irreversible aspiration, came to this Jambudvīpa in order to make offerings to the Tathāgata, to pay homage, to honor and serve him, and to listen to the Dharma. When he had said this, the Blessed One spoke to the lord of nāgas Parasol-Like King of the Splendid Maṇḍala of Ocean-Cloud Arrays with an Endless Retinue: "Lord of nāgas, ask whatever you wish, and I shall satisfy your mind by answering each thing you ask."

When he had spoken, the lord of nāgas Parasol-Like King of the Splendid Maṇḍala of Ocean-Cloud Arrays with an Endless Retinue said this to the Blessed One: "Blessed One, how may all the nāga sufferings of all nāgas be cut off? How may all grains sprout, and all nourishing essences properly arise, so that the people of Jambudvīpa come to have happiness?"

When he had asked this, the Blessed One spoke to the lord of nāgas Parasol-Like King of the Splendid Maṇḍala of Ocean-Cloud Arrays with an Endless Retinue: "Lord of nāgas, you have set out for the benefit and happiness of all sentient beings, and you think to question the Tathāgata fully on this matter. Excellent, excellent! Therefore, lord of nāgas, listen well and carefully and keep it in mind, and I shall explain it to you.

"Lord of nāgas, if one possesses a single quality, all the nāga sufferings of all nāgas will be cut off and they will come to have happiness. What is that single quality? It is loving-kindness. Lord of nāgas, gods and humans who abide in loving-kindness are not burned by fire, not wounded by weapons, not carried off by water, and not harmed by poison. They are not overpowered by hostile armies. They fall asleep in happiness and wake in happiness. They are protected by their own merit. Through the splendor of great merit they become radiant. They are not overcome by the world with its gods. They become beautiful and delightful to behold. They become unobstructed in every destiny. All their sufferings are cut off and they become greatly joyful. They come to have every happiness, and having realized the superhuman Dharma, after the body breaks up they are reborn in the world of Brahmā. Lord of nāgas, these are the benefits for gods and humans who abide in loving-kindness. Therefore, lord of nāgas, one should abide in loving acts of body, loving acts of speech, and loving acts of mind.

"Furthermore, lord of nāgas, one should recite the dhāraṇī called Giver of All Happiness. It cuts off all the nāga sufferings of all nāgas and bestows every happiness. It makes a stream of rain fall on this Jambudvīpa at the proper times, and makes all grasses, shrubs, medicinal plants, forests and crops grow. Lord of nāgas, what is the dhāraṇī called Giver of All Happiness? It is this:

Translator's notes

Most of the nāga kings in this long list cannot be securely matched to Sanskrit names, so their Tibetan names have been translated by meaning. Sanskrit forms are given only for standard identifications: Nanda, Upananda, Sāgara, Anavatapta, Manasvin, Varuṇa, Takṣaka, Dhṛtarāṣṭra, Vāsuki, Mucilinda, Airāvaṇa, Elāpattra, Sudarśana, Citrasena, Indrasena, Kapila, Baladeva, Amoghadarśana, Nāgasena, Uttara, Nārāyaṇa and the like. Nor rgyas kyi bu is the standard rendering of Vāsuki itself (compare the glossary note on Vaiśravaṇa), and sa srung gi bu likewise renders Airāvaṇa. Balira, Pimpika, Vitara and Iṅgira are transliterated in the Tibetan and are given as such. "Wolf Belly" reads the evident spyang lto (Vṛkodara) for the written spyad lto, which makes no sense as it stands. The total "eighty-four hundred thousand koṭis of niyutas" renders the Tibetan compound of very large numbers literally. In this passage རྒྱ་མཚོ་སྤྲིན ("oceans of clouds") is a recurring descriptive phrase of the offering imagery, not the proper name Ocean Cloud used earlier, and དབྱངས་ཅན here means "melodious," not the goddess Sarasvatī. Likewise བླ་མར་བྱ is the verb "to revere," not the name Uttara. The verb བྱིན་གྱིས་བརླབས is rendered "by our power" in the sense of creating or sustaining the offerings through miraculous power. The final refrain reads "bodhisattvas, great ocean, ocean-clouds, oceans." This looks like an expanded or partly duplicated form of the usual "all the oceans of buddhas and bodhisattvas." It is rendered to keep its sense of vastness. The phrase "gateways and leaves" in the second list (sgo lo ma) is uncertain; sgo may be a corruption. The four-fold refrain renders the standard formula of honoring (satkāra, gurukāra, mānana, pūjana); the verb here rendered "revere" is the ordinary word for treating as a superior, not a name. The refrain is prescriptive ("one should..."), consistent with the preceding passage. The narrative frame then calls the whole recitation an aspiration made by the nāga kings. The term elsewhere glossed as Sarasvatī is used in this passage as an adjective qualifying jewel-kings. It has been rendered "melodious," since the context concerns sound and voice, not the goddess. In this passage, "maṇḍala" (dkyil 'khor) refers to discs or circles of light rather than ritual diagrams. The nāga king's long name could not be confidently identified with a Sanskrit form, so it has been translated by meaning. The names of the tathāgatas in the list are likewise rendered in English, following the earlier use of "Great Cloud". In the sixth tathāgata name the text reads "river" (klung); this has been taken as the similar-looking "wind" (rlung), in keeping with the chapter's subject, the wind maṇḍala. The long descriptive passage introducing the tathāgatas' names is syntactically difficult. It is taken here as a set of epithets describing the tathāgatas themselves; it may alternatively be read as describing a single source from whose hair-tip their lineage arose. The tathāgata names and the nāga king's long name are rendered by meaning, consistent with the English names used earlier in the text. No confident Sanskrit forms are given. The last homage is plural in Tibetan and may sum up the names listed or be a single extended name. "Grass, shrubs and trees" renders རྩྭ་དང་ཤིང་གེལ. ལྕེ་འབབ་པ is taken as falling lightning (lightning strikes). In the nāga king's name, "king" renders the final རྒྱལ་པོ of the Tibetan name. ལག་གིས་འགྲོ་བ and ལག་གི་འགྲོ, literally "those who go on their hands" (or "on their breasts"), are taken as the standard Tibetan rendering of uraga, "serpent," which is a term for nāgas and snakes. The phrase མགོ་སྟོང་པ་མགོ་དང་ in the description of the mahoragas reads awkwardly. It is rendered as "thousand-headed, with heads...", and the second "head" may be a scribal duplication. ཆར་ཆེས་བ་གཅོད is read as ཆར་ཆེས་པ་གཅོད, "stops excessive rain." "The eighth person" renders the Tibetan "the eight / the eighth" in the series of noble persons. It most likely reflects Sanskrit aṣṭamaka, the candidate for stream-entry.

The epithets of the rain goddess are Tibetan translations of Sanskrit names, and they are rendered by their meaning. "Mount Sulana" and "Holder of the Six Who Draw the Essence of the pontra" are obscure and are translated literally.

The long name of the first nāga king is likewise translated by its meaning. "The parasol-like king of the maṇḍala of splendor" may be part of the name itself rather than an epithet.

The minds of loving-kindness and of flawless discipline carry the honorific in Tibetan. They are taken here as the minds of the tathāgatas. The Tibetan names no subject for "urge", so the urging is rendered in the passive, consistent with "All the nāgas are summoned" in the preceding passage. Several nāga-king names in the opening list could not be confidently matched to a Sanskrit form and have been translated literally: Released and Held, Earth Guardian (whose son is named), Glorious Splendor, Glorious Excellence, Garland of Lightning, Great Gem Crest, Gem Crest Bearer, Radiant Crested One, and Five-Headed and Nine-Headed in the rite. This follows the literal names already used earlier in the text, such as Gem Crest and Three-Headed. Nuta is kept as transliterated in the Tibetan. "The ocean might let its proper time pass" alludes to the tide, proverbially never failing to come in at its time.
