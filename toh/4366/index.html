About this text

The Means of Generating Insight, in Tibetan Lhaktong Kyepé Tap, is a treatise on meditation that explains how to cultivate insight (vipaśyanā), the wisdom that discerns the true nature of things, on the foundation of calm abiding (śamatha). It opens with homage to the one who clears away with the weapon of wisdom the thicket of conceptual thought that grasps at things as real, yet does not rest even in that realization but through great compassion reaches out to beings with many skillful means, and it then lays out its topics, from the preliminaries and the nature, divisions and objects of insight to bodily posture and the abandoning of faults in meditation. Drawing on the sūtras and on Indian treatises such as Kamalaśīla's Stages of Meditation, it argues that nonconceptual meditation must be preceded by careful analytical discernment, with calm abiding and insight joined in union, and it rejects the approach of entering all at once without such analysis. No Sanskrit title is given and the work appears to have been composed in Tibetan; it is Toh 4366 in the Tengyur, the canonical collection of treatises, and it belongs to the class of meditation manuals.

Translation

The Means of Generating Insight.

Having fully cleared away, with the weapon of wisdom, the thicket-like conceptual thought that grasps at entities,
Yet not abiding even in that thatness, and therefore, toward beings who have not realized it and are lost in darkness,
Entering of its own accord, like a flowing river, through great compassion, with various skillful means:
To the one who taught these I bow with faith, and I shall briefly set out the means of generating insight.

The summary:
Through the preliminary, relying on calm abiding,
It must be realized, even by a beginner:
Its essence, its characteristic, its etymology,
Its divisions, its object, the means of generating it,
Those suited to it, merit, and the limbs,
Adjusting the body, linking, and settling,
Abandoning faults, the thought of rising, and having risen,
Keeping to measure, the stages of generation, the measure of abiding;
The methods taught are to be applied as fits:
Clarity, long sessions, generating and drawing it forth,
And the very doctrines of wrong conception;
One's own position is established by way of establishing nothing.

As for the preliminary: in the manner of the Mahāyāna sūtra collections, a follower who is a beginner should be well established in the vows of individual liberation, should generate the aspiring awakening mind, properly take the Mahāyāna vow from a qualified guru, and train in it respectfully. Abiding in the prerequisites of calm abiding, such as pure moral discipline, and with the higher mind thereby made somewhat stable, one should abide in the prerequisites of insight and, in order to train in higher wisdom, strive to seek thatness. As the Ornament of the Middle Way says:

Those who follow through faith,
Having generated the mind of perfect awakening,
Should take up the discipline of the Sage,
And then strive to seek true knowledge.

As for the prerequisites of insight, they should be understood from what appears in the section on the inexhaustible prerequisites of insight in the Teaching of the Noble Akṣayamati.

Now, clinging to entities as real is the ground from which the various afflictions arise, just as afflictions arise in a man through his wanting to meet an attractive form and clinging to it. Those who cling to the object of meditative absorption without generating insight will likewise have afflictions arise. As the Noble King of Samādhi says:

Though they cultivate that samādhi,
It does not destroy the perception of a self;
Their afflictions return and stir them up greatly,
As with Udraka's cultivation of samādhi here.

Those who have accumulated karma through the condition of the afflictions wander for a long time in saṃsāra and experience various sufferings, like a moth that has flown into a heap of fire.

But if it is asked, "Will one not be liberated by that samādhi, since ordinary conceptual thought is reversed?", it is not so, for the noble Maitreya said, "Clinging to a great feast mixed with poison." The noble Nāgārjuna also said:

If they find any place at all to stand,
They will be seized by the cunning, venomous snake of the afflictions;
Those whose minds have no place to stand
Will not be seized.

Therefore clinging does not abandon clinging. Otherwise it would follow that a burn is healed by putting it into fire, that something wet is dried by putting it into water, that hunger is satisfied by cutting off food, and that thirst is quenched by cutting off drink.

Others say, "If one sees with great vividness something whose nature is a form body, such as a tathāgata, or anything else, one will be liberated by that." It is not so, for the Noble Samādhi of the Direct Presence of the Buddhas of the Present says: "For example, although a lump of gold is something to be taken, if it has become extremely hot from fire it is not to be grasped with the hand. Why is that? Because from that basis bodily suffering arises. Likewise, although the form body too is something to be taken up, nevertheless, if one comes to cling to it, it is to be abandoned." And the Noble King of Samādhi says that illusion-like appearance cannot possibly occur except as ultimately unborn.

Furthermore, some here cling to what the Tathāgata taught with various skillful means, as provisional meaning, in order to lead foolish people who have not understood and have misunderstood the profound meaning of the Perfection of Wisdom and wander for a long time in saṃsāra; and they turn others back even from their faith in the profound meaning, saying: "This is not something to meditate on; it is hard to realize. Even without realizing it, there is another means of attaining awakening." "What is it?" "Meditating on signs." Those who say this and create obstacles accumulate karmic obscurations that are extremely hard to remove, through their wrong view and through obstructing others; and since they will not obtain even the body of a god or a human, how could they obtain unsurpassed awakening? As the Noble Questions of Rāṣṭrapāla says:

Because they do not know the suchness of phenomena,
Beings come to wander;
With compassion, knowing hundreds of methods and approaches,
You teach them.

The Noble Perfection of Wisdom in One Hundred Thousand Lines also says that such a person is born in the great hells, and it also says that someone else is māra who has taken on the guise of an abbot or the like. As the noble Āryadeva also said:

For one obscured by delusion
Who obstructs what is genuine,
There is no virtuous fruit;
What need to mention that there is no liberation?

Therefore a person who wishes to eliminate the immeasurable suffering of saṃsāra, his own and that of others, and who wishes to attain unsurpassed great awakening, whose nature is the three bodies and the four wisdoms, must thoroughly realize the suchness of phenomena, in order to cut down, with wisdom embraced by skillful means, which is like a sharp weapon, the clinging to entities as real, which is like a poisonous tree, the root of the poison-like afflictions that are the cause of immeasurable suffering. As the Noble Cloud of Jewels also says: "Son of good family, bodhisattvas think, 'Having attained this nature of reality, we will establish other sentient beings too in this very nature of reality.' That for whose sake they exert themselves and strive, undertake diligence, and perform hundreds of hardships, son of good family, is called suchness."

Others say, "Although that nature of reality must be realized, it is not something a beginner should train in." That is not so, for the Perfection of Wisdom in One Hundred Thousand Lines says, "Immediately upon generating the mind of awakening, one should train in the Perfection of Wisdom."

But if it is said, "That is something to be trained in only through hearing and reflection; it was not taught for the purpose of meditation," do not view it that way. Although it is hearing and reflection, it is also meditation, for in that sūtra no words of prohibition were spoken, while words establishing it were spoken in abundance. The Ornament of Realization too says, in the context of the path of accumulation, "Nonconceptual samādhi, and," and so on. And the first Stages of Meditation also says:

For the beginner who practices
In the way of the Mahāyāna sūtras,
I shall set forth in brief the stages
Of meditation that begin from there.

And in its section on cultivating insight it says that all phenomena, being without essential nature, are not apprehended. Therefore even a beginner must thoroughly realize that all phenomena are without essential nature.

What realizes this is none other than insight, and its essential nature is wisdom. As the Noble Unraveling of the Intent says: "Blessed One, by which perfection of a bodhisattva is the absence of essential nature in phenomena grasped?" "Avalokiteśvara, by the perfection of wisdom."

Its characteristic is discrimination. The same sūtra says, "That which discriminates and thoroughly discriminates the meaning to be known is insight." Elsewhere too it is said, "What is wisdom? It is the thorough discrimination of those very things."

As for its etymology: here, realizing what others have not realized and seeing what has not been seen is insight, superior seeing. As the Great Noble Questions of the Nāga King Sāgara says: "Blessed One, if one sees any phenomenon at all, that is not insight." That non-seeing is the highest of all seeing. As the Noble Sūtra on the Course of All Phenomena also says: "Blessed One, not seeing any phenomena is seeing correctly," said the venerable Subhūti.

Its divisions are three: the wisdom of hearing, the wisdom of reflection, and the wisdom of meditation.

As for their objects: the wisdom of hearing takes as its object the sound in its own particular character and the letters in which sound is arranged, because its object is the same as that of direct perception. It also takes as its object the sound-generic and the meaning-generic, because the cognition that ascertains what is directly perceived assists it. Some say here that the wisdom of hearing takes as its object only the sound in its own particular character. That is not so, because if, at the time of hearing the sound "pot," one does not understand its meaning, a thing with a slender neck and so on, it is the same as not having heard it. Then from what would the wisdom of reflection be generated? And without the wisdom of reflection, the wisdom of meditation does not arise. Therefore that is a wrong conception. The object of the wisdom of reflection is the sound-generic and the meaning-generic. Again, if one distinguishes by what is principal, since it is said, "Correct knowledge is of two kinds," the object associated with the final inferential cognition is the meaning-generic alone. The object of the wisdom of meditation is of two kinds: the object in its own particular character, and the meaning-generic. The object in its own particular character is the object of the wisdom associated with supramundane yogic direct perception. The meaning-generic is the object of the wisdom associated with the inferential meditation of ordinary beings.

The means of generating it are three: the wisdoms of hearing, reflection, and meditation. Of these, one should first generate the wisdom of hearing.

Here some say, "There is no need to study, for that is the wish to talk to others. Concerning fondness for talking, the Sūtra of the Exhortation to Superior Intention says:

'My practice has failed; what shall I do now?'
At the time of death great grief arises;
Finding no firm ground, they suffer intensely:
These are the faults of delighting in talk,

and so on. Therefore it is enough to meditate and to read the sūtras." That is not so. That passage speaks of talking for the sake of gain and honor and the like. Teaching the Dharma for the benefit of others is not without purpose, because its benefits are great. As that same Sūtra of the Exhortation to Superior Intention says: "Maitreya, giving the Dharma without regard for renown and honor, with no material motive, has twenty benefits. What are they? They are these: one has mindfulness; one has intelligence; one has understanding; one has faith; one has wisdom; one comes to realize supramundane wisdom; one has little desire; one has little aversion; one has little delusion; māra finds no opportunity against one; the buddhas, the blessed ones, keep one in mind; non-humans protect one; the gods infuse one's body with radiance; enemies find no opportunity against one; one is not parted from one's friends; one's words are worthy of being accepted; one gains fearlessness; one has much happiness of mind; one is praised by the wise; and one comes to remember the gift of Dharma. Maitreya, these are the twenty benefits."

It is also said that this upholds the teaching, for it says, "Those who uphold it are those who teach it, and," and so on. Therefore one must study for that purpose too.

Merely reading the sūtras does not bring realization of their meaning, for the profound intent of the Tathāgata is extremely hard to realize. Various understandings enter into even a single utterance; as it is said:

Though your single speech makes clear what is difficult,
Each construes it separately as many things;
"This is suited to my measure alone":
So it seems to everyone.

It is also said, "Scripture is like a stake in the mud." And it is said:

The faithful one sits with legs stretched out;
This sun, the Sugata,
Like a brahmin's tangled thread,
Is far from thatness itself.

Furthermore, if a beginner could understand the profound meaning merely by reading the sūtras, that would also contradict the statement that one should not give up the spiritual friend even for the sake of one's life, and it would follow that everyone who reads is a master of the three piṭakas. Therefore, as it is said,

If you wish to seek the Buddha's Dharma,
Follow after one of great learning,

it is extremely precious to rely on a spiritual friend and to hear much from him.

Its benefits are taught in the Sūtra Requested by Nārāyaṇa: "Son of good family, one who has learning comes to have wisdom. One who has wisdom comes to have the afflictions thoroughly pacified. Māra finds no opportunity against one who is without afflictions," and so on, at length, up to: "The bodhisattva who desires the Dharma, who has stainless splendor, should strive earnestly in the accumulation of learning."

Therefore, having heard from a spiritual friend who teaches without error, and having reflected well on its meaning, one engages in meditation. Otherwise, if one has not heard, what would one reflect on? And without reflecting, what would one meditate on? For a result without a cause is impossible. Therefore the master Kamalaśīla too says in the middle Stages of Meditation: "What are the prerequisites of insight? Relying on holy persons, thoroughly seeking extensive learning," and so on.

Again, some say, "One should only study; moral discipline may be set aside with indifference. Why is that? Because a human body is obtained even through the mere one-day fasting vow, so striving for the sake of moral discipline as a monk and so on is pointless." In that case: all the sūtras teach that moral discipline is the basis of all mundane and supramundane qualities, and its root is the monk's vow. If that were pointless, it would be something to abandon; and if it were abandoned, the root would be cut, so that all seven classes of individual liberation would be abandoned, and thereby all the happy destinies would be abandoned. Once that is abandoned, there is no basis for accomplishing awakening, so how could one accomplish awakening? Therefore one who speaks like this abandons the higher realms and liberation altogether.

But if it is said, "The one-day fasting vow that one has taken up is not abandoned," without the monk's vow that too does not come about. Nor is merely obtaining a human body enough, for here we see some who, though born in the human destiny through projecting karma, are unable to enter the Dharma because their completing karma is weak. How could awakening be accomplished with a human body like that? Therefore, in order to bring about a special happy rebirth, one must guard special moral discipline as one guards one's life. Otherwise one cannot be freed from conditioned things, and one is utterly crushed by sickness, old age, and death. As the Noble King of Samādhi says:

When a man, harried by many bandits,
Wishing to live, seeks to flee and get away,
But his feet cannot walk or run,
The bandits seize him and slay him;
Just so the deluded man whose discipline is broken
May wish to flee from conditioned things,
But with his discipline broken he cannot flee,
And is utterly crushed by sickness, old age, and death.

But if it is said, "When those of little wisdom and no learning break their moral discipline, they are born in the lower realms; but one of great wisdom and much learning does not go to the lower realms even if his discipline is broken," that is true: if the wise confess their transgressions and practice restraint, they do not fall into the lower realms. As it is said:

A ball of iron, though small, goes to the bottom of the ocean;
Yet when it is made into a vessel, it floats on the surface.
Likewise the unwise, though they do a small wrong, go to the lower realms;
The wise, though they do a great wrong, do not fall.

Therefore, like the iron made into a vessel, one does not fall if one confesses and practices restraint, but not by mere learning and knowledge. As the Noble King of Samādhi also says:

However many discourses of the Dharma one has mastered,
If one is puffed up with learning and does not guard moral discipline,
Then through broken discipline one goes to the lower realms;
For that learned one, learning cannot give protection.

Then if it is said, "Through much learning and pure moral discipline one cannot fall into error through afflictions such as disputing over views," that is not so, for the Noble Supreme Excellent Samādhi says: The Blessed One said to Nanda, "I remember that formerly, in a past age, when I was a person of great learning, I disputed with Mañjuśrī over existence and nonexistence. Mañjuśrī said, 'It exists,' and I said, 'It does not exist.' In that dispute over existence and nonexistence each held his own view to be true, and it was never settled one way. At the end, when I passed from that life, I fell into the three lower realms, and for countless eons I ate balls of iron." The same sūtra says: "A man of great learning has a mind of pride as great as a mountain or the ocean. One whose mind of pride is that great will experience suffering in the lower realms for countless eons. And if, freed from that hell, one is then born as a winged bird, what benefit is there in much learning?"

Therefore, abiding in pure moral discipline and possessing the patience that accepts suffering, one should strive at learning for the sake of oneself and others, and one should not dispute because of the afflictions. As the Noble King of Samādhi says:

Guard moral discipline, but do not be proud of it;
Listen to the teachings, but do not be proud of them;
Whoever, with little wisdom, is proud of these,
From that root his suffering increases.

What should one hear? Here one should hear the scriptures, the sūtras of definitive meaning that teach all phenomena to be unborn, and the reasoning that establishes them, which is like a lamp. Which, then, are the sūtras of definitive meaning, and which the sūtras of provisional meaning? The Noble King of Samādhi says:

As the Sugata has explained emptiness,
Know the particular character of the sūtras of definitive meaning;
Wherever a sentient being, a person, or a self is taught,
Know all those teachings to be of provisional meaning.

The same sūtra says, with reference to the sūtras of definitive meaning:

When this world-system comes into being,
And when all the worlds turn into space,
As it was before, so it is afterward:
Know all phenomena to be like that.

Whatever there is in this world,
This mass of water lies beneath it;
As it is below, so it is above as well:
Know all phenomena to be like that.

Just as in a sky without even a few clouds
In an instant a circle of clouds appears,
One should know from where it first arose:
Know all phenomena in the same way.

The tathāgatas who have passed into nirvāṇa:
Just as one brings them to mind, their image appears;
As it was at the prior limit, so it is afterward:
Know all phenomena to be like that.

Just as the many bubbles that are stirred up
And carried by a river a man clearly sees,
Yet when he examines them no core at all appears:
Know all phenomena to be like that.

Just as, when large drops of rain fall,
Water bubbles arise, each one separate,
And having arisen they burst, and there are no bubbles:
Know all phenomena to be like that.

Just as, when a letter is sent to another town,
Good and bad deeds come about, each separately,
Yet nothing has passed from the sound into the letter:
Know all phenomena to be like that.

Just as men who are drunk with liquor
Think that this whole earth is spinning around,
While this earth does not move or shake:
Know all phenomena to be like that.

When a woman with an adorned face looks
Into the surface of a mirror or a vessel of sesame oil,
The fool conceives attachment to what is there
And runs about intensely in pursuit of desire.
Yet the face has not passed into it,
And in the reflection nothing else is ever found;
Just as those deluded ones conceive desire,
Know all phenomena to be like that.

Like a mirage and a city of gandharvas,
Like an illusion and like a dream,
Meditation on signs is empty of essential nature:
Know all phenomena to be like that.

Just as when the moon has risen in a clear sky
Its reflection appears in a clear lake,
Yet the moon has not passed into the water:
Know the character of all phenomena in that way.

Just as people dwelling among wooded mountains
Hear echoes from their singing, talking, laughing, and play,
Yet nothing appears:
Know all phenomena to be like that.

Songs and music and likewise weeping:
In dependence on them an echo arises,
Yet the melody is never in the sound:
Know all phenomena to be like that.

Just as one who enjoys pleasures in a dream
Does not see them once he has woken up,
The childish are intensely attached to pleasures and cling to them:
Know all phenomena to be like that.

Magicians conjure forms,
Making various horses, elephants, and chariots,
Yet nothing there is as it appears:
Know all phenomena to be like that.

Just as a young girl in a dream
Sees a son born and sees him die,
And when he is born she rejoices, and when he dies she grieves:
Know all phenomena to be like that.

Just as at night the moons in the water
Appear in water that is clear and unclouded,
Where did they first come from, and where do they go?
Know all phenomena to be like that.

Since being like space ultimately and like the moon in water conventionally is the definitive meaning, if one asks, "It would be fitting for the Tathāgata to teach that to those who do not know it; what purpose is there in teaching the provisional meaning?" it is not so. The Tathāgata says nothing at all that is without purpose. The purpose there is like this. Some sentient beings say:

While you are not yet dead, seek happiness in life;
After death there is no sphere of experience for it.
When the body too has gone like ashes,
How could there be any birth again?

In order to reverse the conceptions of those who say this, he taught, with reference to the sentient beings of the intermediate state, that there are sentient beings born miraculously. In the same way, in order that certain lazy people might strive in virtue, he taught, with reference to the continuum of the aggregates, that there is a self. Therefore there is no contradiction.

When this is demonstrated by reasoning, the meaning of the scriptures cannot be led astray. Therefore one should hear the reasoning that teaches the selflessness of persons and the selflessness of phenomena. If one asks whether realizing those two realizes the profound meaning of the Mahāyāna, it is taught that it realizes it thoroughly. As the Noble Descent into Laṅkā says:

In the reality of the two selflessnesses
The whole of the Mahāyāna is contained.

The reasoning for the selflessness of persons is given by the noble Nāgārjuna in the Akutobhayā:

If it is said that the person transmigrates:
When it is sought in five ways
Among the aggregates, sense sources, and elements,
It does not exist; so what is it that transmigrates?

If one thinks that the person transmigrates, that is utterly untenable. Why is that? Because when one seeks it in five ways, asking whether it is those very aggregates, elements, and sense sources, whether it is other than them, whether they are in it, whether it is in them, or whether it possesses them, it does not exist. Since it does not exist, what is it that transmigrates?

The reasoning for the selflessness of phenomena is given in the master Śrīgupta's Entering into Thatness:

All these that abide outside and within
Are in reality without intrinsic nature,
Because they are free of the nature of one or many,
Like a reflection.

Whatever is free of the nature of one and many is ultimately without intrinsic nature, like a reflection. All these things that abide outside and within are likewise without intrinsic nature, because they are free of one and many.

That reasoning applies to everything, but here, for the time being, it is taken as the reasoning that teaches the selflessness of phenomena.

Now, do these physical phenomena that appear as if external abide by their own essential nature apart from the mind, or is it the mind itself that appears in that way, as towns, mountains, people, and the like appear in a dream? One should take it that it is the mind itself appearing. As the Blessed One said in the Noble Descent into Laṅkā:

Objects do not exist; it is mind itself.
Seeing external objects is mistaken.
For those who examine with reasoning,
Grasped and grasper cease.

The noble Āryadeva too said:

The root of existence is the mind itself;
Objects are its sphere of activity.
When selflessness is seen in objects,
The mind, the root of existence, is reversed.

The meaning of these words has already been explained without error a thousand times by scholars who have mastered language. Then, one may ask, what need is there to mention it here? That is true. However, a beginner of little learning, not cared for by a spiritual friend, relying on his own slight faith and diligence, gains some slight stability of mind, and so he comes to hate the lamp-like words of the learned. Moreover he says, "What use are words? That is merely staying within the Dharma; it is of no help. This is what I have experienced." He boasts with words unconnected to any meaning, and he says, "Do not show this to people wherever you go; they will come to disparage it." With this he pleases householders and renunciants of straightforward character, and they speak his praises. The learned leave him alone with equanimity, so pride arises in him and his own yoga too deteriorates. And others who practice after him, even if they practice just as he says, will practice wrongly.

As the master Asaṅga says concerning the deterioration of yoga brought about by wrong practice: "Thus, some here, when they exert themselves in what is improper, do not attain yoga and do not attain the appropriate virtuous qualities. Thus, such a person has many afflictions and a nature of strong desire; his consciousness is obscured, yet he has a sharp intellect and innate intelligence. He retains a little learning, or even much, and masters all that he has heard. He teaches the Dharma to forest-dwellers and to householders and renunciants who are straightforward and of straightforward character, who come and arrive; and with conduct of body and speech that follows hypocrisy he pleases their minds. By that cause and that condition a mass of gain and honor comes to him, and he is known as one of great merit, who obtains robes, alms food, bedding, seats, medicine for the sick, and requisites. Among kings, great ministers, and so on down to caravan leaders, he is renowned as an arhat; they honor him and treat him as a guru; and disciples, householders and renunciants, come to follow him. He generates attachment to the followers who have come, and he engages in gathering many of them, and so he thinks: 'My disciples, householders and renunciants, who trust that I am renowned as an arhat, have come. If they should ask me questions about yoga, mental attention, calm abiding, and insight, and I were to answer their questions with "I do not know," then the trust they have that I am renowned as an arhat would deteriorate. So I shall think it out by myself, examine it further, and set out a yoga.' Being attached to gain and honor, for that very purpose he goes alone into solitude, thinks it out by himself, examines it further, and sets out a yoga.

"That yoga of his does not accord with the sūtras, does not appear correctly in the Vinaya, and contradicts the nature of reality. He conceals that basis of yoga from the monks who uphold the sūtras, the Vinaya, and the mātṛkā, and does not show it to them. And he instructs his disciples, householders and renunciants alike, 'Keep this yoga secret.' Why is that? Because he thinks: 'If those who uphold the sūtras, the Vinaya, and the mātṛkā hear that basis of yoga, then if it is to be brought into accord with the sūtras, it will not accord; if it is to appear correctly in the Vinaya, it will not appear correctly; and if it is to be examined in the light of the nature of reality, it will contradict the nature of reality. On that ground they will not be pleased; they will disparage me with displeasing words, and disputes will arise. In that case I will not be served and honored by kings, great ministers, rich men, and so on down to merchant chiefs; I will not be treated as a guru; and I will not obtain robes, alms food, bedding, seats, medicine for the sick, and requisites. That will not do.' Under the sway of that very desire for gain and honor, while knowingly perceiving what is not Dharma as not Dharma, he alters his perception and his intention, and teaches what is not Dharma in the guise of Dharma, and presents it as correct. Those who practice in accord with his view perceive what is not Dharma as Dharma. Because they are inferior and deluded, they perceive what is not Dharma as Dharma, and so, even if they practice just as they have been instructed, it should be understood to be nothing but wrong practice. Such a deterioration of yoga, brought about by wrong practice, is a counterfeit of the pure Dharma made in order to destroy the holy Dharma; it is not the holy Dharma. Thus these four deteriorations of yoga are to be fully understood and fully abandoned by a monk who is a meditator practicing yoga."

Therefore the lamp-like words of the learned that teach emptiness are the supreme of all words, and hearing them is extremely precious. Likewise, the Course of All Phenomena says that the bodhisattva Gaganagañja said: "Words that bring about an understanding of emptiness are the supreme of all." And the Ornament of the Middle Way says that without understanding the words there is no other means of realizing it.

Someone may object: "But the sūtras say,

Awakening is not attained through the purity of words;
they take no delight in practice with earnest diligence as its heart,

and they also say, 'I do not hold words to be the main thing.'" That was said in order to refute those who cling to words alone and do not reflect and meditate. Nowhere in all the sūtras is it said that one should not hear words. Why is that? Because this teaching teaches the Dharma by means of sound.

Therefore it is like this. When gold has been lost in the dark, a great blazing mass of fire at the tip of a torch is close at hand. In the same way, although the suchness of phenomena, which is like the gold, is present in one's own body, one does not know it; and the learned, carrying words that teach suchness, which are like the fire blazing at the tip of the torch, are present in numbers in places not far away. So one should listen with great respect, regarding them as the Teacher, the Sugata, even if it is to no more than a single verse.

Four kinds of misconception about hearing,
one misconception about what is heard;
refuting these in five ways,
one must hear: this is the summary.

This is the summarizing verse.

Now the wisdom that arises from reflection is to be generated. This concerns the place in which, and the kind of person by whom, it is generated; avoiding, for the time being, what is not to be reflected upon; what to reflect on; how to reflect; and to what extent to reflect.

As to the place, one should dwell in a hermitage, and there one should not live together even with a congenial companion, two together. As the master Asaṅga said: "What is reflection? Here, someone goes alone into solitude," and so on. The Noble White Lotus of the Holy Dharma also says:

When the wise one reflects at the proper time,
having entered a hut and likewise closed the door,
he examines all these phenomena properly.

As for avoiding what is not to be reflected upon, it is said: "having avoided the six inconceivable topics, namely, thinking about the self, thinking about sentient beings, thinking about the world, thinking about the karmic maturation of the actions of sentient beings, the domain of the meditative concentration of those who practice concentration, and the domain of the buddhas."

Therefore it is to be generated in a solitary place, by a person who has attained mental engagement and who wishes to meditate on signlessness.

What should one reflect on? One reflects on the sūtras of definitive meaning, and on the reasoning that establishes them, just as one has heard them. As the master Asaṅga said: "One reflects on those very teachings, just as one has heard them, by way of their specific and general characteristics."

How should one reflect? In two ways: reflecting by enumeration and reflecting by evaluation. Here, one should understand that one reflects on the teaching of the aggregates by way of number, and proceeds by reflecting at ever higher levels; reflection of this kind and the like is reflection by enumeration. Reflection by way of evaluation weighs the four reasonings, namely: the reasoning of dependence, the reasoning of efficacy, the reasoning of the nature of reality, and the reasoning that establishes validity. Of these, the reasoning that establishes validity is direct perception and inference; and of these, in the present context, what is to be taken is reflection by means of inference.

Therefore, having entirely given up all gatherings and busyness, the yogin who dwells in a hermitage endowed with the five qualities and who has attained mental engagement with any object of calm abiding whatsoever should do as follows. He should nourish his body with food in keeping with the Dharma, such as ghee, and with drinks, such as beer, of a kind that does not intoxicate, for four or five days, or, depending on his constitution, even for up to ten days. For the time being he should not do recitation, reading and the like either. He should not meditate during the day and should not sleep, and in all his activities he should be relaxed and at ease. In the morning, and in the evening before dusk, he should perform once each the seven-branch practice, together with the generation of the awakening mind, without many prostrations to the deities and so forth. In the early part of the night he should meditate on that object of calm abiding for a single session, no longer than the time it takes to milk a cow, and having made a brief aspiration, he should lie down on his side and sleep, with vigilance going before. However much sleep comes, he should not resist it.

Even if it does not come, apart from relying on the antidote that counters desire, ill will and the like when they suddenly arise, until the adverse state subsides, he should not deliberately seek out even an antidote; how much less should he deliberately think of what is unwholesome! He should not examine even what is morally neutral.

Then how should he think? He should think only this much: "By lying down and sleeping I will strengthen the capacities of body and mind, and then, for the sake of all sentient beings, I will seek the suchness of all phenomena."

Having done so, when the strength of his constitution has been restored, his body is light, and he is eager to engage in activity and can hardly hold back, he thinks: "Having reflected well with the wisdom that arises from reflection, I will be able to abandon the signs that dwell outside and within; and when the signs are abandoned, I will enter the sphere of non-conceptuality. Therefore I should examine well." As the Commentary on the Dhāraṇī of Entering Non-conceptuality also says: "Without close examination one cannot entirely abandon the signs, and without entirely abandoning the signs one will not enter the sphere of non-conceptuality. Therefore one must without fail examine closely."

Some say that discerning wisdom need not go first, and that even beginners should not bring anything to mind at all. Their position has already been refuted by the very learned master Kamalaśīla, who made it extremely clear that beginners need discerning wisdom to go first. That is like the sun risen in a sky cleared of clouds; it has no need to be lit up by one who is like a firefly.

Again, some say: "Relying merely on the words 'there is no intrinsic nature, because it is free of the nature of one or many,' meditating earnestly on non-conceptuality will never reverse conceptual thought. For the Noble Cloud of Jewels says, 'Through mere hearing and reflection the meaning does not become directly perceived.' Therefore one must definitely analyze discerningly." That is a wrong understanding of the meaning of the sūtra.

Why is that? Because what it says is that through mere hearing and reflection the meaning does not become directly perceived, and that one must therefore meditate. So to say, "Through mere hearing and reflection the meaning does not become directly perceived; therefore one must analyze discerningly," is a thesis contradicted by one's own words. The formal argument is this: whatever statement has earlier and later parts that contradict each other is contradicted by its own words, as when someone says, "My mother is barren." This statement, "Through mere reflection the meaning does not become directly perceived; therefore one should analyze discerningly," likewise has earlier and later parts that contradict each other. This is a reason based on nature. Therefore that position is a poor one.

To those who speak as above, one should say this: "Mere hearing is not enough; one should reflect properly." As the master Kamalaśīla also said in the section on the prerequisites for insight: "thoroughly seeking much learning, and reflecting properly."

Some say: "Evaluating with reasoning at the time of hearing is itself reflection; it is not that one reflects again after first relying on calm abiding." That is not so. For the Noble Sūtra on Cultivating Faith in the Great Vehicle says: "Son of good family, by this enumeration as well it should be understood that whatever faith in the Great Vehicle and whatever entry into the Great Vehicle bodhisattvas have, all of it arises from correctly reflecting on the meaning and the Dharma with an undistracted mind." And in what he composed as the first Stages of Meditation he also says: "Therefore, holding it with the hand of samādhi, one should remove, with the weapon of extremely subtle wisdom, the thorn of the seeds of wrong conceptualization regarding form and so forth that is present in that mind. Thus, just as trees pulled up by the roots do not grow, so that mind of wrong conceptualization, having no root, will not arise again."

Therefore the reflection done at the time of hearing is not enough by itself; it must be taught that samādhi goes first. Nor is that enough either: with the wisdom that abides in it, one must analyze discerningly the meaning one has heard. As it is said, after the lines beginning,

Just as Udraka here cultivated samādhi,

it says, in the lines beginning,

If one discerns the selflessness of phenomena,
and, having discerned it ...

Therefore, relying on calm abiding, one should generate the wisdom that arises from reflection. At this stage, the single session of meditation in the early part of the night that one did earlier, while restoring one's constitution, is also set aside. Sitting in whatever posture is comfortable, one reflects for a period lasting about as long as it takes to walk, at an ordinary pace, three arrow-shots for those of strong constitution, two for those of middling constitution, and one for those of weak constitution. As for the number of sessions, those of strong constitution reflect for four a day, those of middling constitution five, and those of weak constitution six. That the weaker have more sessions is offset by their being shorter. All three reflect for one session on the first day, and then go on reflecting, adding one session each day, until they reach the measure suited to their own constitution.

Whether during that period, or while reflecting after having reached one's own number of sessions, if signs of physical unease appear, one should rest and recover, and while not reflecting, settle it at once. If it is during the phase of adding sessions, one should not add more until the unease no longer arises; and if one is unwell during the phase of reflecting at one's own measure, one should reduce the number of sessions and settle the unease with massage and the like, and then reflect as before. None of the three reflects at all at night; the fatigue of the day should be restored through sleep and lying down.

Here, first of all, one reflects, just as one has heard it, with the reasoning that teaches the selflessness of persons. The treatises present, together with their refutations, countless discordant tenet systems, mostly those of the non-Buddhists (tīrthikas) who propound doctrines of grasping at a self. These should indeed be known; but for the time being they do not harm most practitioners. Then what does harm them? It is this. In the ordinary conceptuality of one who has not heard the teachings on the aggregates, elements and sense sources, or who, though he has heard much, does not follow the Dharma, there is the thought: "I was born from this father and mother. I remember having done such and such actions before. This body of mine has remained in this way from before." Thus it grasps the four aggregates as the self and grasps the aggregate of form as belonging to the self; and it conceives that self as permanent and single, or else simply rests content in thinking of it that way. This is what should be examined.

The formal argument is this: whatever, when sought by direct perception and inference, is not observed as being those things themselves, is not found as something separate from them either, in which they do not appear, which is not observed in them, and which is not seen as possessing them, does not exist even conventionally; as, for example, when one searches in these five ways for a man in what is really a cairn, he is not seen. This thought "I" too, when sought by direct perception and inference among the aggregates, sense sources and elements in the five ways, beginning with "Is it those very things?", is not observed. So in effect this is the non-observation of the pervader, because the convention that something exists is pervaded by its being observed by one of the two kinds of cognition.

Here one first reflects a little, thinking, "Does a self exist separately from the aggregates?" Then one thinks: "First, it is not seen by direct perception: even when I look with both eyes wide open, I do not see anything at all that could be a self. Nor is it seen by inference, because there is no evidential sign whatsoever." Since the self is thus not seen as something separate, and the thought "I" arises with regard to the aggregates, one should examine a little how the ordinary conceptuality described above operates, thinking, "These very aggregates are the self."

Then one thinks: "What is that cognition which grasps the aggregates as a permanent and single self? Does it grasp it by direct perception, or would it grasp it by inference? First, by direct perception one does not see the body as single: even when I look with both eyes wide open from the feet up to the head, in all that I see I do not see any partless single thing at all. And even when I stroke it with my hand, I do not find any partless single thing at all."

"Nor do I see it as permanent: even for a moment it appears differently, as going, staying, resting, rising, sitting, lying, reclining, stretching, contracting, hot, cold, wet, dry and so on. Nor do I see it as single by inference. Why? Because, in brief, the aggregate of form has fifteen kinds, the aggregate of feeling three, the aggregate of perception three, the aggregate of formations forty-nine mental factors and fourteen formations not associated with mind, and the aggregate of consciousness eight. Some of these I see by direct perception; some are taught in trustworthy scripture and are also known by inference from their functions. Since they are understood to be many, there is no single thing. Nor do I see permanence: whatever is compounded by causes and conditions is not permanent, like a dance performance. These aggregates too are compounded by causes and conditions, so they are not permanent. This is the observation of what is pervaded by a contradictory property. Therefore, by direct perception and inference I do not see these aggregates as permanent and single."

Then one may think, "Is it seen that the aggregates are in the self?" That is impossible, because the basis is not established. Then one may think, "Is the self in the aggregates?" That too is untenable. For whatever is not seen to be in something cannot be designated conventionally as existing in it, just as horns are not seen on a horse and so cannot be designated as existing there. In these aggregates too the self is not seen by either of the two kinds of cognition, so it cannot be designated as existing. Then does the self possess the aggregates? No. For whatever has no established nature of its own has no property of possessing anything, just as the son of a barren woman does not possess properties such as a beautiful form. This permanent self, so insistently asserted, is likewise not established by a nature of its own, so it has no property of possessing anything. This is the non-observation of the pervader. What exists conventionally is observed by direct perception and inference; therefore its exclusion from the dissimilar class is established.

Not found by the five, it is present in the similar class;
its exclusion from the dissimilar class is established;
being present in the similar class, the example is established;
the reason and the example are without fault.

This is the summarizing verse.

Since the impermanent appears as many,
it is not refuted by direct perception;
since there are no signs to prove the two,
it is not refuted by inference either.
Since it was not stated to be permanent and single,
there is no contradiction with one's own words;
since impermanence is meant with regard to the continuum,
it is not refuted by scripture either;
clinging to false scripture is not understanding.
Since nothing is reckoned as permanent and single,
it is not refuted by common acceptance either.

This is the intermediate verse.

To what extent should one reflect? Until, with regard to these aggregates, one is certain that they have many parts, are many, are porous and full of gaps, and are impermanent, so that the notion of them as permanent and a solid whole no longer arises; and there arises a certainty that shows them to be without core or self, not one but a collection of many, possessing a continuum of perishing phenomena. By the force of this, in some, disenchantment arises and displeasure comes about. In others, joy arises, with the thought, "The holy Dharma is wonderful!" and with the thought, "That cognition which grasps things as permanent and single is certainly mistaken; there is no certainty there of a self or sentient being as permanent and single." One should reflect until this point. As the Noble Descent into Laṅkā also says:

Here there is no self or sentient being;
these phenomena arise together with their causes.

Again, some say: "The person is not established as permanent or as single, as stated above. Nor is it established as impermanent and many, because that would be refuted by our own words. There is a person that cannot be described as either." One may wonder whether such a thing could exist. It could not. For since it cannot be described as permanent, it is devoid of oneness; and since it cannot be described as impermanent, it is devoid of manyness. As the Ornament of the Middle Way says:

Since the person cannot be described
as permanent, impermanent and so forth,
it is clearly understood
to be devoid of the nature of one and many.

Now one should reflect on the selflessness of phenomena. First one should think: "If it is certain that what is called a person, imagined to be permanent and single, does not exist even conventionally, then by what are these aggregates, sense sources and elements comprised, which arise from causes and conditions and are characterized by impermanence and multiplicity? How do they appear? Do they exist ultimately, or are they not established as existent?"

By what are they comprised? By two: the material and the non-material. The material comprises the aggregate of form, ten sense sources and ten elements. The non-material comprises four aggregates, part of the aggregate of form, two sense sources and eight elements.

Here one should reflect: "These material aggregates, sense sources and elements, such as the body, empty and occupied houses, mountains, forests, oceans, rivers and streams, appear to the childish as if they were cut off and outside. Do they exist apart from the mind just as they appear? Or does the mind itself appear as them, through the force of clinging, from beginningless time, to form and so forth that are not real, just as in a dream a body, the ground, empty houses and so forth appear?"

Here one reflects: "If these, the body and so forth, are broken down into infinitesimal particles, and the particles in turn are individually examined as to the nature of their parts, they cannot be definitely apprehended, and so they have no intrinsic nature; just as there is no person, because it is free of being one or many." As the Noble Descent into Laṅkā says: "Mahāmati, even an ox's horn, when broken down into particles, does not remain; and when the particles in turn are broken down individually, they do not remain with the characteristic of particles." It also says:

External form does not exist;
it is one's own mind that appears as external.

Therefore, to think that form and so forth truly exist separately is to follow wrong conceptuality. There is no reasoning that proves it, while there are many valid cognitions that undermine its existence; and since scripture too teaches that it does not exist, it is certain that it is the mind itself that appears.

As that same Noble Descent into Laṅkā says:

Break substances down into particles,
and do not conceive of form.
The establishment of mind only
is not understood by those of bad views.

Since body and so forth are not established even as particles,
they do not exist, just like the person.
It is established by scripture, and since the other is wrong conception,
there is no reasoning to establish that; this stands firm.

This is the summarizing verse.

Here one might think: "Proving that things have no intrinsic nature ultimately would not establish that the mind itself appears either, because the reasoning applies to both alike. But if one proves that objects do not exist separately even conventionally, that is not right. For whatever has no establishment does not appear, just as no eye consciousness arises to which a sky flower, the son of a barren woman or the horn of a rabbit appears. So this idea that things appear even though there is no object would entail that they do not appear. This is the non-observation of the pervader." If one thinks so, that is not right. For what is seen as different does not abide as one, as with a variegated color. A single woman, too, is seen differently by vultures and the like, by a lustful man and by a yogin, so she does not abide as a single entity. This is the observation of what contradicts the pervader. Since there is no separate object, there is no apprehending of it either. As the Noble Descent into Laṅkā says:

There is no object; it is mind itself.
To see external objects is mistaken.
For those who examine with reasoning,
the apprehended and the apprehender cease.

If one proves there is no intrinsic nature ultimately,
the reasoning is the same, so mind too does not exist.
If it is not proved even conventionally,
that is not right, just as horns and the like do not appear.
If one thinks this entails its nonexistence,
that is not right, since one thing is seen as different.
There is nothing apprehended apart, so no apprehender of it:
so the Sugata has said.

This is the summarizing verse.

Therefore one might think: "The root of the three realms of existence is mind alone, and its own aspects appear as objects. Since these are its field of experience, each is correlated with it accordingly. As Āryadeva says:

The root of existence is mind itself;
objects are its field of experience.

Since that mind arises in dependence, it exists ultimately. As the Noble Descent into Laṅkā says:

The imagined thing does not exist;
the dependent does exist.
The conceptions of the extremes
of superimposition and denial will be destroyed."

If one thinks so, that is not right. For let us first examine this body that is under consideration. As the master Dignāga says:

Having viewed all objects of knowledge, without exception,
as being by nature merely one's own aspect,
with the vajra of the wisdom of suchness
one should utterly destroy the machine of the body.

So, if this body exists ultimately, does it exist with the characteristic of being partless, or as something with parts, or as infinitesimal particles? Taking first the first option: if this finger is a partless thing, does it exist apart from the joints, or is it connected with the joints? If it existed apart, then since it would be an object observable as described above, it would follow that it is observed separately, like a pot and a bolt of cloth. Therefore, since it is not observed separately, it does not exist. This is the non-observation of a nature. The option of its being connected is not right either, for then each of the three joints would be a complete finger. If one thinks, "Let it be so," then, since it is partless, if one joint were covered the whole finger would be covered, and if one were red the whole would be red. But if one joint is pervaded by only one part of it, then it has parts, and its singleness is lost. Therefore, since it does not exist apart, the idea that it possesses the joints also fails. For since the joints are not different from the finger asserted to be one, the three joints would become one, just like the finger's own nature. And the finger too, being not different from the three joints, would become many even though it is asserted to be one, just as there are three joints. Since it neither exists apart, nor is there the option of its possessing them or being connected with them, the option of its being partless is not established, and so it does not exist.

Then, according to the second option, one may think that it exists as a coarse thing with parts, or as a collection. That too is not right. For the first joint and the rest cannot be described as either other than their own parts or not other than them, so they are not established and do not exist. Therefore a coarse thing with parts is not established as existing either. In the same way, form and so forth should be understood to be nonexistent, because their nature too is merely a collection, and a collection is taught to be without real entity.

Then, according to the third option, one may think that it exists with the characteristic of infinitesimal particles. That too is not right. For an infinitesimal particle too has different parts, and so is not established as one. This is the observation of what is contradictory to a nature. Then one may think, "Since those different parts exist, is not manyness possible?" If a single partless particle is not established, many partless particles are not established either, because they are an accumulation of that.

The cognition that grasps this with a certain degree of certainty is established by means of inference from a sign. After that, one thinks: "These aspects, the body and so forth, are ultimately free of being one or many, and therefore ultimately have no intrinsic nature, like a reflection." The arising of the cognition that thinks, "Not even one thing can be observed," is inferential cognition. As the Noble Sūtra Requested by Druma, King of the Kinnaras says:

Just as a reflection appears in a mirror,
free of being one thing or another,
appearing, yet not existing there,
so is the essential nature of things.

Then one may think: "Mind and mental factors, being partless, exist ultimately." That is not right. For eye consciousness and the rest are not different from their many aspects, and so the many aspects are one with their own essential nature. Then one may think, "They exist as many." That too is not right, for it is said:

Whatever thing is examined,
in it there is no oneness.
Where there is no oneness,
there is no manyness either.

Therefore, if what appears as objects has no intrinsic nature, how could the mind exist? For the two are not different.

As Āryadeva also says:

When selflessness is seen in objects,
the mind, the root of existence, ceases.

He also says:

There is nothing other than the collections,
nor is there anything not other than them.
Not established in that way, the joints and the rest do not exist;
therefore it comes down to a mere collection.
By the fingers and so forth, which are collections,
all things are shown to be nonexistent;
apart from collections, there is not even a subtle thing.
So where can one look for a mind
that knows a single object?
How could there be here a single object
for a mind together with its collection of mental factors?

The thought that the mind exists ultimately because it arises in dependence is valid only for the conventional, since there it is a reason based on nature. If one tried to prove ultimate existence with it, that would be the observation of what is pervaded by a contradictory property. Otherwise, illusions and the like would also exist ultimately. Therefore Nāgārjuna says:

When what arises from the condition of ignorance
is examined with correct knowledge,
neither arising nor ceasing
is observed at all.

The scripture beginning "The dependent does exist" was also spoken with the conventional in mind; for the sūtra itself says:

When analyzed by the intellect,
there is no dependent and no imagined;
and if the perfected thing does not exist either,
how could the intellect conceive anything?

To what extent should one reflect? When the sign has been established and inferential cognition has arisen, the mind no longer strays from the meaning it has understood to anything else; and since the mind finds no place left to analyze, a resting in its own way comes about. One should reflect to that extent. After that has arisen, there comes the thought, "It seems a mind that abides in nothing at all is arising." Therefore, with an undistracted mind, one analyzes the selflessness of persons and of phenomena just as explained, and when one has reached that measure, one sets it aside and rests for a while.

What comprises these, and an account of how they appear,
the refutation of wrong conceptions about them,
the examination of the body, and the teaching of the measure.

This is the summarizing verse.

Now it is right to generate the wisdom that arises from meditation. One reflects: "The wisdom that arises from reflection has definitely established the sign of being free of one and many, and after recalling the relation, a cognition that has definitely analyzed all elaborations that superimpose entity, non-entity and so forth has arisen firmly. Even so, if it is not made habitual through the wisdom that arises from meditation, it is impossible for nonconceptual wisdom to arise, the wisdom that directly sees all phenomena as like the expanse of the sky. It is as when a racecourse has been pointed out and cleared of every obstacle to running, yet if the horse does not run, there will never come a time when it reaches the end of the course."

As the Noble Gaṇḍavyūha says:

Just as one of the king's men
may count many treasuries
yet not gain even half a coin,
so too is the Dharma not meditated upon.

and so on. The Noble King of Samādhi also says:

Suppose a man is sick and suffering,
for many years never free of it at all.
Afflicted by the suffering of disease,
he seeks a doctor to cure his illness.
He searches for one again and again,
and finds a doctor, skilled and wise;
and that doctor, moved by compassion,
gives him medicine, saying, "Take this medicine."
Yet though he has obtained that excellent, most beneficial medicine,
he does not drink the medicine that would cure his illness.
The doctor is not at fault, nor is the medicine;
the fault lies with the patient himself.
Just so, those who have gone forth in this teaching
and have come to know all the powers, concentrations and faculties,
yet do not strive earnestly at meditation:
not striving at what is right, how could they reach nirvāṇa?

Here some say: "To meditate is simply to make habitual; it has no other meaning. Therefore nonconceptual wisdom arises solely from making habitual the discerning analysis and discrimination of the wisdom that arises from reflection." That is how it is commonly held, but it is not right. For the Sūtra of Scattered Words says:

Like one who gropes with hands and feet
and runs along a perilous path,
one who holds inference to be the main thing
will not find it hard to fall.

Therefore it is also said in the first Stages of Meditation: "Having thus examined the true meaning, discriminating it with the wisdom that arises from reflection, one generates the wisdom that arises from meditation in order to make it directly perceived. That the meaning does not become directly perceived through mere hearing and the like is taught in the Noble Cloud of Jewels and elsewhere." In the Noble Cloud of Jewels, the bodhisattva Sarvanīvaraṇaviṣkambhin asked: "Blessed One, is not the Dharma that sons of good family have understood through the cognition arising from hearing, or from reflection, known by oneself?" The Blessed One said: "No, son of good family. Through mere hearing or mere reflection one cannot understand the Dharma that is known by oneself."

"Son of good family, therefore listen to an example. Son of good family, it is like this. Suppose that in a great wilderness, in the last month of summer, a certain man is going from the east to the west, and then another man comes from the west toward the east. Scorched by the summer heat and tormented by thirst, he says to the man coming from the east: 'O good man, I am scorched by the summer heat and tormented by thirst. Show me the way to where there is a lake, a pond, or a pool full of water that is cool, clear, free of mud, and limpid, where my heat and thirst may be fully relieved.' And the man coming from the east is one who knows the way, who is skilled in the way, who has seen the way, who has seen the landmarks of the way. He has seen that water, free of all the faults of water, before; he has tasted it before and has bathed in it before. He says to that man: 'O friend, go along this road until you see a fork in the road. O good man, leave the left-hand road, and go by the right-hand road until you see a dark green forest, and in the middle of that forest a lake, a pond, or a pool full of water that is cool, clear, free of mud, and limpid. There, good man, rid yourself of thirst and heat.'

"Son of good family, what do you think? Will the man scorched by the summer heat and tormented by thirst become free of heat merely by hearing of the water and by thinking of it? Will he be parted from his thirst? Will he know the coolness of the water for himself?"

He replied: "No, Blessed One. Blessed One, when that man goes there, enters the water, and drinks, then that man's heat will be gone, he will be parted from his thirst, and he will know its coolness for himself."

The Blessed One said: "Just so, son of good family, by mere hearing and mere reflection one cannot understand the Dharma that is known by oneself. Here, son of good family, the word 'wilderness' is a designation for the wilderness of saṃsāra. The words 'a man scorched by the summer heat and tormented by thirst' are a designation for sentient beings scorched by the torment of the afflictions and tormented by craving for sense-objects. The words 'one who knows the way' are a designation for bodhisattvas skilled in the path to omniscience, who have experienced the Dharma and know the Dharma for themselves. The words 'water that is clear, limpid, free of mud, and pure' are a designation for the ultimate nature of reality.

"Again, son of good family, listen to an example. Son of good family, what do you think? Suppose the Tathāgata were to remain for an eon as the measure of his life, and before the people of Jambudvīpa were to speak in praise of the nectar of the gods, of its fragrance, its purity, its excellence, its great sweetness, and how pleasant it is to the touch. Son of good family, what do you think? Would those beings come to know for themselves the very qualities praised in that divine nectar?"

He replied: "No, Blessed One."

The Blessed One said: "Son of good family, therefore you should understand that by merely hearing or reflecting on this account one cannot understand the Dharma that is known by oneself.

"Son of good family, again listen to an example. Son of good family, it is like this. Suppose a man has tasted a kind of fruit that no being has ever tasted before, and before another man he speaks in praise of that kind of fruit, of its perfect color, its perfect taste, and its fragrance. Son of good family, what do you think? Would that man come to know the taste of that fruit for himself?"

He replied: "No, Blessed One."

The Blessed One said: "Son of good family, therefore you should understand that by merely hearing or merely reflecting on this account, however you hear it, one cannot understand the Dharma that is known individually by oneself."

Without meditation it does not become manifest;
The wrong conceptions about this,
And their refutation, have been taught,
And set out at length through scripture.

This is the summarizing verse.

Now, the components of the accumulation of merit are the Three Heaps, the seven branches, the ten Dharma practices, the thirteen Dharma practices, or the twenty Dharma practices; for the Compendium of Trainings (Śikṣāsamuccaya) says, "Because these are heaps of merit."

Therefore, the yogin who abides well in the prerequisites for calm abiding and insight, such as moral discipline, as mentioned above, should, at the time of meditation, first complete every preliminary task there is. Then, in an agreeable place free from the thorn of noise, such as a rock cave, a grass hut, or a house, he should sweep, plaster the floor clean, and make a maṇḍala as best he can. There, painted images of the buddhas and bodhisattvas are set out in front, or cast images are placed in front, and offering articles, as much as one can afford, are arranged in the house in their proper order. Then one thinks as follows:

"In the world-systems of the ten directions, of all the sentient beings there are, there is not a single sentient being who has not many times been my father and mother. So the Tathāgata taught in the Sūtra of Beginningless Time. Therefore, just as a dutiful person in the world gives up any hostile thought toward father and mother, casts away resentment, and wishes only that both father and mother be happy, so I too, toward these parents who from beginningless saṃsāra have brought forth and nurtured countless bodies of mine, shall not give rise even for a moment to a hostile mind, which is like dry, barren ground where no sprout of virtue or benefit can grow. Giving up resentment as well, I shall meditate on the wish that, through faultless Dharma, from today until I sit at the heart of awakening, they may always meet with happiness."

Thinking thus: that is loving-kindness.

Again, in the world, a dutiful person in whom the wish that his father and mother be happy has arisen, if he sees them dwelling in suffering and the causes of suffering, which are the opposite of that, feels within himself an unhappy feeling associated with mental consciousness, and by its condition a painful feeling associated with bodily consciousness arises, and his happiness is forsaken. In just the same way, the yogin too, having generated the wish that all these fathers and mothers be always happy, sees that they dwell in suffering and the causes of suffering, which are the opposite of that. For the five contaminated aggregates are a mass of suffering, as the Noble Questions of Vīradatta says: "Since the five aggregates are established, there is a mass of suffering." Therefore one generates intense anguish of mind, thinking: "How may these beings, who suffer from the three kinds of suffering, each as it applies, be freed from these masses of suffering?" That is compassion. Because through it the yogin's happiness and mental ease are forsaken, it is also called "that which blocks them." The illness of suffering and unhappiness that arises when, seeing others suffer, one forsakes one's own happiness, arises from compassion. As the Noble Teaching of Vimalakīrti says: "The illness of bodhisattvas arises from compassion."

Thus, stirred by the anguish of great compassion, one generates the resolve: "I shall completely eliminate the suffering of all beings and establish them in the heart of awakening." Then, visualizing all the buddhas and bodhisattvas who dwell in the ten directions as directly present to the mind, one says these words:

To the buddhas of the ten directions and three times,
To the Dharma of scripture and the ultimate Dharma of realization,
To the noble ones and to those who train in their footsteps,
I pay homage with faith through my three doors.

At that point one also presents the offerings that were set out earlier:

Flowering trees, fruit trees, and trees of fragrance,
Trees of incense, trees of jewels, and all the rest,
Reaching to the limits of space and owned by no one,
I take up with my mind and offer to the Three Jewels.

Until I reach the heart of awakening,
I go for refuge to the buddhas;
To the Dharma and the assembly of bodhisattvas
I likewise go for refuge.

Whatever my mind, since beginningless time,
Through hundreds of faults such as clinging and desire,
Has done, had others do, or rejoiced in, through body, speech and mind, the three,
And all my dreadful malice, I confess before the Three Jewels.

For the sake of deeds in accord with the Dharma,
To the buddhas and the bodhisattvas
Of all the world-systems,
I shall surely offer my own body.

In all the buddhas' generating the resolve
And turning the wheel of Dharma,
And in the virtue gathered by the bodhisattvas and the rest,
I rejoice.

The Sugatas who, though they have attained awakening,
Remain without speaking at all,
I urge to turn the wheel of Dharma,
So that their assemblies may take up the true Dharma.

Those who say they will display the passing into nirvāṇa,
To tame lazy disciples
And to show that conditioned things are surely impermanent,
I beseech to remain for as long as saṃsāra lasts.

Just as the Sugata Dīpaṃkara and others
Generated the mind of awakening together with its conduct,
So I, having generated the mind set on great awakening,
From today shall abide in the three trainings.

In order to abandon all conceptual thought,
I have paid homage and the rest to the Three Jewels.
Therefore may this virtue I have gathered
Not become a ripened fruit for myself.

Thus one should dedicate.

Now one should arrange the body. On a seat, a low seat, a spread of grass, or a comfortable cushion, one should sit in the cross-legged posture. Why is that? Because one rightly sees five reasons. When the body is well composed, since that posture accords with the arising of pliancy, pliancy arises very swiftly indeed. Likewise, sitting in that way one is able to pass a long time, and that posture does not greatly tire the body. Likewise, that posture is not shared with the other tīrthikas and opponents. When others see one sitting in that posture, great faith arises in them. And likewise, that posture has been permitted and practiced by the buddhas. Rightly seeing these five reasons, one should sit cross-legged, straighten the body upright, and sit with mindfulness set facing the object.

What is an upright body? It is to set the body straightened out, and to set the mind straight, without guile and without pretense. Because the body is held well upright, the mind does not take hold of dullness and sleep and remain in them. Because the mind is without guile and without pretense, it does not take hold of outward distraction and remain in it.

What is it to remain with mindfulness set facing? Mindfulness associated with proper attention is called "mindfulness facing" because it is turned away from and opposed to the whole dark side.

Then set the shoulders level, and set the head neither high nor low, not leaning to one side, and set it straight in line from the nose to the navel. The eyes are neither opened too wide nor closed too far, but directed to the tip of the nose, and the teeth and lips are also left in their natural state. The tongue rests against the upper teeth. The breath going out and drawn in should not be let out noisily, forcefully, or roughly; rather, breathe in and breathe out slowly and effortlessly, so that it is hardly felt at all.

Then what is the linking thought? As soon as the breath has become even, one thinks: "These sentient beings of the three realms of existence have not realized the suchness of all phenomena; they misconceive it and have not become familiar with it. They have not realized that supreme nonconceptual wisdom of the three bodies, which makes suchness directly perceived. By all means I shall meditate on that nature of reality without error, constantly, for a long time, and with devotion, and having attained that wisdom, I shall bring others too to attain the wisdom that makes reality directly perceived."

What is that suchness? All phenomena are not ultimately established as existent, nor are they established as nonexistent. Therefore they are not established as both, and there is nothing that is neither.

So too the Sūtra of Mañjuśrī's Abiding says: "That practice is free of elaboration and fully pacified. It does not assert 'it exists,' nor does it assert 'it does not exist.' It does not assert 'it both exists and does not exist,' nor does it assert 'it neither exists nor does not exist.'" Therefore the wisdom of reflection finds nothing whatsoever, and merely that, in the sense of its not being otherwise, is designated and called "suchness." Therefore one should keep in mind that even mere suchness is not truly established.

Then how is the mind to be placed? Having recalled that even mere suchness is not established, immediately draw the mind and awareness together very tightly, and do not place it by following any of the words that express the profound, such as "this is the nature of objects," "the nature of mind," "phenomena just as they are," "the inconceivable expanse," "nothing whatsoever," "the ultimate," or "free of all elaboration."

Then what is the abandoning of faults? In brief there are four: the fault of the secondary afflictions, the fault of not knowing the characteristics of calm abiding and insight, the fault of the indeterminate, and the fault of physical and mental discomfort. How each is abandoned is taught in the section on that fault.

The secondary afflictions are three: excitement, lack of delight in the object, and laxity.

The nature of excitement is that the mind does not stay on its object, like a distracted person or like a monkey. Its causes are four. If it is the longing of attachment to another's body, arising from attending to the sign of the attractive, one should pacify it by attending to the livid corpse, the festering, the worm-eaten, the bloated, the devoured, the bloodied, and the scattered corpse, to a heap of bones, a skeleton, or a skeleton of bare bones. Or else, the Noble King of Samādhi says:

Whoever, with a mind of desire,
Consorts with rotting women
Will go to a rotten destiny,
And thus will go to the lower realms.

One should pacify it by recalling this.

If ill will arises from the cause of anger and the mind is distracted, one should pacify it by meditating on loving-kindness, the wish that all sentient beings be happy, or by recalling the drawbacks of ill will. The Sūtra of Maitreya's Lion's Roar says: "Whoever gives rise to a hostile or evil thought toward a bodhisattva must put on the armor to dwell in hell for just that long." The Noble Sūtra of Mañjuśrī's Play also says: "Anger, Mañjuśrī, is called anger because it destroys the roots of virtue gathered over a hundred eons." And the Noble Moon Lamp Sūtra says:

Whoever harbors thoughts of harming others,
Neither moral discipline nor learning protects him;
Meditative concentration and dwelling in solitude do not protect him,
Nor do giving and worship of the buddhas protect him.

If one is distracted by thoughts of relatives, of one's homeland, or of not dying, by recalling former laughter, play, and pleasures enjoyed, or by attachment to possessions, one should recall that all conditioned things are impermanent and suffering. Or else one should pacify it with verses such as these from the Noble Lalitavistara:

The three realms blaze with the suffering of age and sickness;
Ablaze with the fire of death, here there is no protector.
Beings who arise from existence are ever deluded,
Circling like bees trapped inside a vase.
Ever-lovely forms and pleasing sounds,
Fragrant scents, the finest tastes, and pleasant touches:
In this net of evil times these beings are caught,
Like a monkey caught in a hunter's net.

If one is distracted by regret and doubt, it is pacified by attending to the profound Dharma.

If one is distracted through lack of delight, that too belongs to the side of excitement, so one should apply the remedy as above. Then again draw the awareness together very tightly and place it without abiding on anything at all.

The nature of laxity is a lack of clarity, like being blind, or like a person who has gone into darkness, or like having one's eyes closed. Its causes are very tiring work, attending to darkness, food and drink that bring on sleep, staying in a closed room, and eating and drinking too much or too little. To abandon it, one takes up the sign of light very well and thoroughly, attends to it well, displays it well, and examines it well. Then, with a mind possessed of light and radiance, whether indoors or while walking on an open walking path, one encourages the mind, makes it take hold, uplifts it, and gladdens it thoroughly through recollection of the Buddha, or of the Dharma, the Saṅgha, moral discipline, or generosity, or recollection of the gods, or any object that inspires faith. Or one looks out in the ten directions, or turns one's gaze to the moon, the constellations, the planets and the stars, or washes one's face with water, or looks at a lamp or the light of a jewel, and so one becomes refreshed. Or else one should recite verses such as these from the Sūtra of the Exhortation to Superior Intention, and so clear the mind:

Whoever comes to delight in torpor and sleep,
In him the great net of delusion grows.
If he finds a view, he falls into doubt,
And many other such things arise in him as well.

If there is no delight, one sinks into laxity; then one should brighten the mind as above, by walking about and so on. Then again draw it together firmly and place it without abiding on anything at all.

The indeterminate mind is when the mind moves neither outward nor inward. It engages neither in virtue nor in nonvirtue, nor does it engage in insight, nor does it hold to calm abiding; it remains dejected and overcome by sleep. It is like a man who has just woken from sleep: his mind is obscured and unclear in his two eyes. In the same way, at that time the mind moves without clarity. One should treat it with the methods of brightening given above for laxity, and again place it without abiding on anything at all.

Now, the fault of not knowing the characteristics of calm abiding and insight is this. Suppose that, finding no delight in the object, the mind has become lax, or one fears it is becoming lax, and one has brightened it; or, finding no delight in the object, one sees that one is distracted, regards that as a fault, and has pacified the distraction; or there is excitement from some other cause of excitement, or one fears excitement, and one has pacified it; or one recognizes the mind as indeterminate, uplifts it, and sets it in clarity. If at that time one looks with vigilance and sees that awareness is very clear and grasps at nothing at all, then it is free of both laxity and excitement, and calm abiding and insight are joined as a pair. At that time, therefore, leave it entirely unaltered.

Departing from this reasoning, some think, "The characteristic of calm abiding is one-pointedness, so surely something must be held," and they search for an object of focus. Thinking, "Surely the characteristic of insight is discerning the real," they engage in conceptual analysis. That is like a man who wants to make a lamp burn brighter and fans it with the hem of his garment: he puts the lamp out, and it does not grow brighter. In the same way, even if that person meditates for many hundreds of thousands of years, he will not attain the path in which calm abiding and insight are joined as a pair. Therefore the characteristic of insight in meditative equipoise is simply realization, and the calm abiding joined with it is simply not moving from that. Since the two are complete in a single instant, rest in that state.

When, after a while, one feels that awareness is grasping at something, or feels heavy and dull, or feels it is sinking or has sunk into the depths, or feels feeble, then calm abiding predominates and insight is weak. At that time, rouse your strength and recall again and again how you earlier realized the nature of reality of all phenomena, and as soon as certainty arises and the mind becomes clear, rest in that state. If, though you do this, certainty does not arise, then follow any reason whatsoever and generate the wisdom of reflection, and as soon as you are certain, rest in that state.

The fault of weariness is twofold: weariness of body and weariness of mind. It may come from meditating too intensely or from some other condition. If body and mind are uncomfortable, change your posture a little, direct your attention, and think: "Conventionally, I shall regard all sentient beings as like reflections in a mirror; ultimately, they are like a garment of frog's hair." So too the Noble Teaching of Vimalakīrti says: Mañjuśrī asked, "How should a bodhisattva regard sentient beings?" When he had said this, Vimalakīrti replied, "Regard them as like reflections in a mirror. Regard them as like a garment woven from the hair on a frog's back." Having reflected in this way and rested, resume the posture in which you sat before, and place awareness without abiding on anything at all.

In brief: if the mind is lax, brighten it; if it is excited, soften it; when it is evenly engaged, leave it in equanimity; if calm abiding predominates, recall the meaning that was realized. If that does not produce certainty, follow a reason, and as soon as certainty arises, rest in that state. Even once certainty has arisen, if one goes on analyzing, insight predominates and the mind wavers like a lamp set in the wind; at that time, relax gradually and rest. If, in that state, awareness remains very clear and grasping at nothing, meditate for as long as you are able.

What, then, is the mind with which one arises? It is of two kinds: arising with the mind of wisdom and arising with the mind of method. Arising with the mind of wisdom is this: when you wish to rise from samādhi, before breaking your posture, think, "All phenomena are unborn from the very beginning, like space." Then think of how they appear conventionally. So the Noble King of Samādhi says:

Just as, from a sky with not the slightest cloud,
In an instant a mass of clouds appears,
Consider from where it first arose:
Understand all phenomena in the same way.

Arising with the intention of method is this: "Although ultimately all things are thus unborn from the very beginning, conventionally the mind itself appears in many forms. The forms included in the world of sentient beings are like elephants and the like conjured by an illusionist. What is included in the vessel world is like a mirage appearing as water. Objects and enjoyments are like dreams. The nature of bodily action is like an optical illusion. The nature of verbal action is like an echo. Beings who move in the desire realm are like reflections. Beings who move in the form and formless realms are like the moon in water. Pure buddha fields and the like are like emanations. Therefore these illusion-like sentient beings, their minds inverted so that they take these phenomena, which ultimately have no essential nature, as existent and so on, accumulate various karma through the condition of the afflictions, and so for a long time they wander in saṃsāra and suffer. By all means, like an illusionist, with an unerring mind I shall gather immeasurable accumulations of merit and wisdom, attain the wisdom of omniscience, and then bring all beings to attain great awakening." Thinking thus, join your palms and say these words:

To the buddhas of the ten directions, with their heirs, I pay homage with devotion and make offerings of all I have.
In their presence I confess my misdeeds, and I rejoice in all merit.
Those who sit at the heart of awakening I urge to turn the wheel of Dharma.
Those who intend to pass into nirvāṇa I beseech to remain for countless eons.

By this virtue, may all beings without exception
Abandon the demon of grasping at things, be filled with love,
And, brought to full maturity in nonconceptuality,
Swiftly attain nothing less than great awakening.

Having made this aspiration, rise.

What is the conduct after rising? In every circumstance, never be separated from the awakening mind. For the Compendium says: "For beginners, not being separated from the awakening mind is needed as the cause of awakening."

That conduct consists of activities such as generosity, having emptiness and great compassion as their essence. So too the Noble Compendium of Dharma declares: "In the bodhisattva who sees correctly, just as it is, great compassion arises toward sentient beings. That is the thought: 'I must accomplish for all sentient beings this gateway of samādhi that sees all phenomena correctly, just as they are.' Urged on by that great compassion, he perfects the trainings in higher moral discipline, higher mind, and higher wisdom, and fully awakens into unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening."

And in the same text: "Blessed One, whatever bodily action, whatever verbal action, and whatever mental action bodhisattvas have, all of it proceeds with regard for all sentient beings. It is preceded by great compassion. It is under the sway of great compassion. It arises from the superior intention to bring benefit and happiness to all sentient beings. One who has such an intention to help forms this idea: 'Whatever brings about benefit and brings about happiness for all sentient beings, that I shall accomplish.'"

On this very matter, in the Noble Gaṇḍavyūha Sūtra, the bodhisattva Jñānaśrī said this to the bodhisattva Mañjuśrī: "O son of the Victors, how do bodhisattvas come to possess blameless bodily action, blameless verbal action, and blameless mental action?" and so on, questioning him at length. The bodhisattva Mañjuśrī then said this to the bodhisattva Jñānaśrī:

"O son of the Victors, you have set out in this way to act for the benefit of many people, for the happiness of many people, out of compassion for the world, and to bring about the welfare, benefit, and happiness of gods and humans. That you thus think to inquire into points such as these is excellent, O son of the Victors, excellent! O son of the Victors, there is an application of attention in the work of the mind, possessing which bodhisattvas obtain all these special qualities. Through it they become unhindered in all the qualities of a buddha; they enter the path of the buddhas, the blessed ones, of the past, the future, and the present; the mind that never abandons any sentient being stays close to them; the vision of all phenomena just as they are stays close to them; they abandon all nonvirtuous qualities; they come to possess all virtuous qualities; they become all-good and all-beautiful and endowed with every supreme aspect; they gain mastery over all phenomena and become a second teacher to all living beings. O sons of the Victors, what then are those applications of attention in the work of the mind which, if a bodhisattva possesses them, he obtains all these special qualities?

"Here, O son of the Victors, when a bodhisattva is living in a household, he forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings, knowing its true nature, not be overwhelmed by the confinement of the household.'

"When keeping company with father and mother, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings keep company with the buddhas, in order to protect all beings.'

"When keeping company with sons and daughters, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings, being wholly free of craving, be free of both delight and displeasure.'

"When engaged with the pleasures of the senses, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings, through the perfection of good qualities, dispel desire and clear it away entirely.'

"When engaged with song, dance, music, and cymbals, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings see all this as a dancer's show, and gain the eloquence by which the true Dharma goes forth.'

"When staying among a circle of wives, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings be free of the tethering post of desire and enter the noble ground.'

"When putting on ornaments, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings go beyond to the destruction of becoming in existence, and lay down their burden.'

"When going up to the upper storey, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings understand the distinctive Dharma and reach the upper storey of the Dharma.'

"When giving gifts, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings be without possessiveness and without a grasping mind, and give away everything that is theirs.'

"When going about in company, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings reach the culmination of omniscience and be wholly free of gatherings.'

"When he sees a prison without reproach, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings move about without any fixed abode and go wherever they wish to find delight.'

"When going forth from home into homelessness, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings have minds neither bound nor freed, be without attachment, and be free of all that is not needed.'

"When crossing the boundary of the Saṅgha's enclosure, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings possess a Dharma free of conflict, and have much that proceeds in harmony.'

"When going before his preceptor and master, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings take up the Dharma and become skilled in its rites.'

"When requesting ordination, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings be without obstacles and become irreversible.'

"When casting off white garments, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings give up striving in wrongdoing, and thoroughly purify their roots of virtue.'

"When shaving off hair and beard, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings be perfectly settled in meditative equipoise and be free of afflictions.'

"When putting on the saffron robes, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings possess the mind of a great sage and be free of turbidity.'

"When going forth, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings, in order to be fully protected, go forth with the going forth of the Tathāgata.'

"When going for refuge to the Buddha, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings, having awakened devotion to virtue, strive so that the lineage of the buddhas is not cut off.'

"When going for refuge to the Dharma, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings hear the treasury of the Dharma.'

"When going for refuge to the Saṅgha, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings be free of every ground of attachment, and gather the Saṅgha together.'

"When correctly taking up the words of the training, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings rightly practice good conduct, and train thoroughly in all the trainings.'

"When the master gives instruction, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings engage in actions that are not wrong, and be perfect in conduct and in their sphere of activity.'

"When the preceptor gives instruction, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings be rightly directed by way of non-abiding, and reach the perfection of the wisdom of non-arising.'

"At full ordination, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings reach the far shore of the distinctive qualities of all phenomena, and attain the perfection of skillful means.'

"When going to the monastery, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings become such that nothing can be stolen from them, and move freely in the unsurpassed abode.'

"When spreading out a seat, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings see phenomena just as they are, and be well ordered in all things.'

"When sitting down, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings, with a mind that abides nowhere, sit at the heart of awakening.'

"When crossing the legs, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May the roots of virtue of all sentient beings be firmly established on the Immovable Ground.'

"When training in calm abiding, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings strive toward the final end, leaving nothing out, and be wholly devoted to peace and to self-taming.'

"When training in insight, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings become wholly free of afflictions, and see phenomena correctly, just as they are.'

"When unfolding the crossed legs, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings be perfectly settled in meditative equipoise, and clearly see that all formations by their very nature fall apart.'

"When setting down the soles of the feet, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings abide firmly in the undisturbed liberation of mind.'

"When lifting the soles of the feet, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings attain blamelessness, and rise up out of their sinking in the ocean of saṃsāra.'

"When putting on the lower garment, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings possess unsurpassed shame and conscience, and may their roots of virtue be firmly established.'

"When tying the belt, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings become undisturbed, and be firmly bound to their roots of virtue.'

"When putting on the upper robe, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings reach the far shore of all phenomena, and gain ever higher roots of virtue.'

"When putting on the outer patched robe, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings become undisturbed, and be wholly clothed in sublime means.'

"When taking up the tooth-stick, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings, in order to gain what is wholly pure, gain the pure and sublime Dharma.'

"When chewing the tooth-stick, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings utterly destroy their afflictions, and may their minds be tamed and their minds be pure.'

"When going to a secluded place, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings abandon attachment, aversion and delusion, and abandon all wrongdoing and all non-virtuous phenomena.'

"When going to the water's edge, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings fully emerge by way of all the qualities that transcend the world.'

"When performing the cleansing, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings possess what is wholly pure, clean and certain.'

"When washing the hands, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings, in order to hold all the qualities of the Buddha and to be pure, have auspicious hands.'

"When washing the face, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings be wholly free of afflictions, and gain the pure gateways of all phenomena.'

"When taking up the monk's staff, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings make offerings and show the path.'

"When taking up the alms bowl, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings become worthy of the offerings of all beings, and become fit vessels.'

"When setting out on the alms round, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings be rightly directed toward non-abiding, and turn toward the sphere of activity of the buddhas.'

"When setting out on the road, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings go everywhere, leaving nowhere out, and enter the path of the buddhas.'

"When walking, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings, having accomplished the wholly pure Dharma that is free of obscuration, realize the sphere of reality, which has no edge and no center.'

"When seeing a road that climbs, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings be utterly undaunted, and rise up above the three realms.'

"When seeing a road that descends, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings have firmly established roots of virtue, and bow low before all the buddhas.'

"When seeing a perilous road, the bodhisattva forms the resolve: 'May all sentient beings be free of crooked and twisted views, and wholly abandon perilous paths.'

So it is taught.

One should train in these as far as one is able, matching them to the capacity of one's constitution. Even while one's constitution is sound, one should also give up thinking with an ill-thinking mind, speaking ill-spoken words, doing ill-done deeds, and letting the time pass in ordinary pursuits.

The conduct after rising has now been explained.

What, then, is gauging the measure of one's constitution? After the wisdom that comes from reflection has arisen, if there is some slight sign that the constitution is unsettled, then for one day or one night, or for as long as two days, do nothing else except recall from time to time just the wisdom of reflection as it arose; and, without meditating either, let the constitution be restored.

Then, once the constitution has been restored, or else when no disturbance of the constitution has occurred, those who do not wish to remain in meditative equipoise after the wisdom of reflection should do as follows. At night they should not meditate at all, but lie down and sleep for as long as is needed for the constitution to be restored. On the first day, one meditates for one session in the morning and one session in the afternoon; a session is about as long as it takes to walk half a bowshot at an ordinary pace. Then, adding one session in the morning and one in the afternoon each day as one meditates, on the fifth day there are ten sessions. Moreover, if no minor ailment arises, one should keep adding in the same way; if one does arise, one should not add more until it has subsided. Even if no ailment arises, from then on, until stability of mind is attained, one should not meditate more than ten sessions a day.

If one's constitution is weak, or even if it is middling, stabilizing the mind is difficult, so that after about ten days one's constitution has little strength left; or if, though one's constitution is strong, one finds that the mind is slow to settle, so that after about twenty days one's strength is small; or if a minor ailment arises: then one should calm it with food in keeping with the Dharma, with drink, with massage and the like, building up one's endurance and not meditating.

Again, if one is generally weak toward the end of a session, or weak in the evening, then from that day on one should meditate while cutting back by one or two sessions at a time. When the constitution has become strong again, one should meditate as before. If one does this, one soon comes to know whether one's constitution is strong, middling or weak, so each day one should meditate only as much as one's strength allows. These are the ways of gauging the measure of the constitution until one has gained fortitude of mind.

What, then, are the stages in which the mind arises through meditation? In brief there are three: arising very quickly, immediately after the wisdom of reflection; arising before very long has passed; and arising after a long time has passed.

Of these, the very quick kind is of two sorts. For some, at the time the wisdom of reflection arises, one recalls how all phenomena are realized, and how the mind may be placed in that realization without contradiction. The moment one has produced a little of this linking awareness, one tightens the mind intensely and places it without resting on anything at all, and it settles all at once. Right then, no longer feeling that the body is there, it becomes like ice melting; and like clear water in a vessel set out before dawn, it is not very vivid. If it does not stir, that is abiding.

When this has happened, one meditates for as long as it takes to walk a little more than three bowshots at an ordinary pace; then one breaks off that bliss while it is still flowing and rises. There is no need to make any adjustment in that session. After that, for many days, so long as solitude of body, an unspoiled mind and one's strength are not exhausted, one can abide as before even without adjustment. If the necessary conditions are lacking, or one's strength grows weak or changes, one adjusts as is fitting, as taught earlier.

For others, when they place the mind as above, it immediately settles steadily and remains unmoving, yet the experience of not feeling that the body is there does not arise; there is merely an absence of the thought that links names with what they name. If this happens, one meditates for as long as it takes to walk one bowshot at an ordinary pace, and rises. From that session on, too, one should not meditate longer than that, and in the periods after rising one should from time to time read and recall only the scriptures and treatises that teach the ultimate. Then, when it develops and an experience of not feeling that the body is there arises, one meditates at that point for as long as it takes to walk about three bowshots at an ordinary pace, breaks off that bliss while it is still flowing, and rises. When to adjust and how to adjust are done as above. These are the two ways of very quick arising.

Arising before very long has passed is of two sorts. For some, when the mind is placed as taught above, four stages of arising occur. First, the awareness scatters and does not want to stay; even when one tightens it and places it, there arises an experience of restlessness, unsteadiness, roughness and stiffness, of not wanting to stay even for a moment. If this happens, one meditates for as long as it takes to walk about half a bowshot at an ordinary pace, and breaks off. For some, no experience beyond that arises for a while.

Then, after many days, it develops, and there arises an experience in which, by the force of tightening, the awareness scatters less and there is less restlessness, and one abides, yet it is rough and stiff and has no firm hold. Then it develops a little more: the mind does not scatter outward, and the sense of restlessness and roughness also subsides, but the breath is uneasy and the awareness sits stiffly; and from time to time, at the start or the end of a session, there arises an experience of slipping into a slight smoothness.

Then, if it develops without many days passing, whether at the start, middle or end of a session of meditation, or at some time when one is not meditating, suddenly, upon recollection, it grows steadier and steadier and more and more at ease, as when, for example, one goes into a warm house straight from being out in a storm; and it abides smoothly and stably. Like being within a sky that is not very bright, it is not very vivid, and one does not feel that the body is there. If this happens, one meditates for as long as it takes to walk a little more than three bowshots at an ordinary pace, breaks off that bliss while it is still flowing, and rises. When adjustment is needed and the means of adjusting are done as stated before.

Again, for some it happens like this. One places the mind as stated before. Coarse thoughts do not arise and there is little restlessness, yet there is no mark of abiding. If an experience arises of merely staying unscattered by the force of tightening, one meditates for as long as it takes to walk about half a bowshot at an ordinary pace, and rises. When, by meditating for that long, it develops, then by the force of tightening there come, at the start and end of a session, occasional touches of smoothness or of holding; sometimes, when a session is going well, it rests as before. When that develops, by the force of tightening it does not scatter until the end of the session, and it gradually abides smoothly, yet there arises an experience in which, when one relaxes, even that much is not there. Then it develops further: if one tightens, it drifts into drowsiness, so there is no need to tighten; and if one lets go, it is hard to let go, so it is enough to leave it relaxed. When, though not very vivid, the experience of not feeling that the body is there arises, one meditates for as long as it takes to walk a little more than three bowshots at an ordinary pace, breaks off that bliss while it is still flowing, and rises. When adjustment is needed and how to adjust are done as stated before. These two are the two ways of abiding before very long has passed.

Arising after a long time has passed is of one sort. For some, when the mind is placed as stated before, it scatters in all directions and does not want to stay; when the awareness is tightened it becomes uncomfortable again, so that when one relaxes there arises something that feels like ease. Such a person meditates for as long as it takes to walk about half a bowshot at an ordinary pace, and rises. Since this is very hard to stabilize, one tightens and places the mind even though it is uncomfortable, and after a long time it develops. There arises an experience in which, even when one tightens for most of a session, there is no discomfort; scattering is slight, and it is easy to notice and easy to stop. Through growing familiar with that, after a long time, there arises an experience in which, when tightened, it is smooth and does not scatter, and when relaxed it scatters only in little flickers. Then, before long, as one carries on from meditation, or while resting, or on lying down to sleep in the evening, for the time is not fixed, it settles to the brim. Even if one tightens, one cannot; relaxing is also hard. Taking on a touch of sinking, there arises an experience in which one does not feel that the body is there, and which is not very vivid. With that, one meditates for as long as it takes to walk about three bowshots at an ordinary pace, breaks off that bliss while it is still flowing, and rises. When adjustment is needed and how to adjust are done as stated before.

The stages of arising have now been explained.

What is the measure of abiding? By the power of its causes it is divided into three: the best, the middling, and the least.

Of these, the best is as follows. Whether awareness is tightened or not, it does not stir from the object of attention, and it is hard to feel even the slight, gentle movement of the breath. No perception of the body as coarse or subtle arises. As the characteristic of pliancy, a wind in harmony with meditative equipoise is caught, so that one feels as though one were sitting inside a house, and softness and lightness are strong. Although there is no sinking, no excitement, no indeterminate mind, no weak insight and no weak calm abiding, because one has not grown accustomed to it for long, it remains like a darkness that is not very black. With such characteristics, whether one settles quickly or settles later, the capacity is small. Apart from the times of adjustment, that mind which was at first hard to enter is able to stay by its own momentum for as long as it takes to walk a little more than three bowshots at an ordinary pace.

The middling is as follows. When one looks at the breath, its gentle movement is easy to feel. One just barely does not know how the body is placed. As the characteristic of pliancy, a bodily wind in harmony with meditative absorption is caught, so that one feels as though one were sitting inside a white tent that does not stir; softness and lightness are moderate; and one is able to stay for as long as it takes to walk about two bowshots at an ordinary pace. The rest should be understood as before.

The least is as follows. When one looks at how the body is placed, one comes to know it. There is a little softness and lightness. Catching a wind in harmony with meditative absorption, the characteristic of pliancy, is easy. When one is off one's guard, even while meditating one feels as though one had gone in under a mere lean-to or a mere canopy. One does not feel the breath coming out through the nose and mouth, and even when it is let half out from within, it is felt as utterly at rest, soft and extremely subtle. One is able to stay for as long as it takes to walk about one bowshot at an ordinary pace. The rest is as above.

The measure of abiding has now been taught.

How should one rely on the methods as appropriate? Gauging by the condition of one's constitution, whichever of the three degrees of mental abiding arises, the best, the middling or the least, one should know its characteristics. For the time being one should be content with that much. One should not become too elated. Meditate matching the capacity of the constitution to its own momentum.

If one does not know the characteristics, that mental abiding is lost. How so? Even when abiding of the best measure arises, some, never having experienced such a thing before, take fright and break it off. And because they were told, "At the stage of making the mind that did not abide before abide, if you meditate both day and night your constitution will become disturbed, so do not meditate at night," they keep that in mind, and if abiding arises at night they think, "This is not something to meditate on," and break it off. And again, because they tightened and focused before, at that point, although there is no need to tighten, they tighten all the more and do not take it into practice. And when the middling or the least arises, having heard that the best arose for other companions, they do not take into account the differences in causes; thinking, "It is the same measure for everyone, so I am lacking this," they make excessive effort. The other three are as before. Therefore one should know the measure of abiding as it was taught before.

The fault of not being content is this. Some, though they know the characteristics of mental abiding, out of the pride of great learning, when the middling or the least arises, expect far too much; and even when the best arises, thinking, "I will do something greater than others," they strive too hard, and so it is lost. Therefore, if the best arises, that is plenty, and even if the middling or the least arises, one should rely on it for the time being.

What fault is there in being too elated? Some, even when only the middling or the least has arisen, become distracted and meditate without keeping to measure. And when the best arises they make far too much of it; thinking, "No one else has this much," they meditate all night long and give up sleep, they meditate in the morning without doing their recitations or eating, and saying, "It will not make me ill," they meditate without keeping to measure. That makes the constitution become greatly disturbed and the object of focus is lost.

Therefore, knowing that the mind has come to abide, abandon those faults and make the number of sessions fewer than one meditated before: one session each in the evening and at dawn, and in the daytime one or two sessions, sometimes three; in a day and a night do not meditate more than five sessions. If one cannot manage that much, then in one day and one night, according to one's greater or lesser capacity, meditate two sessions in the day and two at night, that is four; or two in the day and one in the evening, that is three. Meditate without making more effort than the clarity one had before.

As for duration, meditate as appropriate, according to greater or lesser capacity, for as long as it takes to walk three, two or one bowshots at an ordinary pace, over one night, one day, one full day, two days, three, four, five, ten, fifteen or twenty. In short, it is not a matter of doing as much as one can possibly manage, however much the constitution is disturbed. Rather, meditate for as long as not even slight signs of an unwell constitution have arisen of their own accord: the body wearied, the mind exhausted, the urge to get up as soon as one meditates, or sighing, or the head gradually growing heavy, or swaying, or the body trembling, or light sleep at night, or food tasting bad, or frequent slips of speech, or the mind feeling hollow and light-headed, and the like. Then come out of it and rest; massage the body; and let the body's strength grow with food and drink in keeping with the Dharma. Until the constitution has been restored, meditate only such short sessions, at whatever time of day or night one feels like meditating. Once one's strength has grown, meditate four sessions in one full day and night: one long session each in the evening and at dawn, and in the daytime one in the morning and one in the afternoon. And do the accumulation of merit, the methods for treating the body, the preliminary of compassion, the aspirations and so forth, as stated above.

What is drawing out the growth of clarity? Having made it stable by the methods taught before, and meditating as above, if at first one has caught the object no better than the middling or the least, then even if no obstacle arises, one must draw out the growth of clarity. Therefore, in this way, meditate for as much as a month without too much intensity. Also do small tasks that are not contrary to the Dharma. Then, even if there is no opportunity in the daytime, meditate one short session each in the evening and at dawn, together with the preliminary and the aspiration.

Then, following the first manner of training, rely as far as one can on all the requisites for calm abiding and insight, and make requests to a patron for one's necessities. In a place endowed with as many good qualities of a dwelling as possible, secluded, delightful and so forth, stay there under a vow together with a few companions whose moral discipline is pure and whose view is in harmony with one's own. As it is said in the noble King of Samādhi:

Guard moral discipline as if it were a precious jewel;
always rely on companions who are in harmony.

And again in the same text it says:

Meditating on this emptiness, the king of samādhis,
rests upon the summit of pure moral discipline.
When one is ever in equipoise on the nature of phenomena,
childish beings, striving in unfitting ways, do not know it.

For the time being even activities in keeping with the Dharma are given up; what need to mention those not in keeping with it? Therefore, with a mind like that of someone sitting inside a house all ablaze who longs for cool water, give up everything and devote oneself to suchness. As it says in that same sūtra: "With a mind like one whose head and clothes are on fire, one should give up father, mother, sons, daughters, relatives, kin by blood and marriage, wife and so forth as if they were fire, give up even the pleasures of kingship as if they were a spittoon full of ashes, go forth, and dwell in the wilderness. Why is that? Because for such a youth even unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening is not hard to find; what need to mention a samādhi such as this?"

There, the constant remedies, food and drink, should not conflict with the Dharma, and one should strengthen the constitution with medicines for illness, with clothing and bedding sufficient for warmth, and so forth. For ten days, or half a month, or until one has reached the point of well-being, rest, be at ease and restore the constitution: do not meditate at night, and in the day meditate no more than one short session at a time, and let one's strength grow. Even if at first one had caught the object at the best measure, if one is threatened by an obstacle such as a sudden illness, then do as above, except for the passage from "the object, the middling and" up to "meditate one session each," and let one's strength grow.

Then set aside even that one short session of meditation each day, and with the wisdom that arises from reflection, reflect in three sessions, one in the morning and one in the afternoon of the day, and one in the evening at night, until a lasting certainty is produced. Think as follows: "Since the scriptural collections and the treatises of the Great Vehicle teach that by realizing the nature of the mind one realizes the nature of all phenomena, I shall examine my own mind closely."

Again, think as follows: "Whatever depends on causes and conditions is certainly impermanent, like the tip of a lamp's flame. This mind of mine, too, arises controlled by and dependent on desire for sense pleasures, ill will, discontent, torpor, sleep, excitement, regret, doubt, non-exertion, exertion and so forth, and so it is impermanent. Therefore the Blessed One also said, 'Alas, formations are impermanent.'

"Whatever arises and then ceases has parts that are moments, like a painful feeling. This present mind of mine, too, arises and ceases, and so it has parts. Whatever has parts is certainly not established as one, just as the central particle that makes up a compound, surrounded by ten particles, has parts by relation to those ten and so is not established as one. This mind of mine that has arisen and not yet ceased has a part that arises, a part that abides and a part that ceases, so it is not established as a single partless thing. Since it is not truly established as one, how could it be many? Therefore, being free of one and many, the present mind ultimately has no intrinsic nature whatsoever; it is like space, so one should not rely on it, and one should not abide in it."

As the noble Nāgārjuna said:

Just as an instant has an end,
so examine its beginning and its middle.
Because the instant has this threefold nature,
the world does not abide for even an instant.

Then one may think, "Does the future mind exist?" It too has no nature of its own established, and the evidence of a result is not ultimately established, so ultimately it has no intrinsic nature whatsoever. Then one may think, "Does the past mind exist ultimately?" If the cause of the past does not ultimately exist, what could be called past or future? Were it otherwise, it would follow that even the son of a barren woman could die. Therefore, if the present mind is not truly observed, how could the future and past, which depend on it, be observed?

As it says in the noble Heap of Jewels: "Kāśyapa, when mind is searched for thoroughly, it is not found. What is not found is not observed. What is not observed is not past, not future and not present."

And it says in the sūtra of the noble Ajātaśatru: "Then that emanated man went to where the Blessed One was, and having arrived, bowed his head at the Blessed One's feet and said to the Blessed One, 'Blessed One, I have taken the lives of my father and mother. May the Blessed One grant me protection. What should I do now?' Then the Blessed One gave his approval to that emanated man, saying, 'Excellent! Excellent, excellent! O man, you speak the truth, you speak correctly; you speak just as you have acted. Since you speak words of truth before the Tathāgata, you are indeed not deceiving. Yet, man, understand the cause of the mind. With which mind did you take the lives of your father and mother: with the past, or with the future, or with the present? The past mind is exhausted, departed, ceased, has become something else; since it does not abide even in an object, it cannot be designated. The future has not arrived; it is unborn, has not come about, has not truly come about, has not become, has not transformed, is signless, has not come about; it too cannot be designated. The present mind has no abiding; having arisen it perishes and certainly changes; it has not become a heap, it has not been gathered together. In it there is no designation of going or coming either. O man, mind does not enter into the body within, nor does it roam among outer objects, nor is it found apart from both. O man, mind is not blue, not yellow, not red, not white, not brown, and not the color of crystal.'"

"O man, mind is formless, indemonstrable, ungraspable and unobstructed; it is like an illusion, beyond example; it too cannot be designated. O man, mind does not desire, does not hate, is not deluded. O man, mind does not form anything; it has no agent, no feeling, no intention, no experiencer. O man, mind is clear by nature; it does not become afflicted, nor does it become purified. O man, mind is not in this life, nor in another life, nor is it apart from both. It is not there, nor is it elsewhere. It is like space, equal to the unequalled, incomparable, not something that becomes cognized. The wise should not cling to it, should not make it their own, should not rely on it, should not abide in it, should not think of it as self, and should not think of it as their own. O men, all phenomena are by nature without power. O men, for one who is convinced in this way, I do not speak of affliction or purification, of going to the lower realms or going to the higher realms. Why is that? Because the nature of mind does not become afflicted and does not become purified; it does not go anywhere, does not come, and does not stay."

Again, in that same sūtra: "The king said, 'Mañjuśrī, all my obscurations have been purified. Why is that? Because the nature of mind, primordially clear, cannot be afflicted by desire, or hatred, or delusion.' Mañjuśrī said, 'Great king, it is like this. In space, for example, motes, dust, smoke, darkness, thick haze and clouds may appear, yet space is not thereby afflicted by them. Just so, great king, although the latent tendencies of the views of self and mine, together with desire, hatred and delusion, may arise, they do not afflict the nature of mind. Great king, you should not be remorseful in that way. Great king, mind has not passed over from the prior limit; great king, mind will not pass over to the final limit; great king, mind does not abide in the present either. The wise realize all phenomena as not having passed over, not passing over, and not abiding; knowing this, they do not cling even to a view. Great king, when there is no clinging, that is purity.'"

Once great certainty has arisen in this way, through reasoning and scripture, over some days and nights, do not meditate at night, and in the daytime meditate for short periods with strong effort. In this way, having merely made the bodily adjustments, reflect that, just as mind is, so all phenomena too are free of all elaboration; and when one gently settles, without abiding even in that, tighten greatly and meditate on it simply as the object, as when first settling the mind. On the first day, meditate two sessions in the morning and two in the afternoon; on the second, three in the morning and three in the afternoon; on the third, four in the morning and four in the afternoon; on the fourth, five in the morning and five in the afternoon. From then on, until growth comes forth, do not meditate more than ten sessions a day.

By meditating in this way, there is the mind that arises with the first growth, then the second, the third and the fourth. At that stage, meditate in three sessions: two in the daytime and one in the evening.

The first is as follows. Because excitement has been cut off, it is utterly peaceful, and even the gentle movement felt earlier when looking at the breath is absent. Even when effort is relaxed, by the force of what went before, there is no sinking for as long as it takes to walk about five bowshots at an ordinary pace. By the force of having examined excitement earlier as very faulty, the hold is strong, and since calm abiding predominates, it is like the darkness of night. Although one does not know how the body is sitting, by the force of the earlier tightening, when one begins to rise from meditative absorption it feels rough and bound, as if it were hard to undo.

The second is as follows. Although at that first stage there was no scattering, it gradually becomes unclear, and the worry that one has sunk into sinking sometimes arises. Therefore, even at the time of rising, the thought arises, "I shall develop the portion of insight." And in the state of meditative equipoise too, by developing the strength of a clear mind, from time to time a vivid clarity arises, like the sun between clouds. By its force, even when meditating at night, one thinks, "Has dawn come?" and opens one's eyes to look. As soon as one thinks of rising, the body seems as if bound; yet instead of feeling rough it is calm and feels soft and supple. Some particulars are as before.

The third is as follows. That clarity arises constantly. Yet, like a lamp about to die that flickers of its own accord though there is no wind, it flickers, and doubt arises. Some particulars are as before.

The fourth is as follows. Because one regarded as a fault that flickering which came from time to time through excessive clarity before, the distinctive feature of the object is this: like a white lead vessel or a polished vessel of silver and iron, filled with clean water and standing unmoving, awareness and the knowable are without distinction, utterly open, unmoving and vast, and one does not see at all anything to grasp or to reject. Nor does one know that the mind which realizes this arises at such a point, or what it is focused on. Since it cannot be realized in any way at all, it is not different; it is of one taste. By the force of that, the mind is straight and stable, certain, without doubt, and vast. By the force of having no body, touch is soft and light. It is neither high nor low, and though it is vast there is no dizziness. Even if one looks down below the waist, or in whatever direction one looks, one does not even know that one is meditating. Therefore, even when one thinks of rising it is hard to rise, and when one rises by summoning one's strength, one feels as though the body had suddenly appeared. Some particulars are as before.

This fourth is, for the time being, called the meditative equipoise of mind of a beginner bodhisattva, by way of inference.

As it says in the noble Going to All Phenomena: "The god's son Excellent Mind asked, 'Blessed One, how is a bodhisattva called one in meditative equipoise?' The Blessed One said, 'Son of the gods, when a bodhisattva is free of all mental wishes and does not truly see any phenomenon whatever as something to grasp or to reject, then whatever he does not truly see, he sees as empty and unreal.'"

"In this way he is called a bodhisattva in meditative equipoise."

And in the Questions of Rāṣṭrapāla it says:

The ultimate is not differentiated;
in the buddhas its character is everywhere of one taste.
Those who conceive of differences in it
are deluded, abiding in pride.

And in the noble Purification of Karmic Obscurations it says: "Monks, all phenomena are abandoned, because from the very first they are not observed. Monks, all phenomena are nothing whatever, because they are void of anything at all. Monks, all phenomena are ungraspable, because they are like the body of space and like an empty fist."

And in the noble King of Samādhi it says:

Perceptions no longer roam about;
all wrong views are exhausted;
the mind too is thoroughly settled;
the steadfast one moves like space.

Teaching the accumulations, restoring the constitution, examining the mind;
meditating with strong effort in short and ever more sessions;
the four stages in the growth of its clarity;
and four scriptures teaching the fourth.

This is the summarizing verse. The method for drawing out the growth of clarity has now been taught.

What is drawing out the growth of long sessions? When the growth of clarity has come forth and has not declined, one should draw out the growth of lengthening the sessions. Why is that? Some, with the mind merely a little settled and the body merely a little at ease, meditate for a long time. Although they intend it, by meditating so, without the capacity to pass the day and night in it, the body aches, the mind becomes torpid, overcome by sleep and unclear, the heart-wind rises and enters, both wind and blood disorders arise, and they come to experience suffering. It is like a body with just enough breath to walk a little which intends to go up and down hill all day long: before reaching its destination, discomfort comes to the body and harm to the mind.

Therefore, right at the point when the growth of clarity has come forth, even though there is no discomfort in the constitution, set aside meditation for about two days and nights and stay at rest and ease. Then for a while let one's strength grow through lying down at night, growing used to it, and sleep, and train in meditating long sessions in the daytime only. On the first day, meditating in three sessions, meditate for about as long as it takes for one quarter of the day to pass. On the second day, meditating in three sessions, meditate for as long as one third of it passes. On the third day, meditating in three sessions, meditate for as long as a little more than half the day passes. On the fourth day, meditating in three sessions, meditate for as long as a little more than two thirds of the day passes. On the fifth day, meditating in three sessions, meditate for as long as a little more than three quarters of the day passes. On the sixth day, meditating in three sessions, pass the time in meditation for the whole day, except for eating food and changing posture. On the seventh day, apart from those, pass the whole day with two sessions: one session before the meal and one session after eating.

As for posture, spread two or three very thick, soft cushions here and there, and meditate moving to whichever is comfortable. On them, first meditate seated cross-legged. Then, after a little while, if torpor arises, or one is overcome by sleep, or discomfort arises in the body, stretch out one leg, or stretch out both, or recline on the right side, or recline on the left, or lie on one's back for a moment. In short, by changing posture now and then, harm to the body and harm to the mind will be removed. If, even having done so, torpor and sleep are strong, rise slowly, and in a place made into a walking path that is long and not too wide, walk back and forth two, three or four times, and then sit again. Through all of this do not let go of the object. However one changes posture, though the share of pliancy, such as the softness of the body, may be greater or lesser, it cannot be absent in all of them; so long as it is present, meditate in this way, but if it is absent one must rest.

If the mind is excited, if one does not engage with the appearances but engages with the mind, it will thereby become calm, just as when a form ceases its reflection ceases. Therefore one should not be too afraid of that. Relax, and think as follows: "The bodhisattvas, it is said, remained in meditative equipoise for forty thousand years without changing posture or changing their clothes, yet I cannot rest in equipoise even for a single day and night. From beginningless saṃsāra I have passed through countless bodies, and all to no purpose; and now too, with this life of little worth and short span, it will be just like this in the future. If even my own aim is not accomplished, how could the aim of others be accomplished? Therefore I shall remain in meditative equipoise for the whole day and abide in this posture."

In short, when after a long time the hold grows weak, and one would have to end the session and rise from meditative absorption, if there is still something left to give, meditate in that way. If one stays awake with nothing left to give, meditating brings no benefit and harm arises, so rest.

Once one is able in this way to pass the day with two sessions in the daytime, one should train at night. Leave the day in equanimity, sleep a little in the afternoon, at the time of wind, and at night again spread two or three comfortable cushions. If one keeps sinking, prepare a comfortable place for walking, make one's clothes and boots warm, walk slowly and turn back, keeping hold with mindfulness and vigilance, and meditate. With regard to the need to rest, it is as before. The particular point is to gauge the time by the stars, and to become able to pass the nights of eight days.

By training in this way, if one can leave either a day or a night in equanimity and pass the other one whole in meditation, and remain with ease of body and joy of mind, then by that much one has, for the time being, become practiced in long sessions.

The measure of lengthening the sessions,
training in day and night by turns,
the sequence, the posture and the attitude,
and reaching the full measure: these sum it up.

This is the summarizing verse.

Then, not doing meditation alone, but together with the preliminary and with the attitude on rising, in no more than two sessions in the day and two at night, join skillful means and wisdom as a pair, as taught before.

What is the section on misconceptions? It is twofold: misconceptions about skillful means and misconceptions about wisdom.

The misconception about skillful means is this. Here some, merely from knowing its benefits, generate the mind set on awakening and make aspirations, yet they rely on what was taught with reference to śrāvakas who accomplish only their own aim. The master Asaṅga said: "What is faith in the basis? Here some have faith in, and clear trust in, the Dharma and Vinaya taught by the Tathāgata; that is called having faith in the basis. 'The basis' refers to the Dharma and Vinaya taught by the Tathāgata, because it is the basis from which all the white qualities, mundane and supramundane, arise. Whatever faith in it comes about under the guidance of that is faith in the basis, because all the stains and turbidity of the afflictions are cleared away." Relying on this statement, they say and hold, "Great awakening is attained through the Vinaya alone." They themselves take no delight in hearing the Dharma of the Great Vehicle; even if they hear it, they do not reflect on it well; and they do not meditate on it either. They are hostile toward others, hostile even toward those who have entered the way of the Great Vehicle sūtras, and to those who train in the trainings of beginner bodhisattvas they say, "The Vinaya is itself the bodhisattva's training; that alone is enough. If you train in anything else, you will stand bound and charged under the Vinaya." With such views they will not attain great awakening, nor will they exhaust the outflows.

As it says in the Sūtra of the Seal of Entering the Certain and the Uncertain: "Which is the bodhisattva who travels as if by ox-cart? Suppose some man wished to cross as many world systems as there are atoms in five buddha fields, and mounting his ox-cart set out on the road. After a long time he reached a hundred leagues, and then a great maṇḍala of wind turned him around and drove him back eighty leagues. Would that man cross those world systems by ox-cart? Could he cross even a single world system in eons, even in inexpressible upon inexpressible eons?" He replied, "Blessed One, that is impossible; there is no possibility of it." The Blessed One said, "Just so, if someone generates the mind set on awakening and yet does not uphold the Great Vehicle, does not read it, does not recite it and does not attend to it properly, but relies on followers of the Śrāvaka Vehicle, teaches together with them, reads and recites the Śrāvaka Vehicle, investigates it and masters it, and also causes others to read it and so on up to mastering it, then whatever eye of wisdom he has is drawn back from the path of unsurpassed wisdom and turns back. Whatever faculty of wisdom and eye of wisdom that bodhisattva had, arising from cultivating the mind of awakening, becomes dull and declines. This is the bodhisattva who travels as if by ox-cart."

Therefore, the well-spoken Dharma and Vinaya is, among the three trainings distinguished by what one undertakes, the training in higher moral discipline, and among the three kinds of restraint it is the moral discipline of restraint, so it must be practiced with great respect. Yet it is not enough by itself. As it says in the noble Cloud of Jewels: "Son of good family, how are bodhisattvas restrained by the restraint of the bodhisattva training? Here a bodhisattva conducts himself in this way: 'By the prātimokṣa restraint alone I cannot fully awaken to unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. I shall train in whatever conduct of bodhisattvas and whatever bodhisattva precepts the Tathāgata has laid down in these sūtras.'"

The misconception about wisdom is also twofold: general and particular. The general one is this. Here some put out the fire of the white side with the wind and water of emptiness, saying: "The abandoning of the ten non-virtues and the practice of the ten virtues were taught with reference to śrāvakas who cling to things. When one knows the profound meaning of the Great Vehicle, that all phenomena lack intrinsic nature, then whatever one does is no fault. Going forth, too, does not mean merely shaving the head; what is needed is a mind that is wide open. Therefore, if one has realization, even killing and the like do not become obscurations, and if one has no realization, even the antidotes become obscurations. For in the noble Purification of Karmic Obscurations it says, from 'Desire is an obscuration' up to 'Wisdom is an obscuration.'" Those who say this train in setting the antidotes aside without having overcome the discordant factors in themselves, and so they do the karma of the lower realms alone; and those who train after them also enter into non-virtue and the like. As the master Nāgārjuna said in the Root Verses on the Middle Way:

When emptiness is wrongly viewed,
those of little wisdom are ruined,
like a snake that is wrongly seized,
or a secret mantra wrongly practiced.

And again in the Sixty Verses on Reasoning:

Not knowing the meaning of utter seclusion,
those who engage in mere hearing
and do not make merit:
those base persons are lost.

But if someone says, "I have great wisdom and I also make merit, so I need not be wary of afflictions," that too is unreasonable. For in the noble Ten Grounds it says that the bodhisattva of the second ground is established in pure moral discipline and dwells with vigilance toward even subtle transgressions. So to say, "I have greater wisdom than one who abides on the grounds," is pretension beyond measure. Therefore, conventionally, if afflictions increase even when left in equanimity without an antidote arising, what need is there to mention embracing them? For this reason, one who indulges the afflictions at will and is devoid of training is not a bodhisattva who has gone forth. As it says in the noble Play of Mañjuśrī: "Mañjuśrī said, 'Girl, going forth depends on moral discipline; for one of corrupt discipline there is no going forth at all.'" And in that same sūtra it says: "One who has himself rightly taken up the training and moral discipline, and who also rightly joins beings of corrupt discipline to the restraint of moral discipline and to ascetic vows, is a bodhisattva who has gone forth."

Therefore, one who understands emptiness and yet is defeated by the afflictions is not a bodhisattva. As that same sūtra says: "The girl said, 'Mañjuśrī, it is like this. For example, one who has been beaten by his foes is not called a hero. Mañjuśrī, in the same way, one who is beaten by the afflictions is also not called a bodhisattva.'"

As for the benefit of having great wisdom and meditating after realizing emptiness, the Moon Lamp Sūtra says:
He does not abandon the other trainings of the Sugatas;
That hero never falls under the power of women,
and so on.

The noble Sūtra on the Course of All Phenomena also says: "For example, what dwells together with darkness is not a lamp. In the same way, what dwells together with the afflictions is not the wisdom of a bodhisattva."

The noble Crown Jewel also says: "One should not be separated from the yoga of emptiness, from generosity up to wisdom."

The Compendium of Trainings also says: "Even having meditated on the perfection of wisdom, one should be skilled against the enemy, the afflictions, and skilled in means; one should not be like someone who is brave only in his own house."

Therefore it is fitting for a bodhisattva to say of himself, "Though I have great wisdom, I am defeated by the afflictions, and having little faith, I am lax in the training"; but it is not fitting to draw others onto a wrong path and to wound the teaching.

Holding that moral discipline alone suffices,
And realizing only one side through wisdom:
This brief statement of their refutation
Is the summarizing verse.

Now, the particular misconceptions are of two kinds: misconception about the view and misconception about meditation.

As for misconception about the view, some here say: "What are called the two truths are not established in reality. For whatever is unborn is nondual, like space. The two truths, too, are unborn from the very beginning and completely pure, and so they are nondual." To this we reply: If you are proving that they are nondual ultimately, you are proving what is already established. But if you are proving it in general, your reason is not established, for it contradicts direct perception. One should say to them: "Since the unborn has no suffering, if you were struck with a weapon, would you bear it?"

Some say: "There is no ultimate truth; there is only conventional truth alone." To this we reply: Do you say this in keeping with the thinking of childish beings blinded by ignorance, or in keeping with the abiding nature of things, that is, also with the thinking of the wise? On the first alternative, the two are not established. For the ultimate truth is not established in the mind of such a person, since he has not heard, reflected on, or meditated on the teaching that shows it. Since it is not established, there arises only the thought, "Form and so on are real, they are true." So it is impossible for him to establish "this is false," or "it is called conventional because it obscures," or "it is called conventional because it is invalidated by reasoning." If you say it is the latter alternative, we reply: If one does not know that by which it obscures, or that in which the obscuring lies, and if its being so posited, or its being invalidated by reasoning, is not established, how could it be called conventional? And if you say, "Because it is established as invalidated by reasoning, it is established as conventional truth," then it is known to be conventional truth because that very ultimate truth has already been established first, and so you have accepted both.

Some say: "The intention of the noble Nāgārjuna is that things are not established ultimately; and, going much further, he holds that even conventionally there is no object apart from the name." To this we reply: He said, "There is no quarrel about names." Your view contradicts direct perception: if someone said, "A barren woman's son is here," it would follow absurdly that striking him with a weapon and striking you with a weapon would be alike, since neither would cause harm. The master did not hold this. The Sixty Verses on Reasoning also says: For those skilled in cause and effect, Nonexistence, the ground from which All faults arise, has been turned back. And he said, "Since you have stopped even my affliction of striving to refute it, you have acted as my friend and set it aside."

As for the passage in the noble Sūtra on the Course of All Phenomena where the bodhisattva Unstriving says to the bodhisattva Vikrāntamati, "All phenomena are mere names and mere conventions," and so on, this too is spoken conventionally, as synonyms for names, or as one thing explaining another; it is not a teaching about the ultimate.

Some say: "The two truths are established in reality. Conventional truth is what is invalidated by reasoning, while ultimate truth is what is established by reasoning: a single permanent thing that abides as the nature of reality of all phenomena, a nature turned away from all conceptual elaboration. As the Distinction of the Two Truths says: Because its nature follows reasoning, It is the dharmakāya of the protectors. And the noble Ten Grounds says: 'Whether tathāgatas appear or do not appear, this sphere of reality (dharmadhātu) simply abides: that is, the emptiness of all phenomena and the non-apprehension of all phenomena.' And the noble Cloud of Jewels says: 'Son of good family, if there were no ultimate, the practice of pure conduct would be pointless.'"

To this we reply: As for the words "Because its nature follows reasoning," and so on, that very text, answering the thought "Why is it a nature that abides through reasoning?", says, "because that very nature accords with reasoning." Yet it is contradictory for all phenomena to be seen by reasoning ultimately. It was said in that way when examined with respect to mere non-seeing; but there is no ground for positing that reasoning as established ultimately. As the noble Teaching of the Two Truths says: "Son of the gods, that ultimate truth transcends even the domain of the wisdom of the Omniscient One." The meaning is: leave others aside, there is no basis even for that wisdom to take it as an object. And as for "permanent, abiding from the very beginning," it does not mean that form and so on exist ultimately as long as no tathāgata has appeared in the world, and cease to exist once one has appeared. Rather, it was said in that way because, from the very moment any thing arises, it arises in the manner of not being established ultimately.

If it were not so, of what would that nature of reality, permanent from the very beginning, be the nature, if one did not accept that the two, the nature that was there before and the newly arisen phenomenon that bears it, are one in their being and their continuance?

As for the words "pure conduct would be pointless": they were spoken to childish beings who imagine that there is a self of persons and a self of phenomena and grasp it as true, with only this in mind, that no self is established in persons or phenomena. The meaning is: "It is not true that a self exists, for that contradicts reasoning. If its existence were true, then since all childish beings see it, they would be free of error, and so striving now to attain the pure state through nirvāṇa would be pointless." It was not said in the sense that "if the ultimate were not established as an object of reasoning, it would be pointless."

As for "abiding as the nature of reality of all phenomena," this too was said because, just as one phenomenon is free from conceptual elaboration, so the others too are free from elaboration. If there were some single thing pervading all phenomena, it would be like the non-Buddhists (tīrthikas) asserting a self that pervades everything, and it would be refuted by the verse: Since it mixes with the various directions, How could the pervader be one alone?

As for "a nature turned away from all conceptual elaboration," that itself is an elaboration. If it could be expressed in that way, why did the noble Vimalakīrti not teach it? Therefore it is impossible that there should be, ultimately, a single ultimate established by reasoning.

Then how is it? For this reason the Blessed One, who realized things just as they are, said in the noble Sūtra of the Meeting of Father and Son: "The Tathāgata has fully understood the two, the conventional and the ultimate; and what is to be known, too, is exhausted in these, the conventional and the ultimate." And the noble Nāgārjuna, who was prophesied as one who would realize the intention of the Tathāgata just as it is, said in the Root Verses on the Middle Way, called Wisdom: The Dharma taught by the buddhas Rests perfectly upon two truths: The world's conventional truth And the truth of the supreme meaning. Thus, at the time of settling the matter, things are established, and since the wise too understand them in that way, they speak conventionally of two. At the time of settling the matter, it is neither established nor unestablished; it cannot be asserted in any way at all. This is what was taught.

Some say: "Since the nature of reality and the phenomenon that bears it have one identity, the nature of reality too is seen directly, just as the phenomenon, an object of the senses, is seen directly. As the Vārttika says: The object's own nature is one; Since its very self is directly perceived, What is left for the means of valid cognition to examine? What other, unseen part is there?" To this we reply: Then it would follow that all ordinary childish beings see the truth. Suppose you say: "Rather, since a certain cognition has not arisen in them, it is as if they did not see it. As the Ornament of the Middle Way also says: 'If the voidness of the superimposed aspects of things is called emptiness, then, since emptiness is a nature obscured by the superimposed aspects, childish beings do not become certain of it even while they apprehend the nature of things. Therefore, although it is apprehended, it is just like what is not apprehended, since only what is ascertained is fit to be named conventionally; so this does not count as something meeting the conditions for apprehension. Thus it is a matter of invalidation by direct perception and the rest. This is what is shown.'" If you say this, then for that very reason, do not speak conventionally of eye consciousness as seeing the nature of reality. As the Distinction of the Two Truths says: "For conventional expression is preceded by certainty."

If one objects, "Then this contradicts the intention of the master Dharmakīrti," it does not. The master spoke with impermanence in mind, and since that is a thing, there is no contradiction. But emptiness is not a thing, and so it cannot be seen by sensory direct perception. As the Distinction of the Two Truths says: Just this, as it appears, is conventional; The other is the other. Or else, since he had non-difference in mind, there is no contradiction here either.

On this, some say: "'Non-different' and 'one' do not differ in meaning. If that were so, then by directly seeing the nature of reality one would directly see the suchness of everything; the phenomenon would be true, like the sphere of reality; it would not be afflicted; and the sphere of reality, like the phenomenon, would be conditioned and afflicted. Someone may say that since one does not accept oneness, none of these faults follow. But then the mere selflessness of formations would not be the defining mark of the ultimate, and those bound by the signs of formations would not be overcome by the yogin, since he would conceive of the sphere of reality as different and other. Therefore, like a conch and its whiteness, they cannot be said to be one or different, and so their relation is one that cannot be expressed as the same or other."

To this we reply: Stating it as the same or as other incurs faults; but a relation within a single establishment, posited by stating it as neither of the two, is not faultless either. Those faults of oneness and difference apply where phenomena become things. The nature of reality, free of elaboration, is neither faulty nor faultless in being one with the phenomenon, and neither faulty nor faultless in being different from it. Nor is there any single thing called "the inexpressible," truly established, to be posited as related to the phenomenon and accepted as a third kind of relation. For the earlier masters, Dharmakīrti and others, taught that there is no relation other than the relation of identity and the relation of arising from something.

Now you may think: "But the master Kamalaśīla, in the Illumination of the Middle Way, taught that it cannot be expressed as the same or other." That is true. But there, simply because persons and phenomena themselves are not established as persons and phenomena by correct cognition, those very persons and phenomena are designated as the nature of reality, and so to just that the name "relation of identity" is given; and since it cannot be expressed as the same or other, it is taught to be established as nothing at all.

Therefore the nature of reality is simply the phenomenon itself being free from conceptual elaboration; and that too is obstructed by superimposition, so ordinary childish beings do not see it directly. As the Ornament of the Middle Way says: It is not so: because, in a heavy continuum without beginning, Superimposition onto things has held sway, Therefore it does not come to be Realized directly by all living beings.

The great lords of yoga, from the attainment of the first ground onward, and the tathāgatas realize it clearly. Ordinary beings only cut off superimpositions by means of reasons; they do not make it directly perceived. As the same text says: Cutting off superimpositions about it Through reasons that bring understanding, Those who infer infer it; The lords of yoga see it directly and clearly.

Misconceptions about the two truths,
Misconceptions about seeing the nature of reality,
And misconceptions about the relation
Between property and property-bearer: their refutation.
This is the summarizing verse.

Some say: "When ordinary beings cut off superimpositions, they know through reasons, in this way: 'Whatever is free of being one or many is ultimately without intrinsic nature; and these things spoken of by ourselves and others are also free of being one or many.' Merely by stating that they are free of being one or many, the thing to be proven comes to be realized." To this we reply: It may be right that those of sharp faculties who are trained in the formal argument realize it so. But if this is the kind of case in which one must state a formal argument with a similar instance and so on, then, since it is not certain whether that argument establishes the imputed nature or the dependent nature, it will appear like a pseudo-reason that is inconclusive by being common to both.

Suppose they say: "There is no fault, since we state the positive concomitance in general and say, 'like a reflection, for example.'" To this we reply: Has the relation been established in that similar example or not? If it has not been established, citing the example is pointless. If they say it has been established, we reply: Have you, applying a word of simple negation, established the relation between the reason, 'ultimately free of one and many,' and the thing to be proven, 'ultimately without intrinsic nature'? Or have you, applying a word of implicative negation, established the relation with respect to some conventional truth?

On the first alternative, we say: Is that relation established by direct perception, or by inference? If they say it is established by direct perception, then, since the nature of reality would be seen directly, it would follow absurdly that both the proponent and the opponent are noble ones. In that case, following the reason for the property to be proven would be pointless. Therefore, since it is impossible for ordinary beings to see the nature of reality directly, to assert something impossible is to undermine oneself. So that example appears as a pseudo-example that is not established; and since it is asserted to be present in the subject of the proof, the reason's own nature is not established, and it becomes a pseudo-reason that is unestablished.

That relation is contradicted by direct perception: since it always appears as a reflection, it is impossible for it not to be seen as a reflection. It is also refuted by inference: whatever, though asserted, is not seen cannot be named conventionally, like a place devoid of a pot; and this ultimate truth, the basis of the dispute, is not seen directly. This is the non-apprehension of the pervader. And the reason "because a reflection appears" is the apprehension of something pervaded by what is contradictory.

It is also refuted by common knowledge. If one asks someone who knows the conventions of language and whose mind has not been altered by philosophical tenets, "What is in the mirror?", he will say, "There is something just like a face"; it is impossible that he would say, "I see nothing at all." It also contradicts your own words: do you call a reflection "seeing the ultimate"? It is also refuted by scripture. In the noble Sūtra Requested by Druma, King of the Kinnaras, the Blessed One said: Just as, for example, in a perfectly pure Mirror disk, empty of intrinsic nature, A reflection appears, in that same way, Druma, understand phenomena. He did not say that the reflection does not appear. Therefore it cannot be established by direct perception.

If they say it is established by inference, that too is not right, for such an example is impossible. Even if it were possible, since the reason is not established by direct perception, the relation would not be established directly; so it too would have to be established by some inference, and the faults of an infinite regress of examples and an infinite regress of reasons would follow.

Suppose they say: "That fault does not follow. In the Ornament of the Middle Way, the non-Buddhists called the Victory Reckoners say that the face itself is perceived there and is conventionally called a reflection; the Vaibhāṣikas say that another form has arisen there; the Sautrāntikas say that it is only consciousness appearing in that way; and the example is proven to be without intrinsic nature by means of inference. Therefore there is no infinite regress of examples, and the ultimate is determined in an example agreed upon by all." To this we reply: There too, against those who did not hold it to be so while their minds were not yet altered by tenets, but who later, their minds corrupted by tenets, say, "It is a face that has no function," it merely refutes them by saying, "It is not a face capable of performing a function"; it does not prove that the reflection is ultimately without intrinsic nature. Therefore there is no fault of an infinite regress of reasons and examples.

Thus in the first argument, intending to refute that it has the nature of something capable of performing a function, he said: "The formal argument is this: whatever consciousness appears with characteristics discordant with something, that thing is not its object; just as auditory cognition appears with characteristics discordant with form. The cognition of a reflection, too, appears with characteristics discordant with the face and so on as they actually are as objects; so this is the apprehension of what is contradictory to the pervader," and so forth. That refutes its being a form that performs a function; it does not say there that the reflection is ultimately nonexistent. In the second argument, he spoke with regard to refuting that a material thing capable of performing a function is present in the same place, saying: "The formal argument is this: whatever things are embodied are by nature such that they abide in mutually exclusive places, like a pot and so on."

"These things that are the subject of debate are also embodied, so this is a reason based on intrinsic nature (svabhāvahetu)," he said. There too, he refuted that forms which perform functions can abide together in the same place; he did not thereby prove that the reflection is ultimately without intrinsic nature.

To clarify the third argument, he said this: "Although the opponent accepts that these reflections and so on exist substantially, in the nature of consciousness, those reflections and so on are not what is intended as the example. What is, then? Whatever nature appears in the cognition of reflections and so on. And because that is false, the example is indeed established." There too, he refuted a form imputed as external that is capable of performing a function; he did not thereby prove that the reflection itself is also without intrinsic nature, because of the contradiction with direct perception. If you say that a single valid cognition that invalidates the opposite establishes that it is ultimately without intrinsic nature, then it would follow that every person who knows the conventions and has studied the treatises on valid cognition correctly apprehends the face in a reflection. Therefore, in the concordant example, the relation between the reason taken as ultimate and the probandum taken as ultimate is not established by direct perception or by inference, as was stated where this was discussed.

Suppose you say: We construe it with the wording of an affirming negation (paryudāsa), and since the relation is established by direct perception in that very reflection, which is a conventional truth, we make it the example. To that I reply: In that case, establishing the relation serves no purpose. For in the subject of the proof one must understand the two as related, the reason, "ultimately free from one and many," and the probandum, "ultimately without intrinsic nature," whereas the relation has been established in a conventional appearance alone, which does not accord with that. So the ultimate relation has not been ascertained beforehand, and since the conventional one was established from the outset, this is proving what is already established. Moreover, since the reason applies to the conventional truth, which is the discordant class with respect to the ultimate absence of intrinsic nature, it becomes a contradictory pseudo-reason.

If perchance you say, "We establish the relation in the conventional truth alone," even so there is the fault that establishing the relation serves no purpose, and the fault that the reason is an uncommon inconclusive one, or else a contradictory pseudo-reason. For in the phenomena under proof, the reason "ultimately free from one and many" has entered, whereas in the concordant example, the conventional "free from one and many" has entered.

Suppose you say: Be that as it may, the Ornament of the Middle Way says:

These things spoken of by ourselves and others,
Since in reality they are free
From a nature that is one or many,
Lack intrinsic nature, like a reflection.

Since it says this, "in reality" must refer to the ultimate, and therefore this is a non-affirming negation (prasajyapratiṣedha).

To that I reply: There are also cases in which the word "ultimate" does not enter into the reason, for the Entering into Reality says:

All these that abide outside and within
Are in reality without intrinsic nature,
Because they are free from the very nature
Of one and of many, like a reflection.

"Whatever is free from one and many is ultimately without intrinsic nature, like a reflection, for example. All these things abiding outside and within are also free from the nature of one and many, and so they are without intrinsic nature." And even where the word "in reality" is present, it is construed as a word of affirming negation, as the commentary on that same Ornament of the Middle Way says: "The formal argument is like this: Whatever things are not of the nature of one or many are ultimately without intrinsic nature, like a reflection, for example. The statement, 'The things spoken of by ourselves and others are also not of the nature of one or many,' is a non-apprehension of the pervader, because intrinsic nature is pervaded by oneness and manyness."

Thus, whatever has not had its relation established by direct perception and inference cannot be designated "ascertained"; just as, for example, when the relation of fire and smoke is ascertained, one does not wish to designate it "ascertained in ultimate truth." And this positing of a relation in the ultimate that is the subject of debate is also not established by direct perception and inference: this is a non-apprehension of the pervader. Whatever relation is discordant in character with the property to be proven, ascertaining it serves no purpose for understanding the property of the subject; just as, for example, ascertaining the connection between being produced and being impermanent serves no purpose for understanding a fire on the far side. And this so-called "ascertaining the relation in the conventional truth alone, which is the subject of debate" is also discordant in character with the property of the subject: this is a non-apprehension of the pervader. Therefore it is not held that the relation is to be ascertained in a particular, whether the ultimate truth or the conventional truth.

If you say, "In that case, since there is nothing not included in the two truths, the relation will not be established," that is true. Nevertheless, one must accept what is called a permanent universal, in the case of dissimilar kinds and the like, and what is called "abiding each in its own nature," in the case of similar kinds and the like. It stands like this: freedom from one and many, which excludes one and many, and absence of intrinsic nature, which excludes intrinsic nature, these two apply to the conventional truth and also apply to the ultimate truth; they apply in common.

If you ask, "Then do you establish the relation in that universal?" I reply: Not that either. "Then how do you establish it?" Like this. When one says, "Whatever things are not of the nature of one or many," that states the reason in general. When one says, "are ultimately without intrinsic nature," that states that the general probandum follows the general reason. For worldly people blinded by ignorance take as ultimate this nature capable of performing functions, which by its defining characteristics is the correct conventional and is the object of view, meditation, and experience; whereas in the philosophical systems, what is established by reasoning is called ultimate. So it shows that it follows in both cases. Saying, "like a reflection, for example," shows that universal applied to a particular, the conventional truth. Therefore it shows that the reflection itself, because it is neither one nor many as a face capable of performing a function, does not have the nature of a face capable of performing a function.

The respondent understands it in that way through a single nonconceptual cognition, a mistaken direct perception. Thus, once that universal together with its particular has been established in the example, then, in order to show that the reason is present in the subject of the proof, it says, "The things spoken of by ourselves and others, too." This shows that the subject, which unlike a reflection is capable of performing functions, is precisely this that worldly people hold to be real. Saying "not of the nature of one or many" shows that the reason is present in precisely this subject of the proof, the dependent nature capable of performing functions, which is held to be ultimate. Therefore, the proponent states that just that much, the general "not ultimately one or many" that has entered in particular into the reflection, is present in all the subjects of the proof as well, and the respondent too understands that just that much is present. Accordingly, the fifth fascicle also says, "The concordant example accords in meaning with the universal of the property to be proven."

With just that much in mind, it was said that the subject, the reason, and so on are posited as commonly appearing; it was also said:

Whatever aspect appears
To the cognition of both disputants,
Relying on just that much,
The subject, the property, and so on are understood.

And it was said:

This relation of what is proven and what proves,
Setting aside subjects with particulars
Produced by the treatises, applies correctly
And without exception to the things
That are known to all, from the learned
Down to women and children.

As for that reason in a particular such as a pot, to a learned respondent the pot appears in direct perception as having ten parts, and he understands that it is not one; whereas those who are not learned, such as those whose minds are corrupted through familiarity with wrong treatises, establish it by way of inference. Having examined it in this way and posited it in a commonly appearing subject, one must accept it. If someone in whom certainty has not arisen through mere seeing is in doubt, then, applying the mind intently from the coarse pot all the way down to the subtlest particle, and examining it in terms of ten, six, or four directional parts, one generates certainty that it is free from oneness. If one doubts even that, one looks with the mind intently applied, and, dividing it into ten directional parts, one does not see it as one. Since that does not appear, there is no cause to suspect that it is many partless things; and since that is not seen, the reason "free from one and many" is established by way of inference. What is called "ultimate one and many" and what is called "partless one and many" are not different in meaning. So long as one has not generated the cognition that does not see it, the conventional cognition that it is free from one and many does not arise in oneself, nor does one express it to others with the convention of words; for it has already been shown that this is preceded by certainty.

Some say about this: In that case, the reason comes to be posited in the ultimate. Therefore it contradicts the principle, "Setting aside philosophical tenets, posit things in just the aspect that appears." And because a part of the thesis, not different from the absence of intrinsic nature, has been taken as the reason, either it is not established, or, if it is established, following the reason becomes pointless. There is this fault.

To that I reply: "Posit things in just the aspect that appears" also refers to the time of inference for others, when one applies the words to what appears commonly. But in inference for oneself, where the subject appears in direct perception, certainty that the reason too is free from one and many arises through direct perception and inference. There are also cases where the subject and the reason are posited in what does not appear commonly, such as the subtlest particle. If you say, "Since it abides in things, it appears commonly," that is not reasonable:

In a single mote of sunlight
There are, of the subtlest particles, eight hundred thousand,
And twenty thousand, and three thousand,
And five hundred and forty-three.

Is a part such as that something that appears commonly? Therefore, as for the purpose, there is the purpose of clearing up the yogin's understanding and of drawing out the thorn of doubt; and the purpose has in view the extremely subtle fragments, broken down by the mind, which have not become directly perceptible.

This charge of "a part of the thesis" is also no fault, for he says: "In this reason there is not even the fault of being a part of the thesis, because by means of an object that is established, what is proven is the cognition that has it as object, together with words and conventional usage; just as in the example, 'This is an ox, because it has the nature of a collection of dewlap and so on.' Although it has already been shown, by the arguments stated, that things are free from the nature of one and many, there are those who, because their clinging to things, grown strong through errors handed down one after another from beginningless saṃsāra, is hard to abandon, and because they have not become familiar with it through proper attention, do not with certainty think of or express the absence of intrinsic nature that in reality pervades all things, and who do not apply the convention out of a wish to generate certainty in others as well. For them, it is like saying of something that has been established in that way as the very object of cognition, words, and conventions, and so has come to have the characteristics of the observable, 'Since it is not observed, it does not exist.'" So he says; but he does not say, "The reason, being an affirming negation, is something that appears, while the probandum is a non-affirming negation, so there is no fault." If it were otherwise, how could they even be of a single nature?

If you ask, "Then, in the concordant example, it is construed as a word of affirming negation, and since the property of the subject is stated in accordance with that, there is no difference; so by what is it understood?" In the reflection, what is proven is freedom from one and many as something capable of performing a function; whereas in the subject of the proof, it is freedom from that very object of negation; the imputed nature has already been refuted by others. Therefore this is what is called a formal argument that is condensed and understood by implication; such a name is given in the Compendium of the Great Vehicle. The implication is the implication of the relation. By saying, "are ultimately without intrinsic nature," the property to be proven has been shown. Moreover, it is from the standpoint of correct cognition that this is without intrinsic nature, not from the standpoint of conventional cognition.

On this point some say: What is called intrinsic nature refers to what is permanent, not produced by causes and conditions, not undermined by reasoning, and not fabricated by the mind. For the noble Nāgārjuna said:

If something existed by intrinsic nature,
It would not become nonexistent;
For intrinsic nature to become otherwise
Is never tenable.

How could intrinsic natures
Come to be called made?
Intrinsic nature is not made,
Nor does it depend on anything else.

Since he said this and so forth, such a thing does not exist, but dependent origination does appear.

To that I reply: That is true. But then what is reasoning for? If you say it is correct cognition, then when you are asked, "Does it appear to that?" and you answer, "It appears," that very thing, not undermined by reasoning and established, is what is called the ultimate; it is not the conventional. But suppose you say, "At the time one comes to understand the probandum, it appears, since this is an inference of a universal." The thesis, the position, the probandum, and what is to be inferred are one and the same. The thesis and so on are twofold, "ultimately without intrinsic nature" and "conventionally appearing," but it is not that the two appear to a single cognition. If the subject appears, how could one speak of the nature of reality? With impermanence there is no contradiction, since impermanence is a thing. If you think, "If the subject does not exist, what is that nature of reality?": while one is merely ascertaining it, one applies the designation, "This nature of reality is free from elaboration," or "It is without intrinsic nature"; but once it has been ascertained and the inferential cognition has just arisen, one does not apprehend it as the nature of reality, so there is no fault. Still, there is no contradiction in calling it a universal, in that it focuses on a property, that very subject as distinguished by its freedom from elaboration. Therefore it is that very conventional which correct cognition realizes to be without intrinsic nature; otherwise, how would it be established that this is conventional?

Accordingly, the Sūtra of Mañjuśrī's Abiding says:

Whatever phenomena of the past, the future,
And the present are spoken of
Are not so in fact; they are mere conventions.

By that it says that since they are not ultimately established, they are merely conventional. By saying, "They are not characterized as one or as manifold," it says that ultimately that very conventional is free from one and many. Therefore it says that it is without intrinsic nature.

If you say, "Then, having negated existence, one apprehends nonexistence," it is said:

Whatever is conceived as lacking characteristics,
That very thing becomes a characteristic.

Thus it shows that what is called nonexistence is not established as anything at all. The Noble Sūtra on the Course of All Phenomena also says: "The bodhisattva Unstriving said to the bodhisattva Vikrāntamati: 'Here, if one asks how emptiness is to be understood through selflessness: emptiness is neither an entity nor a non-entity. If emptiness were an entity, emptiness would become conditioned and impermanent. And if emptiness were a non-entity, the empty would become non-empty. Therefore emptiness is not an entity, nor is it a non-entity. In this way, emptiness is to be understood through selflessness.'"

To one who wonders, "Then why does he not say that what is not established as anything at all is nonexistent?" the noble Nāgārjuna said:

This is the stopping of that very thing;
It is not to be grasped as nonexistence;
Just as, when one teaches that something is not white,
One has not said that it is black.

Misconceptions about the relation, and likewise
Misconceptions about the reason for the probandum,
And misconceptions about the probandum itself,
With their refutation, have been taught in brief.

This is the summarizing verse.

Now, misconceptions about meditation are of two kinds: concerning the meditation, and concerning its signs. The first has two parts: the object and the cognition. As for the object, some say, "The present is the time of the cause; one should engage in study and reflection, and we do not hold that one should meditate. It would be like sowing seeds on a rock, for example." To that I reply: For that very reason, since one must apply oneself earnestly to the special cause, one meditates. If you say, "But my mind will not stay settled," that alone is no reason to take others to be like you. A thoughtful person can infer it from trustworthy people of earlier times who speak of their experience. The Noble King of Samādhi also says:

With reference to the final five hundred years:
At that time, those who uphold samādhi
Will appear, not fewer than five hundred.

Some say, "One meditates only on the two, the property and the subject." To that I reply: That is merely knowing impermanence; it is not understanding freedom from elaboration at all. So let it be set aside for now; such a person is not a vessel for the profound.

Some say, "One meditates only on the intrinsic defining character of the nature of reality, and therefore it grows ever clearer, whereas in the aspect of a universal there is no clarity"; and they say there is no contradiction in its having an aspect. To that I reply: That is merely knowing impermanence. In that case, it would follow that one sees the truth. If they say, "That is not equal in its qualities," then for that very reason, do not assert it! That even the aspect of a universal grows ever clearer is stated in scripture too: it says that by placing in the mind the form of the Tathāgata one has heard of, it comes to be like seeing it. And by reasoning too, one can infer it from the words of trustworthy people whose moral discipline is pure. But suppose you say, "The Sixty Verses on Reasoning states: 'It is not tenable that what is established through meditative practice on the aspect of a general characteristic should have a specific characteristic as its object, because that would be absurdly overextended. Such a claim is incoherent.'" To that I reply: That fault was stated against the proponents of real entities, not against us. For in their view the specific characteristic, which is substantially established, and the universal of a thing, which is not substantially established, are two different things, so it would follow that by meditating on one thing one attains another.

Some say, "One meditates only on the aspect of an object universal." To that I reply: What sort of aspect is it? If they say, "One posits as existing just that much, the thought 'it is not established as anything at all,' and that is the aspect of the nature of reality," that is not the aspect of the nature of reality, because it is an elaboration.

Some say, "There is no such thing at all as what is called ultimate truth; one meditates on conventional dependent origination just as it is." To that I reply: Then why is it called conventional? If you say, "It is called conventional because it is not established when analyzed by reasoning," then the ultimate is not established either, for that mere non-establishment of persons and phenomena by reasoning is not itself the ultimate. But if you say, "Reasoning does not apply at all, and the selflessness of persons and phenomena is not established at all," then do not even call it conventional! You are no different from one who keeps company with the childish and has not heard the Dharma. There is no need to meditate on that. Accordingly, the Chapter of Candragarbha says: "The ultimate truth is unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening; it is not shared with any śrāvakas or pratyekabuddhas."

"Therefore, no conventional truth whatsoever can accomplish the supreme accumulation of roots of virtue for unsurpassed, perfect, complete awakening. Candragarbha, it is like this: for example, no spark whatsoever can dry up the great, deep ocean. Candragarbha, in the same way, no conventional truth whatsoever can dry up the ocean of one's own afflictions, let alone those of other sentient beings." So it says.

Some say, "The ultimate truth is not meditated on at all. For the Teaching of the Noble Akṣayamati says, 'If in the ultimate truth there is not even any movement of mind,' and so we do not hold that it is meditated on by the mind." To that I reply: Even if that holds true of mind in these senses, "mind" because it is variegated, "thinking" because it thinks of objects, and "mind" because it is the opposite of matter, one still cannot say that what has arisen in the aspect of whatever is a thing's nature does not realize it. For it is said, "Nonconceptual wisdom, undifferentiated from what it knows, sees all phenomena like the middle of the sky"; and it is said, "Certainty about an object through the force of a reason is not deceptive."

Some say, "Having negated existence, one meditates on nonexistence." To that I reply: Is that a property of the aggregates and so on? If you say it is, is that nonexistence the ultimate truth, or is it the conventional truth? If you say it is the ultimate truth, then this would be meditation on the Perfection of Wisdom, in which all phenomena are inconceivable and there is no thinking; and in the Sūtra Teaching the Accumulation of Jewels, Mañjuśrī said, "Not abiding in anything at all, that is meditation on the Perfection of Wisdom." That, too, is contradicted. That nonexistence, once existence has been negated, is the ultimate is not found in any sūtra. Nonexistence is not the ultimate truth. As the noble Nāgārjuna said:

If an entity is not established,
A non-entity will not be established.
An entity that has become otherwise
Is what people call a non-entity.

If you ask which people say so, it is the Mīmāṃsakas and others. Accordingly, the elder Kumārila said:

In milk there is no curd and so on:
That is called prior nonexistence.
The nonexistence of milk in curd
Is the characteristic of nonexistence after destruction.
The parts of the head that are sunken,
Lacking growth and hardness,
In the form of a rabbit's horn and so on:
That is declared absolute nonexistence.
In an ox there is no horse and so on:
That is declared the nonexistence of one in another.

Which of these do you hold it to be? Whichever you accept, it is not the nature of reality. If instead you say it is conventional nonexistence, that is a claim of denial that contradicts direct perception, so let it be set aside for now. Therefore, who is there to turn back one who speaks of something not included in the two truths, contradicting the Word and the treatises?

Some say, "One meditates on the conventional truth, which is false and flawed, and from that nonconceptual wisdom arises." To that I reply: Why is the conventional called false and flawed? If you say, "Because persons and phenomena are not established by reasoning, this appearance is false and conventional," then that mere non-establishment of persons and phenomena by reasoning is the ultimate truth, and so it is that which one meditates on. But if that is not established, why call it conventional? It would be fitting for it to appear as entirely real. Moreover, even the śrāvaka Sautrāntikas and Vaibhāṣikas do not hold composites to be substantial. Having professed to be a Mādhyamika, would you cultivate composites with a rational cognition? If one saw the truth by cultivating that, all childish beings would see the truth. As the noble Nāgārjuna said:

If, just as the childish imagine,
Things thereby became true,
Then why is their nonexistence not accepted
As liberation? What is the reason?

As a general refutation: whatever one has become familiar with, one comes to have as one's very nature, like clinging to things, for example. And these practices of familiarizing oneself with existence and nonexistence also come to have that as their nature. This is a reason based on intrinsic nature. By that one does not pass beyond the level of ordinary beings. As the master himself said:

Through existence there is no liberation;
Through nonexistence, none from this becoming.

And again:

Ordinary beings who take things to have a self,
Inverted with regard to existence and nonexistence,
Through these faults fall under the power of afflictions
And are deceived by their own minds.

He also said:

Those of small intelligence who view things
As existent or as nonexistent
Do not, by that view, see
The peace of what is to be seen,
Like those blind from birth.

Some say, "One meditates on just the mental recitation, 'without intrinsic nature, without intrinsic nature.'" To that I reply: Is that the ultimate truth, or is it the conventional truth? If you say it is the ultimate truth: whatever has parts is not ultimate, like a pot, for example. This mental recitation of "without intrinsic nature" also has parts. So by a reason based on intrinsic nature it is proven to be conventional truth, or by the apprehension of what is contradictory to the pervader it is proven not to be the ultimate truth. If you say, "In the subject of the proof the reason is unestablished, because it is doubtful": that recitation is meant to express your absence of intrinsic nature, but since you cannot rest in the object itself, you accept that if one familiarizes oneself with the expression, the object expressed will one day become manifest. So the end point of an expression is letters, and that universal, too, is a conceptual thought, which has parts. Or if it is a specific characteristic, that too has parts, since there are the eight places of articulation of sound, such as the palate. That is not possible, for it would amount to becoming a reader; and since it would not be an expression, you do not accept it either. If, perchance, you were to say so, that would undermine the very application of your own words. Therefore that is not the ultimate truth, because it is an expression. Accordingly, the Teaching of the Noble Akṣayamati says, "If in the ultimate truth there is not even any movement of mind, what need is there to mention letters?" And the noble Nāgārjuna said:

There is no single-letter mantra at all;
There are no other, many letters either;
Nor are there letters that cease.

In the Akutobhayā, too, the noble Nāgārjuna said, "Through thorough investigation, with knowledge going before, one comes to realize suchness."

If instead you say it is the conventional truth, that belongs to all childish beings, so let it be set aside for now.

Some say, "One meditates only on the imputed nature." To that I reply: The imputed nature is not established by its own essence even conventionally, so there is no fault even in denying it. The master Kamalaśīla too says, in the Commentary on the Dhāraṇī of Nonconceptuality, "If one denies what does not exist even conventionally, that does not become the extreme of denial." Therefore, again, by familiarizing oneself with what does not exist in things even conventionally, one will not abandon either the afflictive obscurations or the cognitive obscurations.

Some say, "One meditates only on the dependent nature. The aspect of awareness that one meditates on is not different from awareness, just as awareness is dependent." To that I reply: In that case, since dependent origination and the dependent nature are not different, it would follow that one meditates on dependent origination. If you say, "That is no absurd consequence, for the sūtra says, 'Whoever sees dependent origination sees the Dharma'; it speaks of seeing the Dharma," I reply: If you point to its saying "sees," that was meant with respect to the conventional. It is in the state of having risen after seeing the ultimate that one posits dependent origination, the Dharma, the Buddha, and so on, but not in meditative equipoise or in the state of the ultimate. Accordingly, the Teaching of the Noble Sāgaramati says:

Whatever arises in dependence
Is not any entity at all.
Whatever lacks essential nature
Does not arise anywhere at all.

The Noble Gaṇḍavyūha Sūtra says: "The Tathāgata declared, 'Mañjuśrī, if bodhisattvas find these five sources of relief, they will be thoroughly engaged from the very start,'" and further on, "'Thinking, "I abide in the conviction that phenomena, being dependent, do not arise, and I shall establish others too in the conviction that phenomena, being dependent, do not arise," they find great relief. Here, since conditions are by nature unarisen, phenomena are said to be dependent and not to arise,'" and so on. The Teaching on the Non-Arising of All Phenomena also says:

There is no Buddha, no Dharma, and no Saṅgha;
Whoever knows it thus becomes wise.

Some say, "One meditates only on the consummate nature, because it is the ultimate. Otherwise it would follow that one meditates only on the conventional." To that I reply: What is it that is called the consummate nature? If you say, "It is called that which is consummate in being unchanging," you do not understand that very point. Suppose you say, "Because in the state of the childish and in the state of the noble ones it is alike empty of the imputed, it is the unchanging consummate nature, just as the state of mistaking a rope for a snake and the state of seeing it as a rope are alike in there being no snake." Do you not know what is meant by "the imputed" either? It is self and what is one's own, the apprehended and the apprehender, and so on, which the childish impute as existing even though they are invalidated by valid cognition. In that case, this should not be called meditating on the Middle Way; it is meditating on Mind-Only.

If you say, "It is not, for they hold the dependent nature to be ultimate, whereas Mādhyamikas hold it to be mere illusion; that is a great difference": do you call "illusion" what appears conventionally because it is not established ultimately by wisdom and gnosis? Or do you also call "illusion" what appears to rational cognition? If you say, "That is how it is," I reply: That is nothing but the imputed nature, for it is held to exist even while being invalidated by reasoning, like the self of persons. Therefore it would follow that illusions and so on are also ultimate.

Suppose you say, "Because it is invalidated by reasoning, we do not meditate on the dependent nature; we meditate on the nature of reality, empty of those, which is primordially established. Therefore our view is unlike Mind-Only: they do not meditate on what is empty of the imputed and the dependent; they meditate on a primordially established nature that is empty only of the imputed." That is true. But since you focus on a nature of reality empty of the dependent, that too is alike in meditating on the consummate nature. Therefore the Middle Way also takes the consummate nature as its object. In the Noble Unraveling of the Intent, the Blessed One said: "Paramārthasamudgata, it was with the three kinds of absence of essential nature in mind that I taught that all phenomena are without essential nature: absence of essential nature in characteristics, absence of essential nature in arising, and ultimate absence of essential nature. Among these, absence of essential nature in characteristics is the imputed nature's lack of essential nature. Absence of essential nature in arising is the dependent nature's lack of essential nature. Ultimate absence of essential nature is this: even the apprehension of things as empty of those is without essential nature." The Noble Descent into Laṅkā also says:

When it is analyzed by the intellect,
There is no imputed and no dependent nature;
And if there is no consummate entity either,
How could the intellect conceive of anything?

Some say, "There is what is called the Great Middle Way of the master Nāgārjuna. In meditating on it, one does not rely on means either; without doing anything at all, one should attend only to the profound." To that I reply: No great or small was taught with regard to the Middle Way. And the master himself, in the Compendium of Sūtras, teaches that a bodhisattva lacking skill in means should not apply himself to the profound Dharma; as the Noble Teaching of Vimalakīrti says: "What is the bondage of wisdom bound by the absence of means? It is this: to realize with certainty emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness, yet not to give thought to the major marks and excellent minor marks, the adornments of a buddha-field, and the ripening of sentient beings. This is the bondage of wisdom bound by the absence of means." This and more he teaches at length.

Some say, "At the stage of the supreme vehicle, one does not meditate on anything at all, existence, nonexistence, or the like." To that I reply: It is of that very Middle Way that it is said there is no meditating in terms of existence and nonexistence; and within the Middle Way there is no division into a supreme vehicle and one that is not supreme with respect to any teaching.

Some say, "Since it is said, 'in the manner of driving out a wedge with a wedge,' one should first meditate on nonexistence, and then meditate on what is not anything at all." To that I reply: That was not said with regard to meditating on nonexistence in order to enter the ultimate truth gradually. In the first Stages of Meditation, the teaching of the path of the śrāvakas and others, intended indirectly as a means of introduction, was taught simply in the manner of driving out a wedge with a wedge. It says: "In this way, by meditating that these are nothing but mere phenomena, the aggregates and so on, and are without self, the śrāvakas enter into the selflessness of persons. By meditating on the three realms as consciousness only, one enters into the nonexistence of objects, since this is the doctrine of consciousness (Vijñānavāda). By this method one also penetrates that nondual cognition as being without self, and thereby one has entered into the supreme selflessness." Therefore, it was with regard to something of this kind that "in the manner of driving out a wedge with a wedge" was said; but a gradual entry into meditation on the Middle Way by meditating on nonexistence cannot possibly be found anywhere in the whole of the Buddha's Word or the treatises.

Some say, "The followers of the Middle Way abandon the extremes and meditate on the middle. That is not meditating on the nature of reality as it is; that is properly found in the Secret Mantra." To that I reply: I do not refute the Secret Mantra; nevertheless, the Middle Way does not abide in the middle. In the Noble King of Samādhi it says:

"It is" and "it is not" are both extremes;
"Pure" and "impure" are both extremes.
Therefore, having fully abandoned both,
The wise do not abide even in the middle.

The masters of the Middle Way have cited this throughout the treatises of the Middle Way. The noble Nāgārjuna too says in the Sixty Verses on Reasoning:

Those whose minds have wholly passed beyond
Existence and nonexistence, and do not abide,
Meditate on the profound meaning
Of conditions, beyond all apprehending.

On this the commentary says: "Their minds have passed beyond seeing in terms of existence and nonexistence. Since there is no middle over and above those two extremes, there is no abiding in that either; therefore they do not abide." The master Kamalaśīla too, in the middle Stages of Meditation that he composed, says: "Just as the mind has neither extremes nor a middle, so too one realizes that all phenomena have neither extremes nor a middle." And further, where he gives that samādhi its name, he says: "One is called a nonabiding practitioner of meditative concentration."

Some say, "The followers of the Middle Way meditate on emptiness in a thoroughgoing way: having made even the mind nonexistent, they take an emptiness as their object, and by that one does not attain awakening. The intention of the noble Nāgārjuna is to meditate on one's own mind, which is established neither as existent nor as nonexistent and is primordially pure; by that one attains awakening." To that I reply: The Middle Way is what the noble Nāgārjuna taught. And the Tathāgata too said, in the Noble Descent into Laṅkā:

After I have passed into nirvāṇa,
In the southern land of Vedalī,
A monk, glorious and of great renown,
Who will be called by the name of Nāga,
Will destroy the positions of existence and nonexistence,
And in the world he will fully explain
My teaching, the unsurpassed Great Vehicle.
Having accomplished the ground of Perfect Joy,
He will go to the realm of Sukhāvatī.
He will become an upholder of the principles;
He will appear when the ages have passed.
Mahāmati, know this!

Given this prophecy that he would destroy the positions of existence and nonexistence and explain the Great Vehicle just as it is, and given that the earlier master Kamalaśīla and others also spoke of him as "the one who illuminated the Perfection of Wisdom," it is impossible that anything which is not a cause of awakening should be taught within the Middle Way. If taking an emptiness as one's object is taught in no treatise of the Middle Way whatsoever, then what is it that you are mistaken about? Or if, because the mind is taught to be unborn, you call that "emptiness," then it is meditation on mind only; and since that is faith in what was taught in order to lead one gradually through the philosophical tenets, that is true; let us set it aside for now.

If you say, "It is not like that. We follow the intention of the noble Nāgārjuna, and that is not to meditate on the mind as either existent or nonexistent," then do you mean that the mind is not established, ultimately, as either existent or nonexistent? Or is it not established as existent or nonexistent even conventionally? If it is the first alternative, the followers of the Middle Way too do not meditate on the mind as ultimately either existent or nonexistent; that is the Middle Way of yogic analysis, and there is not the slightest difference. If, however, it is the latter, do you mean that it is not established as nonexistent? Or else, a mind nonexistent even conventionally is taught neither in the Buddha's Word nor in the treatises, and such a nonexistent thing would not be reckoned as either mistaken or unmistaken, just like the mind of a barren woman's son.

Therefore, at the ultimate level, if one views the mind as empty, that too is superimposition; if one views it as existent, that too is superimposition; and if one views it as neither existent nor nonexistent, that too is superimposition. Even viewing it as free of defilement is superimposition. The expression "primordially pure," too, was spoken with reference to the nature, not with reference to that which has the nature. In the Noble Descent into Laṅkā, Mahāmati asked: "In other sūtras that the Blessed One has spoken, buddha-nature (tathāgatagarbha) is taught, and the Blessed One describes it as luminous and pure by its intrinsic nature, and therefore pure from the very beginning, endowed with the thirty-two major marks, and present within the bodies of sentient beings. When the Blessed One describes it as wrapped in the garment of the aggregates, elements, and sense sources, like a precious jewel of great value wrapped in a soiled cloth, as overpowered by attachment, aversion, and delusion, as sullied with the stain of conceptual imagining, and as permanent, stable, and everlasting, then, Blessed One, how is this teaching of a buddha-nature not the same as the doctrine of a self held by the non-Buddhists (tīrthikas)? Blessed One, the non-Buddhists too teach a doctrine of self, saying, 'It is a permanent agent, an agent of qualities, and admits of no distinctions.'"

The Blessed One said: "Mahāmati, my teaching of buddha-nature is not the same as the non-Buddhists' doctrine of a self. Mahāmati, the Tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly complete buddhas, having given the teaching of buddha-nature for the meaning of such terms as emptiness, the limit of reality, nirvāṇa, the unborn, signlessness, and wishlessness, teach the nonconceptual state, the domain free of appearances, by teaching the gateway of buddha-nature, so that the childish may avoid the state of being frightened by selflessness. Mahāmati, bodhisattva great beings of the future and the present should not cling to this as a self.

"Mahāmati, for example, a potter makes vessels of many kinds from a single mass of clay particles, through the combination of hand, craft, a hand tool, water, thread, and effort. Mahāmati, in the same way the Tathāgatas too teach that very selflessness of phenomena, from which all the characteristics of conceptual thought have fallen away, through various combinations of wisdom and skill in means, whether by teaching it as buddha-nature or by teaching it as selflessness; like the potter, they teach it through many kinds of synonyms in words and letters. Therefore, for that reason, Mahāmati, the teaching of buddha-nature is unlike the teaching of the non-Buddhists' doctrine of a self.

"Mahāmati, in this way, in order to draw in those who cling to the non-Buddhists' doctrine of a self, the Tathāgatas teach buddha-nature through the teaching of buddha-nature. If one asks how those whose dispositions have fallen into the view that imagines an unreal self could come to have dispositions that dwell in the domain of the three liberations, and swiftly awaken fully to unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening: Mahāmati, it is for that purpose that the Tathāgatas, the arhats, the perfectly complete buddhas, give the teaching of buddha-nature. Therefore, this is not the same as the non-Buddhists' doctrine of a self. Therefore, Mahāmati, in order to turn back the views of the non-Buddhists, one should follow the buddha-nature that is selflessness. That is to say, this teaching that phenomena are emptiness, unborn, nondual, and without intrinsic nature is the unsurpassed tenet of the bodhisattvas." Thus it is said.

As for misconceptions about the Tathāgata: some say, "Since the ultimate truth is not established as anything at all, one meditates solely on a cognition that is clearer than the previous one." To that I reply: Whatever takes a cognition as its object is not meditation on the ultimate truth, just like the mental consciousness whose object is the four conditioned factors in the sense source of mental objects. This yogin too takes as object a cognition clearer than the previous one: this is an observation of what contradicts the pervader. Without meditating on the ultimate truth, one will not pass beyond the stage of an ordinary being. But enough elaboration.

Some say, "One should meditate solely on a solitary self-awareness." To that I reply: It should be refuted with these words:

Since it is aware of itself,
A cognition that has become the domain of mind:
That it is also truly pure,
Is this right, or is it not?

Some say, "How can one speak of meditating by way of self-awareness? Cognition must cognize something other than itself; therefore one should cognize only the nature of reality." To that I reply: In that case, since it conceives in terms of two, it is not meditation on the nature of reality, just as with a vase and that which apprehends it. Therefore both of these are refuted by the passage that begins:

Since there is no object of knowledge, it is not awareness of another;
Since there is no duality, it is not self-awareness;
If it is examined, it is not real.

Some say, "One should meditate solely on a cognition that is without awareness." To that I reply: That amounts to accepting the view of the non-Buddhist Mīmāṃsakas, for they say: "Not only is cognition without an image, it is not self-aware either." How could such a one even be a yogin? But enough elaboration.

Some say, "In meditative equipoise one simply makes it an object." To that I reply: Whatever makes something its object does not have the ultimate as its object, just like eye consciousness. This yogic cognition too makes something its object: this is an observation of what contradicts the intrinsic nature.

Some say, "It is simply a matter of not making it an object." To that I reply: Whatever does not make suchness its object at all will not see suchness, just like the cognition of an ordinary person. This yogic cognition too does not make suchness its object at all: this is a non-observation of the pervader.

Some say, "If one exerts effort, that contradicts the nature of reality, so one should rest entirely uncontrived. Since it is stated, 'If one rests without striving, that becomes meditation,' mere understanding is enough." To that I reply: One should answer with such words as these: "That contradicts direct perception. In actual practice, is thirst quenched by understanding that there is water? Is hunger dispelled by understanding that there is food?" This is to speak by following the words, without understanding that the statement was made with the intention that, while one rests calmly in the state of meditative equipoise, effort becomes a fault. The Sugata, for his part, said in the Perfection of Wisdom: "For a distracted mind, even generating worldly meditative concentration is rare; what need is there to mention supramundane meditative concentration? Therefore, from the very moment one generates the mind of awakening, one should not be distracted."

Some say, "One meditates by direct perception." To that I reply: Sense direct perception has things as its objects, so it is not that. Mental direct perception is posited as occurring together with that for a single instant, so it is impossible. Self-awareness is merely the opposite of inert matter, so that is impossible. Yogic direct perception is impossible at the worldly stage.

And if it is supramundane, it would follow that one is a noble one: thus it is refuted.

Some say, "I have an inconceivable instruction. When the mind rests in it, there is the cognition of an ordinary person, a cognition that bears no weariness, a vividly clear cognition; it is something like that which one meditates on." To that I reply: What is it that you call "inconceivable"? Is it the mind? Or do you mean the dharmatā of the mind? If you say, "That is found in the Secret Mantra; the sūtras and the perfection teachings do not teach it, for they set forth all phenomena merely as without intrinsic nature, but do not set it forth as the dharmatā of one's own mind," then, though I do not refute the Secret Mantra, that is nevertheless taught in the sūtras as well. In the Heap of Jewels, in the sūtra teaching that all phenomena are beyond apprehension and without thought, the Tathāgata said to Mañjuśrī, "Mañjuśrī, as for what is called 'the inconceivable expanse,' what is it that is called 'the inconceivable expanse'?" Mañjuśrī replied, "Blessed One, what is called 'the inconceivable expanse' is the expanse in which there is no mind, in which the mind is not to be liberated, in which the mind is not to be negated, and which cannot be analyzed by thought; nevertheless, Blessed One, the mind itself is the inconceivable expanse. Why is that? In the mind there is no mind; the mind itself is not mind, and so the mind does not exist by its essential nature. Since the mind is just as it is, the very absence of mind is the mind. Blessed One, this is the inconceivable expanse." So it says. This is a scriptural source for the Yogācāra Middle Way, and there is no difference. However, since such an ordinary mind is a morally neutral mind, it is not meditation on the nature of reality.

Some say, "If one recognizes one's own ground of all (ālaya), the awakening mind, which is unconditioned throughout the three times, unchanging and permanent, that is what is called awakening, the nature of reality, and so forth; and so it is taught neither in the Perfection Vehicle nor even in the exceedingly profound Secret Mantra. This is buddha-nature; since it is the heart of the teaching, it is permanent." To that I reply: I do not refute the followers of Secret Mantra; nevertheless, with a view to leading gradually, three vehicles too were taught in the Noble Descent into Laṅkā, which also says, in the passage that begins:

As long as there is perception,
There is no reaching the end of vehicles.

Also, in the Stem Array Sūtra, fourteen vehicles are taught, yet it teaches that in the definitive meaning there is only one vehicle, because there is only one expanse of reality (dharmadhātu). As it says in the Chapter of the Great Assembly: "Perfect convergence into one vehicle means that all vehicles converge perfectly into sameness as one vehicle; they are one in the manner of entering the indivisible expanse of reality." Therefore it is by virtue of having the nature of reality in mind that the mind itself was taught to be primordially pure, as in the question Mahāmati asked. That is accepted by the Yogācāra followers of the Middle Way as well; there is no difference.

Or if you say, "We conceive it as unconditioned, whereas the followers of the Middle Way hold it to be the path," the Noble Miraculous Display of Mañjuśrī says:

Whoever conceives of the unconditioned,
And whoever conceives of nirvāṇa,
Both are in the domain of Māra:
So the wise have explained.

Or if you say, "That which has the nature is itself like that," then you have accepted the doctrine of the non-Buddhist Vedāntins, who propound the secret teaching. For they say this, as taught in the secret teaching of the Drop of Nectar (Amṛtabindu):

Mere awareness is always pure;
Thus the awakened one is always the goal.
Having examined well that there is nothing to take up or cast away,
Abide always as Brahman, free of sorrow.

So it reads. Therefore one should not wish to set oneself above others on the strength of mere words, without understanding the Tathāgata's intention. But enough elaboration.

Some say, "Although one does not conceptualize anything at all, one nevertheless meditates on the ultimate awakening mind. Awakening is free of elaboration, like space, and the sattva's meditation on that is called the meditation on ultimate awakening. As it is said:

Awakening has the characteristic of space;
It has abandoned all conceptualizing.
Whoever wishes to realize it
Is called a bodhisattva.

The followers of the Middle Way do not have that." If they say this, then I reply: Do you speak of it in terms of its essence, or do you speak of it on the basis of examination? If you say, "As in the first case," then you are claiming to be a noble one, so let us set that aside for now. Or if you say, "It is on the basis of examination," then I reply: The followers of the Middle Way accept that too. The master Kamalaśīla spoke of it in the passage that begins, "That ultimate awakening mind is supramundane, free of the extremes of elaboration," and he also said that the cause by which it is accomplished is free of conceptual thought and analysis; so there is no difference.

Some say, "The reflecting one does at the time of hearing is the wisdom of reflection; later, having trained in calm abiding, from then on there is meditation alone." To that I reply: The reflection done earlier at the time of hearing is counted as hearing; otherwise hearing would be wasted. Afterwards too one must reflect, as was taught in the section on the wisdom of reflection. Therefore it is not right to say that one does meditation alone.

Some say, "There are two kinds: meditation on discerning insight and meditation on nonconceptual insight." There is no fault in that; however, that discerning insight is included within the wisdom of reflection. Thus, with reference to the insight of the wisdom of meditation, the Noble Unraveling of the Intent says, "Insight arises from the cause, pure hearing and reflection." And the master Kamalaśīla too said, "Whatever has been examined by the wisdom arising from hearing and reflection, that very thing is to be meditated upon by the wisdom arising from meditation, and not something else, just as a horse runs a course once it has been shown it."

Some say, "One meditates by inference alone." To that I reply: Do you call it an inferential cognition because it is the continuation of an inference? Or, even in meditative equipoise, does one rely on the logical reason and produce the inference again and again? If you say it is as in the latter case, then it should not be called meditation, because it is the wisdom of reflection.

If some say, "One meditates solely without conceptual thought," then I reply: That is impossible, for it would follow that it is direct perception.

If some say, "One only familiarizes oneself with the wisdom of reflection. What is called meditation is familiarizing oneself with what has been seen before, as in the meditation that follows the path of seeing. At present one has not seen, so it should not be called meditation. Therefore, up to and including the supreme worldly quality, it is the wisdom of reflection," then I reply: I do not reject the wisdom of reflection; nevertheless, there is meditation too, because the Noble Unraveling of the Intent teaches the two samādhis, that of the image with conceptuality and that of the image without conceptuality, for the stages up to and including the supreme worldly quality. The master Kamalaśīla too said, "This is the path in which calm abiding and insight operate in union; it takes as its object the image with conceptuality and the image without conceptuality."

Again, some say, "At present, even meditating nonconceptually is only the meditation of calm abiding; insight does not arise." To that I reply: Is it meditation done after examining with discerning wisdom? Or do you mean meditating without having examined? If you mean the latter, that is reasonable; but the master Kamalaśīla said that meditation preceded by discernment is meditation in which calm abiding and insight are united.

Some say, "From now up through the ten grounds, one meditates solely on insight, the discerning cognition; for the Noble Great Nirvāṇa says: 'The śrāvakas do not see the Tathāgata lineage, because their calm abiding is predominant and their insight is weak. Bodhisattvas see it, but not clearly, because their insight is predominant and their calm abiding is weak. The Tathāgatas see everything, because the two are united.'" If they say this, then I reply: I do not reject that; however, it teaches the distinction between the path of training and the path of no more training. It does not say that either one is absent on the path of training, for it uses the words "calm abiding predominant" and "insight predominant." Moreover, if the truth were seen by insight alone, that would contradict what the Noble Unraveling of the Intent says: "All supramundane virtues are the fruit of calm abiding and insight." Therefore the master Kamalaśīla too taught that even ordinary beings must train in both.

Some say, "What is called the union of calm abiding and insight was said of first meditating on calm abiding and afterwards meditating on insight; it means that both are possessed in the continuum of a single person. Otherwise, two incompatible things could not possibly come together in a single instant." To that I reply: Whatever has a before and after is not simultaneous, as with spring and autumn. This meditating on calm abiding in the summer and other seasons, and meditating on insight in the winter and other seasons, also has a before and after: this is an observation of what contradicts the intrinsic nature. Whatever do not come together in a single instant need not be balanced in strength, like an ox that stays at home and an ox that is out in the field. The yogin's calm abiding and insight too do not come together in a single instant: this is a non-observation of the pervader. If you say, "Just so," then how will you interpret what was said: "When, through meditating on insight, wisdom has become very predominant, then, because calm abiding is weak, the mind wavers like a lamp set in the wind, and one does not see suchness very clearly; therefore at that time one should meditate on calm abiding. And when calm abiding is predominant, one does not see suchness very clearly, like a person overcome by sleep; therefore at that time one should cultivate wisdom"? It would follow that this means: "If now, through meditating on insight, the mind wavers because wisdom is predominant, then at that time one should meditate on calm abiding next winter," and so on. Therefore it would also follow that the statement, "When both have come to engage evenly, just as two oxen yoked together engage evenly, then, as long as body and mind are not harmed, one should remain without deliberate exertion," was made about minds of different times engaging evenly, and about wanting to plow a field with one ox staying at home and the other out in the field.

As for "two incompatible things," that too is settled as follows. Whatever two things are such that if either one is missing the result does not arise, while if both are present they produce it, those two are concordant in producing it. For example, it is like the eye and light: if either of the two is missing, the eye consciousness of a vase and the like does not arise, and if both are present it arises, so they are concordant in producing it. So too with calm abiding and insight: if either one is missing, mental consciousness does not arise clearly with respect to the nature of reality, and if both are present it arises. This is a reason of intrinsic nature. Thus the Noble Discussion of All Phenomena says, "One in meditative equipoise comes to know reality just as it is." Meditative equipoise is nothing other than samādhi. The Sixty Verses on Reasoning too says, "From one-pointedness of mind one comes to know reality just as it is." But enough elaboration.

Some say, "Whatever cognition in which an image of an object arises without meeting the object is conceptual; as, for example, in a cognition that arises on the basis of smoke, the appearance of fire on the far side is conceptual. This cognition of an ordinary being too arises in the image of the nature of reality without meeting the nature of reality, and so it is conceptual; this is a reason of intrinsic nature.

The nature of reality must be something that is neither existent nor nonexistent and has not arisen in the image of anything at all. Therefore, to say that one meditates on a general image entails conceptual thought fit to be joined with verbal expression, and so it becomes a so-called meditation on the nature of reality. Or else, like the cognition of a small child, the yogin would not know how to give it the name 'meditation on the nature of reality.' Therefore it is not a conceptual meditation; and since it is impossible to see the specific characteristic of the nature of reality, it is not a nonconceptual cognition either." So they say.

To that I reply: In that case, since the two, conceptual and nonconceptual, pervade all cognition, no other cognition could arise either; for when the pervader is negated, the pervaded is excluded as well. Therefore the yogin would not attain any special qualities, for nothing nonexistent can be a cause from which a result arises, as with a rabbit's horn and the like. If you say, "Are you charging us with a fault when we do not hold it to be nonexistent?" then, if it exists, is it conceptual or nonconceptual? If you say, "We do not wish to say which," that is refuted with these words:

If, saying that state cannot be shown,
You stretch out your legs,
That is lovely to the faithful
In whom renown has stirred up faith.

Or if you say, "We do not wish to call it either conceptual or nonconceptual; the cognition is like, for example, an arrow fletched like a mouse, drawn back and aimed high," that would amount to teaching:

Neither conceptual nor nonconceptual,
Do not cultivate it closely through any aspect!
The cognition aimed higher than before
Abides, truly and with certainty.

So let us set it aside for now.

If some say, "What is called 'clear' in that state of insight is clarity without an object," then I reply: In that case it would follow that it is just like the cognition of calm abiding. If you say, "They are not alike: the focus of calm abiding engages things as existent or nonexistent, whereas insight grasps at neither of the two," then for that very reason it is an imputation onto an object, just like the designation "nonconceptual."

Some say, "From the first ground upward one meditates nonconceptually, but up to and including the supreme worldly quality one meditates on discerning wisdom. As the Heap of Jewels says: 'Kāśyapa, it is like this. Just as, for example, fire arises from rubbing two sticks together and then burns those very sticks, in the same way, when the fire of knowing things as they are arises from correct discernment itself, it burns the wood of conceptual thought, like the fire of a fire drill that has been twirled.' And the Noble Descent into Laṅkā too says:

Having relied fully on mind only,
One does not conceptualize external objects.
With focus resting on suchness,
One should pass beyond mind only as well.
Having passed beyond mind only,
One should pass beyond the nonappearing.
The yogin who abides in nonappearance
Sees the Great Vehicle.
The peaceful state, spontaneously present,
Is purified by aspirations;
The sublime primordial wisdom, selfless,
Comes to be seen without appearances.

"Therefore these are a gradual meditation on discerning wisdom, not a meditation in which one conceptualizes nothing at all. Those who nowadays say that one meditates without conceptualizing anything at all are training in the footsteps of the Tönmun; theirs is not the method of meditation by gradual entry. If you ask, 'Then why do the Dhāraṇī Commentary and the Stages of Meditation speak of nonconceptuality?' that applies from the first ground upward. Or else it was meant as cutting off elaborations one aspect at a time, for a beginner's mind cannot possibly rest in not conceptualizing anything at all. As the last Stages of Meditation that he composed says: 'Further, when the yogin has entered into the absorption of samādhi, if mental consciousness is present, it must without doubt focus on something, for the cognition of ordinary beings does not suddenly become objectless. If it is absent, how then would one examine the essential nature of phenomena? By what antidote would one abandon the afflictions? For the mind of an ordinary being who has not attained the four meditative concentrations cannot possibly cease.'"

If they say this, then I reply: It is true that one first meditates on discerning wisdom, as the Tathāgata taught; and the master Kamalaśīla too said, "Therefore one who wishes to generate nonconceptual primordial wisdom should first meditate on insight, which has the characteristic of correct discernment; through it one will thoroughly abandon signs." However, there is a little more to go into. The term "discernment" has two grounds for its use: it is used for the wisdom of reflection that, once calm abiding has been accomplished, realizes and searches out the selflessness of persons and of phenomena; and it is used for the wisdom of meditation that does not conceptualize, does not distinguish, and does not search, being applied to what does not conceptualize, distinguish, search, change, or move.

Of these, with reference to the wisdom of reflection, the Noble King of Samādhi says:

If one discerns the selflessness of phenomena,
And if, having discerned it, one meditates on it,
That itself is the cause of the fruit, the attainment of nirvāṇa;
Whatever other cause there is, it does not lead to peace.

And the Noble Unraveling of the Intent says: "Having attained physical pliancy and mental pliancy, he abides in just that; and having abandoned the aspect of mind, he discerns those very phenomena he has reflected upon as images within, the domain of samādhi, and he resolves upon them. Thus, with respect to those images that are the domain of samādhi, whatever distinguishing of the knowable object there is, thorough distinguishing, complete examination, complete investigation, forbearance, interest, differentiation, view, and conceptual examination, that is called insight. In this way the bodhisattva is skilled in insight."

Also, in the Commentary on the Dhāraṇī of Entering Nonconceptuality, in the section on the seventh summarized meaning, it says: "There too, having settled the mind on the appearing signs as described, and having pacified dullness, agitation and the like, one accomplishes calm abiding; and through that non-attention whose defining characteristic is correct discernment, one completely abandons those signs. There too, one should discern in a manner that brings certainty about the meaning, just as it has been heard and reflected upon: whether by meditating on dependent origination, or by discerning that there is no coming and no going, or by investigating that things do not arise from self, from other, or from both, or by discerning their parts. In short, any manner of examination will do; one should discern in such a way that certainty arises about the absence of entities." And the same text says: "The absence of entities is the absence of phenomena; it refers to the sphere of reality (dharmadhātu)."

As for the first insight being called "discernment" with reference either to a discerning just as it is, one that does not examine, does not distinguish, does not seek, does not change and does not waver, or to mere examination: in the noble Sūtra Teaching the Accumulation of Jewels, in Which All Phenomena Are Unobservable and Without Thought, the Blessed One declared, "Mañjuśrī, of what phenomenon is 'correct discernment' a designation?" Mañjuśrī replied, "Blessed One, what is called correct discernment is the discernment that treats no phenomenon whatsoever as distinct, nor as one. Blessed One, it is changeless and nonconceptual. Blessed One, what is called correct discernment is a designation for the absence of correct discernment. Blessed One, one who relies on correct discernment does not think, 'I am an ordinary being,' or 'I am a noble śrāvaka.' Why is that? Because one does not perceive any phenomenon by which one would become an ordinary being or a noble one. Blessed One, when a son or daughter of good family relies on correct discernment, things appear to them in this way, in the manner of the sphere of reality: they treat no phenomenon whatsoever as equal, nor as unequal. They do not think, 'The phenomena of ordinary beings are unequal,' nor do they conceive, 'The phenomena of the noble ones are equal.' Why is that? Because they do not apprehend any object that might be made equal or unequal. Nor does that monk have anything by which he would make things equal or unequal. Thus a monk who is nonconceptual does not even grasp at the thought, 'I am nonconceptual.'"

In the Commentary on the Dhāraṇī of Nonconceptuality itself, in the section on the eighth summarized meaning, it says: "It teaches that when, by the power of meditating on the true meaning, there arises an aspect that examines all phenomena as being without essential nature, and it is self-aware, and when the examination arises that it itself too is without essential nature, one sees with the gnosis of the yogin." The Commentary on the Noble Questions of Kāśyapa speaks of "discerning the real just as it is." Therefore the statement "There are two grounds for the use of the word" is established.

Now, how is one to interpret what the Illumination of the Twenty Thousand says: "It is like the bird that does not see the shore"? It does not mean that, just as the bird, not seeing the shore, turns back and stays where it was before, so one who has discerned and seen nothing discerns again. If that were so, it would be a matter of going off to look again, and one would not say, "It stays where it was before." Therefore what it means is this: just as that bird looks for the shore of the ocean and does not see it, and, having nowhere to go, stays where it is, so too, when one has examined with the wisdom of reflection, the wisdom of discernment, and has seen nothing, samādhi lends its support, and the consciousness, without moving from that, remains vividly present.

As for the words "burned by the fire produced by rubbing with the fire drill," these too do not mean that, just as rubbing makes fire blaze up at once and burn, so searching, dissecting and examining make gnosis arise suddenly, right in their wake. Rather, it means this. Starting from the stage of an ordinary being, one relies on logical reasons and examines things as not established as anything at all, and samādhi supports that examination. If calm abiding and insight are out of balance, they are brought into balance; once they are balanced, one remains so for as long as one can. From there, through effort, exertion and striving, it is as in this example. Suppose a certain person who wishes to do the work of fire with fire, and who is in search of fire, rubs the fire drill upon the base stick, rubbing it vigorously, making effort, exerting himself and striving. Through this effort, exertion and striving of his, first of all heat arises from the base stick. As that very heat increases, a peak arises and smoke billows forth. When it has increased greatly, a fire without flame grows, and as soon as the fire has come forth, a flame arises. That flame, arising and blazing, does the work of fire.

Likewise, just as one rubs vigorously and makes effort, exertion and striving, so one should regard relying thoroughly on the five faculties, cultivating them, and practicing them many times. Just as heat first arises from the base stick, so one should regard the stages of heat, which come before and become the cause for the arising of the dharma of the selflessness of persons and phenomena, which like a fire burns up the afflictions. Just as, when that very heat increases, a peak arises, so one should regard the stages of peak. Just as smoke billows forth, so one should regard the forbearances that accord with the truths. Just as, when it has increased greatly, a fire without flame arises, so one should regard the supreme worldly dharmas.

Just as, as soon as the fire has arisen, a flame arises, and that flame, arising and blazing, does the work of fire, so too, when what is supramundane, arising immediately after the five powers that are fully embraced by the supreme worldly dharmas, is free of all conceptual elaboration, there arises an extremely clear seeing of what had not been seen. So it is merely the freedom from the striving and effort of cognizing a generic aspect as a specific characteristic that is called "burning." When one who has not seen the specific characteristic as characterless relies on logical reasons and the mind rests on the mere certainty that things are free of all characteristics, that is called "burning" because there arises a cognition that is similar, though mistaken. As the Questions of Kāśyapa says, "It is like being burned by realizing the real just as it is." Therefore it is taught that, just as fire burns the cause from which fire arises, so it is with the cause from which nonconceptual gnosis arises, the examination that relies on logical reasons and finds things not established as anything at all: with samādhi as its ally, through familiarity with calm abiding and insight joined in union, when the gnosis arises in which nothing at all is established, or nothing at all is seen, there is no longer any need to meditate on the mere certainty gained by relying on logical reasons, for one is free of it.

In the state after rising from meditation, one must also rely always, never separated from it, on the cognition that distinguishes and seeks; for the Crown Jewel speaks of "the meditative concentration on emptiness that is not separate from generosity, up through wisdom." In the Noble Descent into Laṅkā, the stages of meditation on wisdom are declared in brief, and the passage beginning, "Having firmly settled in mind only," also refers to examining, once calm abiding has been accomplished, step by step with the wisdom of reflection until conviction arises. It does not mean examining and then meditating on the nature of reality, taking turns. Accordingly, the first Stages of Meditation, after explaining well everything from "Having thus stabilized the mind on the object, one should analyze with wisdom" up to "through nonappearance, the supreme gnosis comes to be seen as selfless," says, "Thus, by these stages of meditation, one should meditate on reality." This means that one should cultivate familiarity with the conviction gained through the wisdom of reflection. Therefore, with reference to meditating with conviction on that meaning, it says, "At this point, if dullness, agitation and the like arise, they should be pacified as taught before." Since it says this, the meaning is that just as earlier, at the stage of cultivating calm abiding alone, one pacified dullness, agitation and the like, so too in this meditation, in which calm abiding and insight are joined in union upon the meaning discerned by the wisdom of reflection, one must pacify them.

The words, "When, in observing all phenomena as without essential nature," show that a partial cutting off of conceptual elaborations is not what is meant. The words, "free of dullness, agitation and the like," mean that one should also be free of a preponderance of calm abiding, a preponderance of insight, lack of joy, and indeterminate, neutral cognition. The words, "then, when cognition proceeds without deliberate effort," mean that even without any striving, it remains vividly present. As for the words, "The path in which calm abiding and insight engage joined in union is complete," this is held to be so designated because both, though small, are present equally and joined in union; and it means that for the time being, at that point, one should make no effort. The words, "At that time, for as long as one is able, one should meditate through the power of aspiration, abiding on the stage of conduct through aspiration," mean this: the nature of reality of all phenomena has not become fully manifest as it has for a noble one; relying on reasoning, certainty about it as a hidden object has arisen; and since on the stage of ordinary beings this is aspiration, one should become familiar with it. This shows that even on the stage of ordinary beings there is a meditation, by way of aspiration, in which one conceives of nothing at all. Were it not so, it would follow, absurdly, that after meditating gradually and generating nonconceptual gnosis, one would meditate by way of aspiration once again.

If someone asks, "In that case, how is one to interpret the statements, 'There will be twelve distinct states,' and, 'In the states of heat and peak one abandons the external apprehended object, and in the states of forbearance and the supreme worldly dharmas one abandons the internal apprehending mind,' and so forth?" That is true. Nevertheless, even though one meditates without dwelling even on the mere freedom from all characteristics that is the nature of reality of all things, one parts from the conceptions to be abandoned gradually, from the coarse to the subtle; those statements were made with this in mind.

If one objects, "If one meditated on the nature of reality gradually, it would be reasonable to part from them gradually; but since one meditates on it all at once, it is not reasonable to part gradually from the things that bear that nature, which are to be abandoned; it is reasonable to abandon them all at once," then, in that case, once the gnosis that directly perceives the nature of reality of all phenomena has arisen on the first ground, it would be reasonable for the afflictions to be abandoned by meditation also to be abandoned at a single time. If you say, "They are like the stains on a cloth: they are removed from the coarse to the subtle, and therefore they cannot be abandoned at a single time," it is just the same here.

If one asks, "Well then, what about the Dhāraṇī of Entering Nonconceptuality, which also teaches that the four signs, conceptualizing intrinsic nature, conceptualizing the antidote, conceptualizing reality and conceptualizing attainment, are abandoned in sequence?" That too says that once calm abiding has been accomplished, and one has examined with the wisdom of discernment, which belongs to the wisdom of reflection, until one is convinced, one then meditates on its meaning without conceptualizing. In that same text, in the section on the seventh summarized meaning, the correct application for completely abandoning the signs, it says that once calm abiding has been accomplished, one discerns the sign of conceptualizing intrinsic nature and then abandons it; and at that point the passage beginning, "Should yogins by chance think thus," says, "If conceptions arise again, one should discern them." If, at the point of gaining conviction about the conceptualizing of intrinsic nature, one gains conviction about all four, then it says to meditate from there on. If one is not convinced, then even the conceptualizing of attainment should be abandoned by the wisdom of discernment. That these are abandoned by ordinary beings is stated in the same text: "These engagements with the signs of conceptualizing the grounds and so forth belong only to beginner yogins, not to those who have entered the grounds, because for them they are impossible."

Then, with reference to meditating on the meaning of which one has become convinced through the wisdom of reflection, namely that none of the four signs is established, it says, "Then the yogin, through the power of aspiration and through wisdom, should abandon the entire net of signs that conceive of existence and so forth, and, aspiring to reality with a mind that is without examination, free of analysis, and of a single taste in the inexpressible, should remain meditating with great clarity, without deliberate effort," and so forth. This teaches the following: an ordinary being, abiding in samādhi and having discerned with the wisdom of reflection, should abandon the net of conceptions about all signs and remain meditating, by way of aspiration, on not conceiving anything at all. It does not teach, "Meditate after cutting off elaborations piecemeal," nor a beginner's meditation on not conceiving anything at all.

In the middle Stages of Meditation too, after teaching the wisdom of reflection in the passage from "Then, once calm abiding has been accomplished, one should cultivate insight; one should reflect as follows" up to "is called one who practices meditative concentration with supreme wisdom," it says, for the stage of the wisdom of meditation, "Thus, one who has entered reality, the selflessness of persons and phenomena, has nothing else to examine thoroughly or to look at; and so, with a mental attention that is free of examination and analysis, has become one with the inexpressible, and engages of its own accord, without deliberate effort, one should remain meditating on reality with great clarity," and so forth. There too it speaks of meditation in which elaborations are cut off all together, and it also establishes that beginners meditate nonconceptually; for it is impossible that one on the first ground would be unable to remain for more than an hour, half a session, or so.

In the last Stages of Meditation too, with reference to the wisdom of discernment that belongs to the wisdom of reflection, exercised while abiding in samādhi, it says, "One should practice meditative concentration until the body of the Tathāgata is seen clearly, as if seated before one. Then one should cultivate insight by examining the coming or going of the image of the Tathāgata's body," and so forth. Then, with reference to meditating on its meaning, it says that having analyzed, "It is free of any nature such as existence," one should meditate on reality with a mind that is nonconceptual and has become one with the inexpressible, and remain so for as long as one wishes, and so forth. It also says, in the passage that runs from "Once the yogin has accomplished calm abiding, then, in order to realize reality, one should cultivate insight. Discerning the real is called insight" to "having examined with wisdom, since one does not observe the essential nature of any entity at all, one is also one who practices meditative concentration with supreme wisdom." Then, with reference to meditating on that, it says, "Thus, since one who has entered reality, the selflessness of persons and phenomena, has nothing else to look at or to examine, the mind, without analysis, inexpressible, and become of a single nature, engages of its own accord; and since there is therefore no deliberate effort, the yogin should hold reality with certainty, with great clarity, and remain so. Abiding there, one should not let the stream of mind be distracted," and so forth. This too means that, abiding in calm abiding, one does not observe persons or any phenomena, by means of the insight that discerns with the wisdom of reflection; then, in order to meditate on its meaning, one again holds it with certainty, without conceptualizing, in the manner in which there is nothing to hold; this is what is called "holding." It is not a piecemeal meditation on elaboration; nor is it that beginners have no nonconceptual meditation apart from examining and seeking; nor does one see the truth by that alone; for it teaches that excess, deficiency and the like must be corrected again and again, and that even such a practitioner cannot remain for more than an hour or so.

Nor is a beginner's meditation on nonconceptuality free of all elaboration, for in the Perfection of Wisdom in Seven Hundred Lines the Tathāgata said, "Mañjuśrī, do you not enter the inconceivable samādhi?" and Mañjuśrī replied, "Blessed One, I do not enter the inconceivable samādhi. Why is that? Formerly, when I was a beginner, I entered the inconceivable samādhi. Now, since I myself am inconceivable, I do not enter the inconceivable samādhi." So it says, together with an example. As for "inconceivable," the commentary on that same text says, "'Inconceivable' means beyond thought. Beyond thought means that it cannot be thought." This is said with regard to freedom from elaboration. The Dhāraṇī of Entering Nonconceptuality also says, in the section on the method for abandoning signs, "Even if one analyzes, 'That which is not conceptualized by any conceptualization is the sphere of nonconceptuality,' that is still analysis within conceptuality."

What is called Tönmun means "entering all at once." As for what is called entering all at once, the master Kamalaśīla showed that it consists in abandoning the prior careful examination, by the wisdom of discernment, of the nature of reality free of all elaboration, and merely meditating without conceiving anything at all. That it does not teach skillful means has already been shown elsewhere. So it is that kind of practice that the name refers to; but not conceiving anything when preceded by discernment is entering gradually, as the master himself said, as mentioned above.

The statement, "The cognition of ordinary beings does not suddenly become objectless," also means that it does not become so without a cause. It does not mean that nonconceptual gnosis fails to arise because a long time has not yet passed, for even others do not accept that. Therefore, what it means is this: whatever is not preceded by careful examination does not become objectless, just as an appearance of water in a mirage, when not preceded by careful examination, does not cease to be apprehended as water. This yogin too lacks any prior examination, made with an undistracted mind, that destroys the apprehension of persons and phenomena; so this is the non-observation of the pervader.

As the Noble King of Samādhi says:
Though one may cultivate that samādhi,
It does not destroy the perception of a self;
So afterward the afflictions rise up in force.

Whatever is preceded by careful examination without distraction becomes objectless. For example, it is like this: in one who sees water in a mirage, through an undistracted examination going first, asking of what appears to be moving upward, "Is it going to the edge, or going to the middle?" and so on, a cognition apprehending water arises. This beginner yogin too, abiding in calm abiding, is preceded by careful examination, "Is this truly one, or is it many?"; so this is a reason of nature. Accordingly, the Noble Cloud of Jewels says, "To realize, having examined with insight, that things are without essential nature is to enter signlessness." The Noble Descent into Laṅkā says, "Mahāmati, because, when one examines with the intellect, one does not conceive of any specific or general characteristic, all phenomena are said to be without essential nature." And the same text says, "Mahāmati, even an ox's horn, when broken down into minute particles, does not remain; and the minute particles too, when each is broken down, do not remain as having the characteristic of particles." The Play of Mañjuśrī also says: Mañjuśrī said, "Girl, how is a bodhisattva victorious in battle?" The girl replied, "Mañjuśrī, it is the one who, though discerning all phenomena, apprehends them." The Noble King of Samādhi says: If one discerns that phenomena are without self, And if, having discerned them, one cultivates this, That is the cause of the fruit, the attainment of nirvāṇa; Whatever other cause there be will not bring peace.

The last Stages of Meditation, in that very context, speaks of being able to apply attention, and of being able to be objectless, once discernment has gone before. It says: "Therefore, the absence of mindfulness and absence of attention spoken of in the sublime Dharma should also be seen as preceded by correct discernment; for it is only through correct discernment that absence of mindfulness and absence of attention are possible, not otherwise. For when the yogin examines with correct wisdom and, ultimately, sees no phenomenon whatsoever arisen in the three times, how then could he be mindful or attend?" and so forth. The first Stages of Meditation also says, "Held by the hand of samādhi, with the weapon of extremely subtle wisdom, one should remove the thorn that is in the mind, the seeds of wrong conceptualization about form and the rest. When one does so, just as trees pulled out by the roots do not grow in the ground, so, since it has no root, that mind of wrong conceptualization will not arise again."

As for the words "not having attained the four dhyānas": he had said to the opponent, "At the time of meditative equipoise, if there is mental consciousness, then it must necessarily focus on something, for the cognition of ordinary beings does not become objectless without a cause. But if there is no mental consciousness, then how will one realize that phenomena are without essential nature? By what antidote will one abandon the afflictions? And so how will one be a yogin?" Fearing that the opponent might perhaps reply, "In the attainment of non-perception there is no mental consciousness, yet one is called a yogin; and in the attainment of cessation, though mental consciousness is present, one does not focus on an object; so that fault is inconclusive," he said in answer, "Though one is an ordinary being, one cannot stop mental consciousness without attaining the fourth dhyāna; and to become objectless while mental consciousness is present, without discerning wisdom having gone before, is possible for noble ones but not for ordinary beings. So what use is it to say that?" This means that an ordinary being comes to apprehend nothing when samādhi has gone before and careful examination has been made as well. Therefore it is shown that to cite as authority at times, without examining with an impartial mind, "It is impossible to become objectless now," and at other times to say, "Do not meditate without conceiving anything, or you will end up in the attainment of non-perception," does not win over the minds of the learned.

Now, misconceptions about the indications of progress are of two kinds: those concerning meditative equipoise and those concerning rising from it. Regarding these, some say that as soon as they settle in meditative equipoise, parts of the body and so on itch intensely, tickle, seem to drip, feel rough, tremble, quiver and throb, grow warm, sink down or are lifted up, and the like; to take these, or mere joy in the mind, as indications of insight is a misconception, because they merely accompany calm abiding. To regard as wonders the hearing of the sounds of nonhuman beings, the seeing of forms, lights and so forth, and being given food; and, since one does not see the face of an emanation body without being free of the three coarse stains, the hope some have, after only a year or a month, of receiving prophecies and blessings from noble ones: these are misconceptions, because they are the blessings of Māra. In the state after rising, some say, "I see atoms," or, "I see nothing at all"; that too is a misconception, because conventional designations are applied through aggregation and duality, and because it is impossible for cognition after rising to arise without an aspect. Therefore the Noble Descent into Laṅkā, with reference to the meditative concentration practiced by the childish and the meditative concentration that thoroughly analyzes meaning, says: When the yogin applies himself with diligence, He sees forms like the sun and the moon, Things like light and like lotuses, And patterns like fire in the sky. These various signs Lead onto the path of the non-Buddhists (tīrthikas). When all of these have been cast aside And he has become free of appearance, Then the hand of the divine Buddhas, Coming forth when all is stilled, Strokes his head: The omen of following what is true.

Therefore, one who wishes to guard the trainings should behave like a mute. One who wishes to give rise to gnosis like space and gnosis like an illusion must train, at the time of meditative equipoise, in not abiding in anything at all, and, at the time of rising, since things are empty of any true nature, must train in seeing them as like the appearances of a dream. So if, in deep solitude, a firm aspiration toward that arises, it is an indication of having entered the path. The other indications of the unerring path, For those who make practice their very heart, I shall teach in due order. This is an interim verse.

Concerning the object and the cognition,
The brief refutation of misconceptions,
And the refutation of misconceptions about indications:
By these the misconceptions about meditation are summed up.
This is a summarizing verse.

Here, to establish one's own position in a way in which there is nothing to establish is what is called "not giving rise to clinging," for one follows what is taught: For those who have no position, How could there be an opposing position? Nevertheless, whatever cognition arises after one has recalled a logical reason related to some probandum, and has recalled the relation, is conceptual; for example, like the cognition that arises on the basis of smoke after the relation has been recalled, in which a hidden fire appears. The cognition of the nature of reality, which is free of all elaboration, arises on the basis of the reason related to it, being free of one and many, after the relation has been recalled; so this is a reason of nature. Whatever is conceptual has a generic object; for example, like the cognition that focuses merely on the exclusion of what is not fire. This focusing on the nature of reality, by an ordinary being who analyzes a hidden object, is also conceptual, and so it has as its object a generic image free of all elaborations such as existence and nonexistence. Here, since from the standpoint of the cognition in meditative equipoise nothing whatsoever is asserted, all the faults mentioned above are dispelled.

As for the conventional designations applied by the cognition after rising, I would like to set out most of what was mentioned above: that in terms of its nature it is called "conceptual"; that it is called "nonconceptual," because it is not fixed even as something merely fit to be associated with expressions of existence and nonexistence, nor even as the extremely general "unobservable"; that just this is called "a clear object"; that it is called "the awakening mind"; that, since there is clinging to it as a specific characteristic, it is called "meditation on the imputed nature"; and so forth. But fearing that it would be too long, I have not written it here. Through the cognitions of equipoise and of rising, Taking up the weapon of the two modes, The one who strikes with the hand of reasoning Abandons misconceptions, and insight is born. This is a summarizing verse.

Since I am utterly blind, as one blind from birth,
For whatever is improper in the profound meaning
Of the method for generating insight, I beg forbearance.
I have made this so that students may give rise to insight.

By this virtue, may all beginners
Teach cause and effect, neither too lofty nor too low,
Guard moral discipline, and, free of pride, meditate on the nature of reality;
May they enter the unerring path, and may heat and the rest arise.

Here ends The Method for Generating Insight, set down in brief.
