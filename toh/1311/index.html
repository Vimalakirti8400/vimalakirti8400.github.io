About this text

The Means of Accomplishing Nairātmyā, in Sanskrit the Nairātmyāsādhana and in Tibetan Bdag med ma'i sgrub pa'i thabs, is a concise tantric meditation manual devoted to Nairātmyā, the goddess of selflessness who is the consort of Hevajra. It leads the practitioner through a full day of practice: rising at dawn and protecting the place, offering flowers to the fifteen goddesses of her maṇḍala, taking refuge and cultivating the four immeasurables, dissolving all appearances into luminous emptiness, and then visualizing Nairātmyā herself as a fierce, dancing, three-eyed goddess, followed by empowerment, mantra recitation, dedication and the practice of sleep. Its author, the master Kṛṣṇapaṇḍita, presents it as a condensation of longer instructions taught by Śāntipāda and others. It is Toh 1311 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the section of tantra commentaries, among the sādhanas of the Hevajra cycle.

Translation

In the language of India: nairātmasādhanaṃ. In the language of Tibet: The Means of Accomplishing Nairātmyā.

Homage to Vajrasattva.

That which is neither saṃsāra nor nirvāṇa,
neither possessed of form nor formless:
its own nature, in conventional terms,
I shall now set down for the sake of the immature.

The yogin who is entitled to this meditation rises from his bed at dawn, visualizes the seed syllable of Nairātmyā upon a moon at his heart, and, reciting oṃ rakṣa rakṣa hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā three times, should protect the place, himself and the yoga. With hūṃ he consecrates the cow dung, and with oṃ āḥ hūṃ sarva tathāgata viśodhana vajre svāhā he consecrates the water. Having made the maṇḍala for recitation, with oṃ medinī vajra bandha hūṃ he should regard the ground as having the nature of vajra and scatter flowers.

Visualizing the maṇḍala in his mind as a celestial palace, he consecrates the flowers with oṃ vajra puṣpe āḥ hūṃ. On the hub at the center of the four-petaled lotus that stands in the middle, he places a flower with oṃ nairātaim vajra puṣpe āḥ hūṃ svāhā. On the eastern petal, with a vajri. On the southern petal, with i gaurī. On the western petal, with ī vari yoginī. On the northern petal, with u vajraṭāki. In the northeast, the quarter of Īśāna, with ū pukkasī. In the southeast, the quarter of fire, with ṛ śavarī. In the southwest, the quarter of Nairṛti, with ṝ caṇḍalī. In the northwest, the quarter of the wind, with ḷ ḍombinī. At the eastern gate, with ḹ gaurī. At the southern gate, with e caurī. At the western gate, with ai vetālī. At the northern gate, with o ghasmarī. Below, with au bahūcaryī. Above, with ā khecaryī. Having placed them, he should perform proper worship: having worshipped with the offerings set out, using oṃ vajra dhūpe āḥ hūṃ, he recites three times:

I go for refuge to the Three Jewels.
I confess every misdeed, each one.
I rejoice in the virtue of beings.
I hold the awakening of a buddha in my mind.

Having recited this three times, he prostrates, places his hand upon the maṇḍala, and praises her with:

Seeing beings crushed by aversion,
in order to pacify the aversion of beings,
the protector Nairātmyā has taken on form;
therefore, homage to Nairātmyā.

With oṃ vajra muḥ he requests her to depart.

After that, he should cultivate loving-kindness, which wishes to join all sentient beings with divine bliss; compassion, which wishes to remove all their suffering; joy, which wishes that divine bliss continue unbroken; and equanimity, which wishes to draw them onto the unsurpassed path.

After that, focusing his mind on all phenomena, he ascertains: "These are mind itself. Although they are not external realities, they are a delusion appearing in the aspect of objects, as in a dream, for example." Having thus discarded the aspects of all phenomena superimposed by delusion, he makes directly manifest the luminosity that is like the middle of a stainless sky at midday in autumn, and should bless it with this mantra: oṃ śūnyatājñanavajrasvabhāva ātmako'haṃ.

He rests for a moment in the wisdom of emptiness. Then he clearly meditates on a moon disc arising from the transformation of a garland of the fifteen vowels. With the light of the seed syllable a he illuminates the immeasurable and limitless world-systems, establishes all beings in the nature of the buddhas, draws the light back, and sees it enter the seed letter. Then in an instant he should meditate on Nairātmyā, complete.

She abides upon the corpse of the dharmadhātu,
her yellow hair bristling upward,
the goddess who bears the five mudrās,
adorned with an ornament of a garland of heads.
She stands in the dance of the half cross-legged posture,
her lower garment of tiger skin,
her face with fangs bared,
of fierce form, with three eyes.
In her left hand are a skull cup and a khaṭvāṅga,
and in her right a curved knife.
She is adorned with ornaments of human bone,
and her seat is the hare-marked, radiant moon.

After that, on the moon at the heart of the Blessed Lady is a curved knife, and on it, in turn, the letter a. At the head, on the hub of a thirty-two-petaled lotus, is haṃ. At the throat, on a sixteen-petaled one, oṃ. At the heart, on an eight-petaled lotus, hūṃ. At the navel, on a sixty-four-petaled one, he should meditate on eṃ.

After that, he visualizes in the middle of the sky all the buddhas, drawn there by the light of the seed syllable at his heart, and should make the request: "May all the tathāgatas empower me!" Then they confer empowerment with vases that have the nature of the five tathāgatas. A rain of flowers falls, drums sound, and a rain of saffron falls as well. On the crown of the head, Akṣobhya appears.

After that, on the eyes is Vajrā; on the ears, Nairātmyā; on the nose, the Secret Gaurī; on the tongue, Vāriyoginī; on the body, Vajraḍākinī; and on the mind also Nairātmyā. He should think of each as having her nature. Again, he should imagine oṃ at the head, āḥ at the throat and hūṃ at the heart.

If he grows weary of meditating, he should recite the mantra. Its procedure is this: on the moon at his heart he mentally writes the fifteen seed syllables, blazing like a garland of lamps, intones them in order as if reading them, and recites.

When he wishes to rise, he should make an aspiration, with:

By this virtue, may I attain
the state of the great Vajradhara,
who is all of all the buddhas,
and may beings attain it as well.
Whatever is practiced for the sake of awakening,
whatever conduct of perfect awakening,
whatever Bodhivajra has praised:
may I accomplish that nondual conduct.

Likewise, in the other sessions too, he should abide in the form of the deity. At the time of sleep as well, he should make the innate joy directly manifest and then sleep. He should do this every day in this way until he sees the signs of accomplishment. After that, he should accomplish the practice just as it is taught in the tantra.

Thinking that the extensive versions
are for the most part hard for women and others to grasp,
I have made this condensed yoga
taught by Śāntipāda and others.

The Concise Means of Accomplishing Nairātmyā, composed by the master Kṛṣṇapaṇḍita, is complete.

Translator's notes

In the sentence on all phenomena being mind, the Tibetan as written says the phenomena "are not a delusion in the aspect of objects", which contradicts the dream example and the following instruction to discard what delusion superimposes; I have translated the evident sense, that they are a delusion appearing as objects, on the assumption that the negative is a slip. In the aspiration verse, "Bodhivajra" is taken as the name of the teaching buddha praised in the Hevajra cycle; it could also be read as "the vajra of awakening". The fifteen seed syllables are the vowels used for the fifteen goddesses of the maṇḍala.
