About this text

The Brahmajāla Sūtra, known in Tibetan as the Tsangpé Drawé Do (Tshang pa'i dra ba'i mdo), or "The Sūtra of Brahma's Net," is a discourse in which the Buddha, traveling through Magadha with a large assembly of monks, laypeople, and wandering mendicants, sets out an extensive analysis of the views held by various religious teachers of his day. The text takes its evocative title from the image of Brahmā's celestial net, whose countless jeweled nodes serve as a metaphor for the interconnected web of speculative doctrines — concerning the self, the world, and ultimate existence — that the Buddha systematically examines and transcends. It belongs to a class of early sūtra literature concerned with distinguishing the Buddha's teaching from the philosophical and ascetic movements of ancient India, and it is particularly notable for its detailed enumeration of sixty-two wrong views. This work is Toh 352 in the Kangyur, where it is preserved among the sūtra collections.

At one time, the Blessed One was traveling through the land of Magadha together with a great Saṅgha of monks numbering one thousand two hundred and fifty monks, together with one thousand laymen and five hundred wandering mendicants. While journeying in that way, one night he took up residence with the monastic Saṅgha at a royal rest-house lying between Rājagṛha and the village of Ambalatthikā.

Meanwhile, the wanderer Suppiya's own pupil, a brahmin's son named Brahmadatta, was by way of many arguments and formulations speaking in praise of the Buddha, and likewise speaking in praise of the Dharma and the Saṅgha. Translator's notes

The place name rendered "the village of Ambalatthikā" follows the Pāli; the Tibetan སྐྱ་སྣར་ཅན་གྱི་གྲོང is a calque of the Pāli Ambalatthikā, meaning "the village with the mango sapling." The term ཀུན་དུ་རྒྱུ་རབ་དགའ is the standard Tibetan rendering of Pāli "Suppiya," a named wandering ascetic (parivrājaka); the literal Tibetan meaning is "the greatly joyful wanderer," but as it functions as a proper name it is rendered as Suppiya throughout. The passage beginning with "the ripening of karmic results of traveling on the paths of the sun and moon" is a list of low-level, worldly prognostication practices — reading celestial omens, interpreting meteor showers, atmospheric thunder attributed to divine drums — condemned as wrong livelihood for ascetics and brahmins. The Tibetan here compresses what in the Pāli Brahmajāla-sutta and its Sanskrit parallels is an extended catalogue of fortune-telling arts. The text as given breaks off mid-list, so the translation preserves that open-ended quality. The phrase "others whose knowledge is bent live by wrong livelihood" renders the contrast between legitimate recipients of offered food and those who earn their living by such omen-reading arts. The passage moves without a clear break from the closing of the preceding section into the Buddha's own first-person teaching. The Tibetan abruptly reprints the same sentence about the Tathāgata being praised by outsiders twice in succession; this is likely a scribal dittography, but both occurrences are rendered faithfully as the text stands. The phrase rendered "those on the other side" translates pha rol po, which in this context means outsiders or opponents who nevertheless speak the Tathāgata's praises accurately — a polemical framing preserved from the Pāli Brahmajāla Sutta tradition. The passage breaks off mid-sentence at the end; the translation reflects this exactly. The fourth ground shifts the basis from recollective samādhi to the divine eye (lha'i mig, Skt. divyacakṣus), which is a significant doctrinal distinction: instead of recollecting past lives, these practitioners observe the karmic process of death and rebirth directly. The passage breaks off mid-sentence at the close of the fourth ground description, consistent with the continuous sūtra structure of the Brahmajāla-sūtra discourse as found in the Tibetan Dīrghāgama. The phrase rendered "leads beyond suffering" translates a term that literally means "what draws out or extracts from suffering," corresponding to the standard doctrinal pair of duḥkha and its cessation or nirvāṇa; the rendering captures the soteriological meaning without technical distortion.

The gods of Radiant Light render Ābhāsvara, the class of form-realm deities whose role in cosmogonic accounts is standard in Pāli and Sanskrit sources alike; the passage here closely parallels the Brahmajāla Sūtra and Aggañña Sutta cosmogony. The passage presents the classical Buddhist account of how the eternalist view arises — the newly reborn human meditator recovers a memory of a previous existence in Brahmā's realm and, misreading that cosmogonic narrative, concludes that Brahmā is a permanent creator-deity. The Tibetan here closely parallels the Pāli Brahmajāla Sutta and the Dīgha Nikāya account. The distinction drawn at the end — between the material elements as impermanent and consciousness as permanent — represents a specific sub-variety of the "partly permanent, partly impermanent" view (ekaccasassatavāda), attributed in the Pāli sources to a second class of speculative theorists who have attained the formless absorptions and thus experienced consciousness as comparatively stable and unshifting relative to the body and the physical world. The gods called "Revelers in Play" render the Tibetan lha rtsed mos myos, corresponding to the Sanskrit Paranirmitavaśavartin or, more likely here, the Nirmāṇarati class; the specific class intended is the gods who delight excessively in their own pleasures to the point of exhaustion. The doctrinal context — some of that class being permanent and some impermanent — tracks the Pāli Brahmajāla Sutta's account of the Khiddāpadosika devas. The Tibetan here has the text break mid-sentence at the fourth basis, which is accurately reflected by the dash at the close of the translation. The phrase rendered "those on the other side likewise speak accurately and in praise of the Tathāgata" (ཕ་རོལ་པོས་ཀྱང་དེ་བཞིན་གཤེགས་པ་ལ་གང་ཡང་དག་པ་ཇི་ལྟ་བ་བཞིན་སྙན་པར་སྨྲ་) is somewhat elliptical; "those on the other side" (ཕ་རོལ་པོས) here likely refers to opponents or outside critics who nonetheless, when accurately describing the Tathāgata's teaching, speak correctly — a standard formula in the Brahmajāla Sutta context indicating that even adversaries who praise the Tathāgata only praise these relatively surface qualities, whereas the truly profound Dharmas go deeper. The passage repeats the formula twice in quick succession, which is faithful to the source text's repetition. The phrase rendered "neither finite nor infinite" in the opening summary and "both finite and infinite" in the detailed exposition reflects a deliberate and doctrinally significant oscillation in the source text: the summary statement uses the negation form (mtha' yod pa'ang ma yin, mtha' med pa'ang ma yin — "neither finite nor infinite"), while the detailed exposition shifts to the affirmative (mtha' yod pa'ang yin, mtha' med pa'ang yin — "both finite and infinite"). This is not an error; it reflects the two ways of characterizing the antinomian position, and both formulations are standard in the Pali parallels (the Brahmajāla Sutta's amarāvikkhepavādins and the finite-infinite speculators). The translation preserves this distinction faithfully. The phrase rendered "no more, no fewer" translates the Tibetan "de las mi lhag mi mang," literally "not exceeding those, not more than those," a standard scholastic formula meaning that the classification is exhaustive and exact. The doctrinal point is that the Tathāgata's understanding encompasses and surpasses all four positions without being entangled in any of them — a standard Dīghanikāya (Brahmajāla Sutta) theme carried through the Tibetan commentary here.
