About this text

The Torma Rite of Glorious Hevajra, in Sanskrit Śrī Hevajra Balividhi and in Tibetan Pal Kyé Dorjé Tormé Chok, is a short tantric ritual manual on offering torma, ritual cakes, to the deities and spirits connected with the practice of Hevajra. Quoting scripture, the tantra and earlier masters on the great benefit of such offerings, it explains when torma should be given, to the twenty-four bhūtas, the eight great gods, the eight great nāgas and the guardians of the directions, what substances it should contain, how it is visualized and blessed with mantra, and how the guests are invited and asked for long life, health, prosperity and accomplishment. A second part sets out the fierce form of the rite, in which the torma is dispatched to subdue enemies and obstructors. No author or translator is named in the text itself. It is Toh 1287 in the Tengyur, in the section of tantra commentaries, among the ritual works of the Hevajra cycle.

Translation

In the language of India: śrī hevajra baliṃ vidhi nāma. In the language of Tibet: The Torma Rite of Glorious Hevajra.

Homage to glorious Vajrasattva.

Having the nature of wisdom and method,
arising perfectly from two syllables,
supreme lord of body, speech and mind:
homage to Heruka.

Now, this has been composed so that the yogin may benefit and may subjugate at the time of the maṇḍala of glorious Hevajra, the fire offering, the rite of worship, and the practice of accomplishment.

If one asks what the purpose of the torma is, the scripture says:
To pacify the obstructors,
to delight the deities,
and to restore broken commitments,
one undertakes the torma rite as best one can.

Again, the tantra says:
If the yogin makes offering well
to all the bhūtas with these tormas,
at all times gain will come easily,
the gods will be pleased, and beings will prosper.
If one gives a torma to the hosts of bhūtas,
it brings subjugation and fierce rites, crushes the ranks of enemies,
divides, slays and strikes dumb,
and it pacifies, brings well-being and increase.

Again, certain masters say:
For all who long for the supreme accomplishments,
worldly and beyond the world,
the glorious, perfect Buddha
has highly praised the torma rite.
Since this is said, this torma rite is of very great benefit.

Now, first of all the mantrin must give torma to the twenty-four bhūtas, as it is said:
To the eight great gods with their retinues,
the eight great nāgas and the eight guardians of the directions,
and the eight that belong to their retinues,
continually, or else monthly,
or likewise in the yearly round,
at the maṇḍala, the fire offering and the recitation,
and likewise at times when harm has arisen,
as is fitting and proper,
and in keeping with whatever one can afford,
one should diligently give torma.

If one asks what the substances of the torma are, it is said:
Flowers, incense, lamps and ointments,
medicines, perfumes and food offerings for the gods,
brewed beer, grain, various beers and the like,
ulopi, laḍḍu sweets and pastries,
warm porridge, and what has been made into the sixty,
mixed together with flour and so on;
cooked rice, rice flour and the seven grains,
blood mixed with flesh, fish and the like,
cooked, raw and old,
mixed likewise with the five nectars:
filling a vessel with all of these,
set it upon a vase filled with water.
Heap pastries or coarse food and lamps on top,
and at night offer these to them as well.

Since this is said, on top of these, for a peaceful torma one mixes in curd, molasses, milk soup and the like. For a fierce one, one places an effigy of the target on top.

Now, sons of good family, the wise who wish for long life, who wish for little illness, who wish harm to be pacified, who wish glory and fame to increase, who wish to be free of debts and karmic debts, who wish to avert untimely death, who wish wealth and prosperity to increase, who wish to subjugate adversaries, and who wish to attain the accomplishments of the world and beyond the world, should apply themselves to the torma rite.

Now, having vividly meditated on oneself as the body of the Great Seal, first, in a space like the sky, from yaṃ arises a maṇḍala of wind marked with yaṃ, and from raṃ a maṇḍala of fire marked with raṃ. From that arises a, and from a, inside a skull cup, one generates the five hooks and, from the five seed syllables, the five nectars. From hūṃ arises a vajra and from a a moon, upon that. With oṃ one purifies it into the nectar of wisdom, with āḥ one generates its splendor, and with hūṃ one makes the nectar of wisdom and the nectar of commitment nondual.

Then one blesses the torma, saying this:
By the power of my merit,
by the blessing of the tathāgatas,
by the might of the secret mantras and vidyā-mantras,
and by the power of the sphere of reality,
through the wish to benefit all sentient beings,
may all my aims and all my intentions
for the realm of sentient beings without exception,
all of them, as is fitting,
come about without obstruction.

oṃ akāro mukhaṃ sarvadharmāṇāṃ ādyanutpannatvāta oṃ āḥ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā. One recites it three or seven times.

Having blessed it in this way, in order to invite the guests of the torma, one binds the mudrā of Heruka, father and mother, and recites this mantra: oṃ aralli hoḥ jaḥ hūṃ vaṃ hoḥ vajra dākinī samaya stvaṃ dṛśya hoḥ oṃ āḥ hūṃ. Having recited it three times, one imagines before oneself the twenty-four bhūtas and the rest, the eight classes of the retinue, and offers the five offerings as well.

You eight great gods with your retinues,
eight great nāgas with your retinues,
eight guardians of the world,
all you hosts of yakṣas without exception,
all you hosts of rākṣasas without exception,
all you hosts of bhūtas without exception,
all you hosts of pretas without exception,
all you hosts of piśācas without exception,
all you hosts of unmādas without exception,
all you hosts of apasmāras without exception,
all you hosts of ḍākinīs without exception,
and the other hosts of bhūtas,
however many there are without exception:
come here and pay heed to me!
Enjoy this offering, gift and torma,
and to me, named so-and-so,
grant health, long life and power,
glory, fame and good fortune,
the attainment of all vast enjoyments,
and the accomplishments of the rites
of pacifying, increasing and the rest!
You who are bound by commitment, protect me!
Be my companions in every accomplishment!
Pacify untimely death and diseases,
the grahas and the obstructors!
Let there be no bad dreams, no ill omens,
and no evil deeds!
May the world be happy, the harvests good,
and the grain increase and flourish!
May all well-being be accomplished,
and may all the mind's wishes be fulfilled!

Then, opening the lotus hands upward and directing them toward the torma, one dedicates it with this mantra. One recites: oṃ inda jama jala jakkha bhūta vihni bāu rakṣa canda sujja māda pappa talapātāle aṭṭa sappa svāhā enemies and obstructors māraya phaṭ idaṃ baliṃ bhuñja jigha phulla dhūppa māṃsa piṃgha aṃbha sappa kajja sāda khinta khuṇi pheḍa gāda oṃ akāro mukhaṃ sarvadharmāṇāṃ ādyanutpannatvāt oṃ āḥ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā.

One should offer praise to the five families, recite the hundred-syllable mantra, dispatch the enlightened activities, and make aspiration prayers. This is the torma for benefiting.

Those mantrins who perform rites to subjugate enemies, obstructors and the like should set out in front of them the torma substances mentioned above, visualize the form of whatever enemy or obstructor it may be inside the torma, invite the twenty-four bhūtas and the rest, and send off the torma. The dispatching of the rite runs from "You eight great gods" up to "all you hosts of ḍākinīs without exception," and then:

And you hosts of yakṣas and the rest,
fierce and savage bhūtas, come!
Against these malicious beings,
all you fierce and compassionless ones,
yoginīs and mātṛkās,
ḍākinīs, yakṣas and piśācas,
mahoragas and rākṣasas,
and all who take the forms
of flesh-eating, blood-drinking
dogs, hawks and herons,
owls, vultures and the like, come here!
The enemies and obstructors
who do harm to me, named so-and-so:
seize their wives, their wealth and their lives,
and take away their harmfulness!
Expel them from their own land,
divide them and drive them away!
My own forces have no strength;
the enemies and obstructors alone are strong.
So that you may eat them,
make them into flesh, blood, fat and marrow,
together with food and drink.
Devour the harm-doers and steal their luster!
According to your own yoga,
take them and make them your food;
partake of them so that all of you are satisfied.
So that they cannot harm me,
crush all the enemies and obstructors!
Having fully bestowed on me
the vast accomplishments of the rites,
depart to your own abodes and lands.
Accept this offering, as rich as I can make it,
and let the fruit of my rite come about.
Planting my knees upon the ground,
joining my palms, I make this request to you:
together with your queens, your sons and your servants,
hear me, come here, and in your kindness protect me!

Again one recites the torma mantra. Then one gathers into one the flesh and blood, the hearts and the rest of the enemies and obstructors, together with the foods of the torma, imagines that countless beings of hideous form, yakṣas and the rest, eat and drink them, and sends it off. At the time of sending it off, one sends it while sounding music, bells and small drums. Then one goes inside and does whatever recitation one wishes. At the time of sending off the torma, one sends it off with whatever exhortations one wishes, with curses and imprecations, crying out aloud, making the sound "su," and with māraya.

The Torma Rite of Glorious Hevajra is complete.

Translator's notes

In the list of torma substances, ཨུ་ལོ་པི is an unidentified foodstuff and is given as written ("ulopi"), and "what has been made into the sixty" (དྲུག་ཅུ་པར་བྱས་པ) is obscure. The blessing prayer reads ཐོག་མ་མེད་པར ("without beginning"), which does not fit a wish; it is translated as the evident reading ཐོགས་པ་མེད་པར, "without obstruction." The last line of the verse on substances has བལྟམ, taken as "heap on top." The "five hooks" generated in the skull cup are, in this tradition, the five meats that accompany the five nectars. The bird called འོལ་བ is not securely identified and is rendered "owls." The phrase སུ་བ་བྱ, rendered "making the sound su," is obscure; it may refer to a shout or a whistle used in fierce rites.
