About this text

The Noble One Hundred and Eight Names of Ākāśagarbha, together with the Dhāraṇī-Mantra, known in Tibetan as Phakpa Namkhé Nyingpö Tsen Gya Tsa Gyepa Zung Ngak Dang Chepa, is a short devotional and ritual text in praise of the bodhisattva Ākāśagarbha, whose name means "Essence of Space." It opens with homage to Ākāśagarbha as one whose vast countenance and radiant eyes perfume the center of the sky and whose sphere of activity is space itself, illuminating the whole earth. It then presents a brief dhāraṇī. As soon as the dhāraṇī is spoken, the lower realms are pacified, the earth quakes, great drums resound, and all the buddhas voice their approval. After this the Blessed One Vairocana addresses the assembled beings and extols this supreme dhāraṇī of the heart-essence of Ākāśagarbha. The work is Toh 876 in the Kangyur, where it belongs among the dhāraṇī texts, the collection of incantations and name litanies recited for protection, purification, and blessing.

Translation

The Noble One Hundred and Eight Names of Ākāśagarbha, together with the Dhāraṇī-Mantra

Homage to noble Ākāśagarbha, whose countenance is vast and the light of whose eyes streams forth and makes the center of the sky fragrant.

Homage to the one whose sphere is the sky, who illuminates the whole round of the earth.

oṃ svasti kamālākṣi bipula sambhava dharma dhātu gocara svāhā

As soon as this dhāraṇī had been spoken, all the lower realms and bad destinies were completely pacified. The earth quaked, and the sound of great drums resounded. All the buddhas also gave their "Excellent!"

Then the Blessed One Vairocana declared: "O beings seated in the circle of this assembly, listen to this supreme svāhā dhāraṇī of the directly manifest maṇḍalas of the heart-essence of Ākāśagarbha. It is extremely hard to come upon, of great merit, burning away all wrongdoing and afflictions, the mother of all bodhisattvas, a very heap of jewels. By hearing it you will become stainless, well bathed, and utterly pure. You will become like a lotus, unsullied by the mud of saṃsāra. You will become like the sun, illuminating all sentient beings. You will become like the moon, thoroughly refreshing all sentient beings. You will become like space, unsullied by any of the sufferings of the lower realms. You will become like the earth, with no high or low. You will become like Mount Meru, which no sentient being can move. You will become like liberation, untouched by pleasure and pain.

"One will obtain these and other qualities, measureless and countless, thousands upon thousands in number. Merely by hearing it, holding it, reading it, reciting it and teaching it, and merely by repeating it, whatever one's wishes, aspirations and hopes may be, they will be fulfilled. Even merely by repeating it, one is fully protected from the sufferings of excessive rain, drought, disturbance by hostility, human epidemics, bad dreams, the arising of harm, times of fear, death, robbers, wilderness, battle, wild beasts and famine; and happiness, wealth, whatever one desires and whatever one delights in will all be accomplished."

Then all those in the assembly cried "Ha ha!" with clamor and laughter, and raising a long cry, they waved their garments and their various ornaments. Bowing from the heart, they recited in full the ocean-like hymn of praise of the one hundred and eight names of noble Ākāśagarbha, who holds mastery over all bodhisattvas:

Translator's notes

The line rendered "whose mount is a mountain and a magical display" is uncertain; the Tibetan "khrul" could also mean a contrivance or apparatus. The weapon rendered "staff" translates gsal shing, a wooden club or stake whose exact form is unclear here. "Best knower of the three" and "the three knowledges" likely refer to the three knowledges (trividyā). Several epithets in this hymn are compressed and admit more than one reading. "Bearing it in his hand" renders a line that names no object. "Born from the three" and "the three precious things" are left as the Tibetan gives them. The stanzas on the Great God, Śaṅkara, and Fair Desire apply epithets of Śiva and Kāma to Ākāśagarbha. "Fair Desire" is a literal rendering, kept for consistency with this text's drafts; the Sanskrit name behind it is not securely identified, though it evidently refers to Kāma. "Great Lord", followed by the vajra thunderbolt, may stand for Mahendra (Indra). In the stanza on hearing the names, the reciter is called "supreme son of the sugatas", that is, a bodhisattva.
