About this text

The Sādhana of the Seven Syllables, in Sanskrit Saptākṣarasādhana and in Tibetan Yi ge bdun pa'i sgrub thabs, is a short tantric meditation manual devoted to Heruka, the wrathful form at the heart of the Cakrasaṃvara cycle, in union with his consort Vārāhī. It guides the practitioner through a full day of practice: rising at dawn, taking refuge and generating the mind of awakening, setting up a protective boundary, visualizing the six-armed Hero and his six surrounding goddesses arisen from the seven syllables of his mantra, receiving empowerment, putting on the armor of mantras, reciting the mantra, and later offering torma, the inner fire offering and the inner offering, before closing with a brief meditation of the completion stage. Its colophon attributes it to the great paṇḍita Kṛṣṇācārya and records its translation by the Indian preceptor Vāgīśvara with the Tibetan monk Sherab Tsek, later revised by Marpa Chökyi Wangchuk. It is Toh 1453 in the Tengyur, where it belongs to the tantra commentaries section among the ritual and practice texts of the Cakrasaṃvara cycle.

Translation

In the language of India: sapta akṣara sādhana. In the language of Tibet: The Sādhana of the Seven Syllables.

Homage to the venerable Tārā.

Through these who first take hold,
by merely recollecting him within oneself,
those endowed with understanding become excellent:
to glorious Heruka I bow.

The mantra practitioner who has become entitled to meditate on this rises from bed at dawn. At his heart, upon a sun disk arisen from raṃ, he visualizes a black hūṃ. With its rays he makes himself free of stain, and with its hook he invites the wisdom maṇḍala. He visualizes it in the expanse of the sky, possessing the bodily color, arms and weapons that will be described, together with its retinue, and he worships it fully with offerings that are of the nature of the mind. In its presence he performs the confession of wrongdoing and the rest:

"I confess each and every one of my wrongdoings. I rejoice in the merit of all the buddhas, bodhisattvas, noble ones and ordinary beings without exception. From now until awakening, I go for refuge to the Buddha, supreme among two-legged beings. I go for refuge to the Dharma of the Great Vehicle collection. I go for refuge to the Saṅgha, the assembly of irreversible bodhisattvas. I generate the mind set on unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening. For the benefit and happiness of all sentient beings, and in order to establish them, for as long as it takes, in the supreme and ultimate nirvāṇa, the awakening of a buddha, I offer my body to all the buddhas and bodhisattvas without exception. I fully dedicate all these virtues to unsurpassed awakening."

Having said this, he pleases the Blessed One by reciting the hundred-syllable mantra, oṃ śrī heruka samaya manupālaya heruka tvenopatiṣṭha dṛḍho me bhava sutoṣyo me bhava anurakto me bhava supoṣyo me bhava sarvasiddhimme prayaccha sarvakarmasu ca me cittaṃ śreyaḥ kuru hūṃ ha ha ha ha hoḥ bhagavān sarvvatathāgatavajra mā me muñca vajrī bhava mahāsamayasatva āḥ vajra muḥ, and with this he requests them to depart.

Then he should cultivate loving-kindness, in the aspect of bringing divine bliss to all sentient beings; compassion, in the aspect of removing all suffering; joy, in the aspect of being certain that divine bliss will continue without interruption; and equanimity, in the aspect of bringing the path that is the remedy for the afflictions and the secondary afflictions.

After that, he takes all phenomena as objects of the mind and examines them closely as being like an illusion and a dream. He makes all phenomena manifest as like the center of the stainless sky at midday in autumn, and he should consecrate this with the mantra oṃ śūnyatā jñāna vajra svabhāva ātmako'haṃ.

After that, from the syllable hūṃ he emanates himself as the form body of glorious Heruka, possessing the color and weapons that will be described. With the hook of the seed syllable at his heart he summons the hosts of obstructors dwelling in the ten directions and makes them enter a pit, and he pins them down with the ritual pegs of the eight goddesses, Kākāsyā and the others.

Those goddesses are of a brilliant, blazing red color, two-armed, with reddish-yellow hair blazing upward. They hold a curved knife, a skull cup and a khaṭvāṅga, their faces are wrathfully contorted, their lower bodies take the form of a spike, and they are adorned with garlands of heads.

With wrathful eyes the mantra practitioner recites the mantras and makes the obstructors enter the pit: oṃ sumbhani sumbha hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ oṃ gṛhṇa gṛhṇa hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ oṃ gṛhṇāpaya gṛhṇāpaya hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ oṃ ānaya hoḥ bhagavān vidyārāja hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. He sets the goddesses in place, circling them counterclockwise in the east and the other cardinal directions, and circling them clockwise in the southeast and the other intermediate directions.

Then: oṃ gha gha ghātaya ghātaya sarva duṣṭān hūṃ phaṭ kīlaya kīlaya sarva pāpaṃ hūṃ phaṭ hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ vajra kīla vajradharo ājñāpayati sarva duṣṭān kāya vāk citta vajra kīlaya hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. With this the pegs should be planted firmly on the crowns of their heads. oṃ vajra mudgara vajra kīlaya ākoṭaya hūṃ phaṭ. With this one should strike them with the vajra hammer.

Then, beneath the blazing light rays of the ritual pegs, he visualizes a ground of vajra nature; on the sides a vajra fence; above, a vajra tent, a vajra canopy and a net of arrows; and outside, a blazing vajra fire. He should also make this stable with the mantras: oṃ medini vajri bhava vajra bandha hūṃ oṃ vajra prākāra hūṃ vaṃ hūṃ oṃ vajra vitāna hūṃ khaṃ hūṃ oṃ vajra sarajāla traṃ saṃ traṃ oṃ vajra jvala analārka hūṃ raṃ hūṃ phaṭ.

He sees the ground as free of obstructors. In its center, from the vowels and consonants fully transformed, upon a moon and a sun, he visualizes a vajra arisen from hūṃ, with that very syllable hūṃ at its hub. Sending forth clouds of its light rays, he imagines all beings as having the nature of the Hero; and by drawing these back in, in an instant, upon a variegated lotus and a sun encircled by a crossed vajra, in the center of a six-spoked wheel, there is the Hero, trampling Bhairava and Kālarātri completely, three-faced and six-armed:

Like a dark blue rain cloud,
wearing a skirt of tiger skin,
he stands in the posture with the left leg outstretched.
He has a crown ornament of a garland of skulls,
a half moon set as his crest,
and bears a crossed vajra on the crown of his head.
He is adorned with fifty heads.
With his two hands holding vajra and bell
he is intent on embracing Vārāhī.
On the left, a skull cup and a khaṭvāṅga;
in the right hand, a trident;
with two hands, the garment of human skin.
The lord is adorned with the six seals.
His root face is deep blue,
the right is yellow,
the left green and most terrifying.
He is adorned with ornaments of human bone,
bears the thirty-two major marks,
and is adorned with the eighty excellent minor marks.
Vārāhī is like the lord,
except that she has no human skin.
The goddess holds an arrow and a bow,
and has the form of innate joy.
Then on the six spokes of the wheel,
in order counterclockwise from the east:
first is the goddess Helaki,
second Vajrabhairavī,
third the Fierce and Violent One,
fourth the Vajra Sun,
fifth is Vajraraudrī,
sixth is Vajraḍākinī.
Blue, yellow, red,
green, smoke-colored and white, in that order.
These goddesses are exceedingly fierce,
clad in the directions, with loosened hair,
all on seats of a hungry ghost and a sun,
four-armed and three-eyed.
They always hold a human skin,
and grasp well a bell and a hand drum.
They have crown ornaments of garlands of skulls,
and stand in the posture with the left leg outstretched.
All of them bear the five seals
and are adorned with garlands of heads.
Arisen perfectly from the seven syllables,
they bestow every accomplishment.

Having seen the maṇḍala thus transformed as complete, with the hooks of the seed syllable at his heart he draws in the wheel of wisdom, visualizes it in the sky, and with jaḥ hūṃ vaṃ hoḥ he summons it, makes it enter, binds it and brings it under control, contemplating the commitment maṇḍala and the wisdom maṇḍala as one. With the hooks of the seed syllable at his heart he invites all the buddhas, worships them fully with mental offerings, and for the sake of empowerment he should make supplication with this verse:

Just as the great offering was bestowed
upon the buddha Bodhivajra,
so too, for the sake of my protection,
bestow on me today the vajra of space.

The very moment he has supplicated thus, those buddhas, taking the form of Heruka, confer empowerment with substances of empowerment arisen through their own power, reciting:

The empowerment of the great vajra,
to which the three realms pay homage,
arisen from the place of the three secrets,
is to be given by all the buddhas.

and the mantra oṃ sarva tathāgata abhiṣeka samaya śriye hūṃ. Having been empowered, he generates Akṣobhya on the crown of the head of the lord of the maṇḍala, and imagines Herukī and the others, in their proper order, as sealed by the five tathāgatas.

Then one should know the armor. At the heart, upon a sun disk, one should generate oṃ hriḥ h ha hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. After that, at the heart, upon a vajra, he imagines a white ha: vajrasatva hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. At the crown of the head, upon a moon and a wheel, he visualizes a yellow hi: vairocana hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. At the top of the head, upon a sun and a lotus, he visualizes a red hu: padma narteśvara hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. At the two shoulders, upon a sun and a vajra, he visualizes a blue he: bauṣaṭ he śrī heruka hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. At the eyes, upon a sun and a jewel, he visualizes a yellow ho: hūṃ hūṃ ho vajra sūrya hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. At the forehead, upon a crossed vajra, he visualizes the syllable haṃ, green: phaṭ haṃ parama aśva vajra hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. These are the mantras of the six armors.

Furthermore, the armor mantras of Vārāhī are these. At the center of her vajra, on a moon disk, he imagines a red vaṃ: oṃ vaṃ vajra vārāhī hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. At the heart, on a moon, he visualizes a blue yoṃ: haṃ yoṃ yamenī hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. At the tongue, on a lotus, he imagines a white hriṃ: hriṃ moṃ mohani hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. At the crown of the head, upon a crossed vajra, he visualizes a green hreṃ: oṃ hreṃ hriṃ sancālani hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. At the top of the head, upon a jewel, he visualizes a sky-colored hūṃ: hūṃ hūṃ saṃtrāsani hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. At the forehead, upon a crossed vajra, he visualizes the syllable phaṭ, smoke-colored: phaṭ phaṭ caṇḍika hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ.

After that he imagines at the crown of the head, on a white lotus and a moon, oṃ; at the throat, on a red lotus, a source of phenomena (dharmodaya), āḥ; and at the heart, on a variegated lotus and a sun, hūṃ; and with oṃ āḥ hūṃ he blesses body, speech and mind. He blesses the body, speech and mind of all the goddesses of the maṇḍala as well, and then he should make it stable by again reciting the hundred-syllable mantra.

Because it is said, "Through recitation of the vidyā-mantra one will attain accomplishment," one should perform the recitation. At the heart, on the sun of a lotus, on a six-spoked wheel, he arranges the seven syllables in their proper order. He recites the mantra as though speaking it with the mind: oṃ hrīḥ h ha hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. He recites for as long as he does not grow weary. When he grows weary, he recites the hundred-syllable mantra, dissolves the wheel of the maṇḍala into the seed syllable at his heart, and rises; then he should abide at ease through the yoga of the deity.

In the midday and evening sessions he enters the house of the deity and sits on a comfortable seat. He sends forth the wheel of the maṇḍala from the seed syllable at his heart and visualizes it as it abides; and he should do everything as before, the entry of the wisdom being, the empowerment and the rest. At the beginning and the end of meditation one should give the torma.

The sequence for this is as follows. From yaṃ and raṃ fully transformed come a wind maṇḍala and a fire maṇḍala, and upon them is a skull cup born from the syllable āḥ. In its center are the substances marked by the syllable oṃ. Above it is a vajra born from hūṃ. Then, from the union of vajra and lotus, fire blazes, and by it those substances spread out, are cooked and melt, becoming white like the autumn moon. With the hooks of its light rays he draws in the nectar of the seed syllables at the hearts of all the buddhas, makes it enter into that very skull cup, and blesses it with oṃ āḥ hūṃ. With oṃ sarva vajra ḍākinī bhakṣi me śodhaya duhyaṃ vajri ṇri svāhā he consecrates it three times. Visualizing the wheel of the maṇḍala in the expanse of the sky:

Make a knot with the index fingers, join the middle fingers firmly,
join the thumbs securely as a vajra,
then set it at the center of the forehead,
and turn it round and round, revolving it in every direction.
Pressing down with the feet and looking upward,
proclaiming upward the sound pheṃ,
summon the heroes and the yoginīs
who dwell in the world-realms of the ten directions.

Then one should recite this verse:

The goddess is valid, the commitment is valid,
and the words she has spoken are supremely valid:
by this truth may these goddesses
become the cause of their caring for me.

After that, he opens the vajra palms facing upward, sets them well upon the crown of his head, recites oṃ āḥ āralli ho jaḥ hūṃ vaṃ hoḥ vajra ḍākinya samaya stvaṃ dṛśya hoḥ three, four or five times, and offers it to the wheel of the maṇḍala together with its principal deity. After that he makes offerings and recites another verse:

Having become existence and peace, you have crushed the waves of clinging conceptions;
in reality you behold all things without exception as like space;
protector whose heart lotus has blossomed in the water of most weighty compassion,
and you goddesses, be most loving toward me!

Having praised with this, one should give the remainder of the torma to the night-wanderers with this: oṃ kha kha khāhi khāhi sarvva yakṣa rākṣasa bhūta pret piśāca unmada apasmāra graha ḍākinyadaya imaṃ paliṃ gṛhṇantu samaya rakṣantu mama sarva siddhimme prayacchantu yathaivaṃ yatheṣṭaṃ bhuñjatha pibatha mā me atikramatham ma sarvva kārtaya satsukha vivriddhaye sahayikā bhavantu oṃ āḥ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā. He requests them to depart by reciting the hundred-syllable mantra ending in muḥ.

At mealtime, he purifies all the food as before. He imagines a fire the size of a thumb at the pistil of the lotus at his navel, kindles the fire in between by restraining the life wind and its exertion, and worships it with mental offerings. Seeing that very fire become a blaze, in the center of the blaze he generates the Blessed One from the seed syllable at the heart and makes him one with all the tathāgatas. He imagines his tongue arisen as a vajra from hūṃ, and with that nectar he should perform the fire offering. This is the rite of the inner fire offering.

At the time of offering, he visualizes his own consort instantly in the form of Vārāhī, and performs for her the set of practices taught in the sādhanas and elsewhere: the entry of the wisdom being, the empowerment, the two armors and the blessing of body and speech. He himself likewise takes the form of Heruka. From the syllable e comes the goddess's three-petaled lotus, and from the syllable āḥ its pistil. There is a vajra born from hūṃ and a vajra born from the syllable oṃ, and at its opening is phaṭ. Recollecting the mantra oṃ sarva tathāgata anurāgayami and moving, with the sound of supreme joy he summons all the tathāgatas. They enter through the path of the mouth into the heart, melt into great bliss, and are brought down through the path of the vajra into the lotus. Then with that very nectar one should worship the wheel of one's own maṇḍala. This is the rite of the inner offering.

Then one who wishes to make the mind stable through the samādhi of the completion stage:

Having first contemplated the vowels and consonants,
the great yogin, at rest,
should meditate at the center of the navel
on a full moon disk.
Supreme as the abode of the letters,
imagine what dwells at its center:
possessing vajra body, speech and mind,
it is to be meditated in the form of the deity.
That is beyond form:
meditate on it as mind itself.
Contemplate, too, that which is inconceivable;
do not examine it as mind itself.
When one contemplates that inconceivable,
from it form will be attained.

Through the merit gained by striving at the sādhana
of this seven-syllable mantra of Saṃvara, a treasure of incomparable qualities,
may the whole world, all beings without exception, become one
like the lord of heroes, and be firmly established in the ultimate.

The Sādhana of the Seven Syllables, composed by the great paṇḍita Kṛṣṇācārya, is complete.

It was translated by the Indian preceptor Vāgīśvara and the monk Sherab Tsek. Later the Tibetan translator, the master Marpa Chökyi Wangchuk, revised and finalized it.

Translator's notes

The opening verse of homage is terse and its first two lines are obscure in the Tibetan; the rendering given is tentative. The refuge to the Saṅgha reads "irreversible bodhi... heroes" with the syllable for "mind" (sems) evidently dropped, and is translated as "bodhisattvas". "The southeast" renders "fire," i.e. the quarter of Agni. The first goddess is written Helaki; this may be a scribal form of Herukī, the name used later in the passage, but it is kept as written. The third and fourth goddesses are given by meaning ("the Fierce and Violent One," "the Vajra Sun") since their Sanskrit names cannot be identified with confidence. "Clad in the directions" means naked. The color of the forehead syllable haṃ is written "ljagu," evidently a slip for ljang gu, "green." "His own consort" renders rang gi reg pa, literally "his own touch," evidently a slip for rig ma, "consort." The opening line of the praise verse, "having become existence and peace," is compressed and may mean "transcending both existence and peace." The life wind and its exertion refers to breath control (prāṇāyāma).
