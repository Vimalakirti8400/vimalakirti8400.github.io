About this text

The Analysis of the Reality of Hevajra, known in Tibetan as Kye rdo rje'i de kho na nyid rnam par phye ba, is a short tantric work that explains the inner meaning of the iconography of Hevajra, a principal deity of the Highest Yoga Tantra tradition. In verse, it interprets the deity's sixteen hands as the sixteen emptinesses. It also reads the animals held in his skull cups as symbols: the elephant, horse, donkey, ox and camel stand for the afflictions of delusion, aversion, pride, attachment and envy, and are identified with the buddhas Vairocana, Akṣobhya, Ratnasambhava, the Lotus-Born Amitābha and Amoghasiddhi, while further figures such as the human, the śarabha and the cat represent karma, affliction and suffering. By showing how the deity's form embodies the transformation of afflictive states into awakened wisdom, the text serves as a contemplative key to visualizing Hevajra. It is Toh 1211 in the Tengyur, where it belongs among the Indian treatises and commentaries on the tantras, specifically the body of literature associated with the Hevajra cycle.

Translation

The Analysis of the Reality of Hevajra

As stated in the Analysis of the Reality of Hevajra:

Likewise, the sixteen emptinesses
Are renowned as the sixteen hands.
Since the elephant is the nature of delusion,
The elephant is declared to be Vairocana.
Since the horse is the nature of aversion,
The horse is proclaimed to be Akṣobhya.
Since the donkey is the nature of pride,
The donkey is declared to be Ratnasambhava.
Since the ox is the nature of attachment,
The ox is declared to be the Lotus-Born.
Since the camel is the nature of envy,
The camel is Amoghasiddhi himself.
For karma, affliction and suffering
There are the human, the śarabha and the cat.
They arise perfectly as the eight emptinesses;
They abide perfectly as the eight skull cups.

As stated in the Analysis of the Reality of Hevajra:

Because it cannot be split at the three vajras,
It is renowned as Abhedyā, the Indivisible.
Because it is divided like the tip of a hair and so on,
It arises as subtle;
Because its nature is that of a barren woman's son,
It is proclaimed as form.
Because it arises from the body,
It is renowned as the Loving One.
That which turns to the left
Is called Vāmā, the Left One.
When the goddess is round,
She is proclaimed Vāmanī, the Short One.
When she is born from a tortoise,
She is called Kūrmajā, the Tortoise-Born.
That in which existence is manifestly present
Is called Bhāvakī, the Meditating One.
The cool is the channel of Śītadā, Giver of Coolness;
The hot, likewise, is the Giver of Life.
Because by nature it is free of the threefold,
It is declared to be Lalanā, the Outstretched.
By its nature of lacking intrinsic nature,
It is proclaimed Avadhūtī, the All-Trembling.
Because it bestows every taste,
It is called Rasanā, the One of Taste.
When it has the form of the moon's aspect,
The wise proclaim it the Measure.
In the form of fearsome darkness
The black color too arises.
Through the union of an even mind
She is called Sāmānyā, the Common One.
When she gives all causes,
She is renowned as Hetudāyikā, Giver of Causes.
Through the suffering of separation from union with the cause,
She is proclaimed Viyogā, the Separated.
Being dear in the manner of a kinsman,
She is known as Premaṇī, the Beloved.
Because what the mind desires is accomplished,
She is proclaimed Siddhā, the Accomplished.
By the nature of the fire element
She is surely called Pāvakī, the Cooker.
By the nature of the water element
She is likewise called Sumanā, the Good-Minded.
The goddess who dwells in fierce deeds
Is likewise proclaimed Caṇḍikā, the Fierce One.
Because the maiden is in motion,
The maiden is called the Render, Māradārikā.
By these divisions of the channels
These goddesses are renowned.

As stated in the Saṃpuṭa and the Vajraḍāka:

The left channel, starting from the throat,
She who has the body of enjoyment,
Comes to rest within the navel;
With its mouth downward, the intoxicant flows.
The channel that rises from the navel,
Likewise with its mouth upward, flows;
Coming to rest within the throat,
It is renowned as carrying blood.
Here the intoxicant is said to be the moon,
And the blood is said to be the sun.
These two, the moon and the sun,
Are renowned as the two channels.

So it is said. As stated in the Saṃpuṭa:

The lotus that lies within the heart
Has eight petals, together with a center.
The channel that lies within it
Has the very nature of a lamp;
Like a plantain flower,
It is elongated, with its mouth downward.
From within it the hero,
The size of a mustard seed,
The unstruck seed syllable hūṃ,
Drips like an essence.
This, which goes and comes,
Abides throughout the whole body.
In the form of an aspect of non-entity
It is known as the short.
At the heart, the aspect of hūṃ
Abides as the long, with two measures.
At the throat, in the form of oṃ,
It has three measures, and is prolonged.
At the forehead, this aspect of haṃ,
Sound and drop, unstruck,
In the form of Vajrasattva,
Here sports in bliss as it pleases.
On four of the petals, moreover,
Abiding in the four directions,
Are the channels of the four elements,
Each like the nature of a lamp.

Abhedyā abides at the crown,
And is said to carry teeth and nails.
At the very top of the crown is Sūkṣmarūpā,
Said to carry the hair of head and body.
In the right ear is the Loving One,
Carrying skin and grime.
On the back is Vāmā,
Said to carry flesh.
In the left ear is Vāmanī,
Said to carry sinews.
Between the eyebrows is Kūrmajā,
Carrying the string of bones.
In the eyes is Bhāvakī,
Said to carry the kidneys.
At the root of the arms is Sekā, the Empowering One,
Said to carry the heart.
She who is called Fault, Doṣā the Faulty,
Is in the armpits, carrying the eyes.
She who is called Entering, the Great Viṣṭā,
Is in the breasts, carrying bile.
At the navel Mātarā carries the lungs.
At the tip of the nose Śarvarī, the Night,
Carries the coil of the intestines.
At the mouth is Śītadā,
Carrying the sinews of the sides.
At the throat is Uṣmā, the Hot One,
Carrying the stomach.

Lalanā, Rasanā and Avadhūtī: these three are just as explained above.

At the heart Pravaṇā carries feces.
She who is called the Delighted, the face of the Delighted;
The Embracer, lying within the edge of the boundary.
At the sexual organ is Surūpiṇī, the Very Beautiful,
Said to carry phlegm.
At the anus Sāmānyā
Is said to carry pus.
In the thighs Hetudāyikā
Is said to carry blood.
In the calves Viyogā
Is said to carry dust.
In the toes Premaṇī
Is said to carry fat.
On the backs of the feet Siddhā
Is said to carry tears.
At the big toes Pāvakī carries saliva.
In the two knees is Sumanā,
Carrying flesh and mucus.
The anthers of the heart lotus,
In the order of the directional petals,
Are Trivṛtā, Kāminī, Gehā,
Caṇḍikā and Māradārikā.

Lalanā, Rasanā and Avadhūtī,
These three are Nairātmyā, the yoginī of selflessness.
Abhedyā and Sūkṣmarūpā
The Victor has declared to be Vajrā.
The Loving One and Vāmā,
These two are said to be Gaurī.
Vāmanī and Kūrmajā
Are called the Water Yoginī (Vārī).
Bhāvakī and Sekā
Are declared to be Vajraḍākinī.
Doṣā and Viṣṭā,
These two are said to be Pukkasī.
Mātarā and Śarvarī as well
Are well known as Śabarī.
Śītadā and Uṣmā
Are also said to be Caṇḍālī.
Pravaṇā and the face of the Delighted,
These two are said to be Ḍombinī.
Surūpiṇī and Sāmānyā, the two,
Are Gaurī, who guards the door.
Hetudāyikā and Viyogā
The Sage has taught to be Caurī.
Premaṇī and Siddhā as well
Are named Vetālī.
Pāvakī and Sumanā
Should be remembered as Ghasmarī.
Trivṛtā and Kāminī,
These two are said to be Bhūcarī.
Gehā, Caṇḍikā and Māradārikā,
These three channels are Khecarī.
Those held to be the ḍākinīs
Are fifteen, and they encompass all.

Translator's notes

The channel names follow the list of thirty-two channels in the Hevajra Tantra. Where the Tibetan clearly renders a known Sanskrit name I give it, followed by the meaning the verse plays on. Three names have no confident Sanskrit match in this text and are translated: "the Loving One" (in the Hevajra list this position is held by Divyā), "the Measure," and the pair "the Delighted" and "the Embracer," whose two lines are obscure.

The two couplets on "subtle" and "form" together explain the name Sūkṣmarūpā, "Subtle Form"; the Tibetan does not state the name there, so it is not added to the translation.

"The Lotus-Born" is literal; in context it stands for the lotus-family buddha, Amitābha.

"The hot, likewise, is the Giver of Life" follows the text's reading; it may be a slip for "Giver of Heat," which would match Uṣmā.

"The threefold" renders the transliterated word tripata, whose sense is uncertain.

"Māradārikā" in the verse on the maiden plays on dārikā, which means both "girl" and "she who rends."

In the Saṃpuṭa passage on hūṃ and oṃ, the word otherwise meaning mātṛkā clearly means prosodic measures (mātrā): short, long and prolonged vowels.

"Carrying the eyes" for Doṣā follows the Tibetan. Some versions of the list may have "liver" here, which is close in Tibetan spelling.

"She of urine" at the anus is read as a slip for Sāmānyā, which sounds similar and fits the list's order. "Petal" in the last verse is read as a slip for Kāminī, which looks similar and completes the list. This passage maps the thirty-two channels onto the fifteen goddesses of the Hevajra maṇḍala. The goddesses are identified by their usual Sanskrit names: Śabarī for "mountain-dweller", Caṇḍālī for "outcaste woman", Ḍombinī for "Ḍomba woman", Caurī for "thief woman", Bhūcarī for "earth-goer" and Khecarī for "sky-goer". Gaurī appears twice in this text: first for the Loving One and Vāmā, and again as door guardian for Surūpiṇī and Sāmānyā. Both occurrences are translated as the text gives them.
