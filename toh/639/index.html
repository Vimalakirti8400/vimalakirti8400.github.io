About this text

Toh 639 in the Kangyur is a dhāraṇī text known in English as "The One Hundred and Eight Names of the Noble Mañjuśrī in His Youthful Form, Together with Dhāraṇī and Mantra," corresponding to the Sanskrit title Āryamañjuśrīkumārabhūtāṣṭottaraśataka-nāma-dhāraṇī-mantra-sahita and the Tibetan 'Phags pa 'Jam dpal gzhon nur gyur pa'i mtshan brgya rtsa brgyad pa gzungs sngags dang bcas pa. The text belongs to the dhāraṇī section of the Kangyur and combines a litany of one hundred and eight names of the bodhisattva Mañjuśrī — revered in his youthful, princely aspect — with accompanying dhāraṇī and mantra formulas. Mañjuśrī is one of the most celebrated bodhisattvas in the Mahāyāna tradition, embodying wisdom, and texts of this type were recited for the merit and blessings believed to arise from invoking and venerating his names and sacred syllables. The opening homages to the buddhas of the ten directions and to Mañjuśrī himself set a devotional tone characteristic of this genre of liturgical and ritual literature.

Homage to the perfectly awakened buddhas of the ten directions, who possess utterly pure intelligence, encompassing without exception all that is to be known, supremely wondrous beyond compare.

Homage to Mañjuśrī in his youthful form, whose mind is free from all stains and faults, whose body is adorned with the rays of light of a collection of immaculate virtues, and at whose lotus feet — upon which he rests — gods, humans, asuras, nāgas, and vidyādhara mantra-holders press the crests of their topknots and the tips of their crowns.

When Mañjughoṣa speaks this mantra that accomplishes every aim of the vidyā,
immediately all sentient beings
are freed from birth and old age.
All the lower destinies become pacified,
the hosts of māras tremble greatly,
all the gods rejoice with great delight,
and those who lead others astray are seized with fear.
Even in the very moment this dhāraṇī that pacifies evil is spoken,
countless hundreds of thousands — as many as the drops in the ocean —
are liberated from the prison of saṃsāra.

When Mañjuśrī in his youthful form
proclaims the gentle melody
and speaks this vidyā mantra,
the very ground — of rocks, forests, and groves alike —
trembles there in that place,
whether among beings that move or those that do not move.
The light of the buddhas' radiance
spreads and pervades the surface of the earth.
All obstructors, misleaders,
and rākṣasas of terrifying form —

They raised their voices together in harmonious words:

who demonstrates the path of peace,

Great obstacles, false guides,

those who steal radiance, the fierce and the wild,

Then Mañjuśrī, having gazed with steady, outstretched attention,
even looked upon them with ease,
and having looked upon them all,
spoke these very words:

"May all of you always be free from obstacles,
may you attain the excellently sure liberation,
fearless and truly auspicious.
Do not regard me with fear."

Then the Tathāgata, greatly and gently smiling,

his face luminously clear,
gazed toward the east and declared:

"Alas, O supreme domain of secret mantras —
for the sake of dispelling the delusion of sentient beings,

has come to be supremely vast.

Alas, for the thorough purification
of those sentient beings who have greatly accumulated merit,
this accomplished dhāraṇī of the supreme gift
is taught with a gentle recitation.

Whoever, entering into meditative equipoise,
recites this accomplished dhāraṇī
that removes all suffering,
or hears it, or even holds it in mind —
all their aims will be accomplished,
and they will be liberated from all suffering.
The purification of all misdeeds
will certainly be attained by them.
The great ground of the Tathāgatas, too,
without long delay will they obtain.

The bodhisattvas' grounds —
Supreme Joy, the Stainless, the Luminous, the Radiant,
the Difficult to Cultivate, the Manifest,
the Far-Going, the Immovable,
Good Intellect, and Cloud of Dharma —
those great and sacred grounds as well
will be attained without delay, swiftly,
by those great-minded sentient beings
who have the wish to firmly abide there.
The purification of all misdeeds,
being beloved to all sentient beings,
the pacification of all conceptual elaborations —
unsurpassed awakening will be attained.

This pleasing collection of one hundred and eight names —
which arouses all holders of vidyā mantras,
pacifies all misdeeds,
and is supremely auspicious and virtuous —
pressing together the ten fingers in añjali,
sitting before Mañjuśrī of melodious voice,
and with a mind extremely clear and pure,
rising at dawn and reciting it:

For that one, all holders of vidyā mantras,
and likewise the lords of gods,
along with many yakṣas and rākṣasas,
will always and constantly provide protection.
All the sons of the Tathāgatas,
the Tathāgatas themselves,
and likewise those who bear the vajra weapon —
they will certainly provide that one with protection.

That one will appear to abide in the form of a buddha,
appearing to be in meditative equipoise.
The lords of gods, the yakṣas,
the great obstacles and false guides,
with joy and with pure intention,
from afar will offer prostrations."

Therefore, though the obstacles take delight and would seek to harm,
they are unable to do so.
One who recites it even a single time
will attain merit equal to that of the Great Brahmā.

Whoever, possessed of great wisdom,
recites the supreme name each day and each night —
stainless and utterly pure —
will attain the radiant, clear-sounding resonance,
and will obtain the abode of joy
pleasing to all sentient beings.

Incomparable, generating power,
traveling the supreme path,
praised by the gods who possess luminosity and sacred commitment,
enchanting assemblies of gods and demigods (asuras) who gather with devotion —
this dhāraṇī has been well spoken by the Victors.

Here is completed the dhāraṇī together with its mantra of the noble Mañjuśrī's one hundred and eight names.

Translator's notes

The mantra section is transliterated Sanskrit in Tibetan script and is rendered here in reconstructed Sanskrit phonetic form, as is standard practice for liturgical dhāraṇī texts. Some syllables are corrupt or hybrid forms not reducible to clear Sanskrit lexical meaning; the transliteration follows the Tibetan spelling closely without normalization of every form.

The closing verse section ends mid-sentence in the supplied passage, cutting off after the mention of rākṣasas of terrifying form; the translation renders what is present and does not supply what follows. The passage is a long hymn of praise, structured as a litany of epithets, with nearly every line functioning as a name or quality in the vocative. The phrase rendered "the teacher of the blue-throated one" refers to Mañjuśrī as teacher of Śiva, whose blue throat is a well-known mythological mark. The line "knowing Maitreya as supreme and as the last" likely refers to the bodhisattva Maitreya as the future and final buddha of this era, with Mañjuśrī being praised as knowing him in both capacities. The final vocative "Tathāgata who upholds the Dharma" is doctrinally unusual as an address to Mañjuśrī but follows the tantric convention of assimilating bodhisattvas to buddha-rank epithets. The name rendered "Nirmita, son of no-craving" translates སྲེད་མེད་བུ, literally "son of Nirmita" or "son of the craving-free one." In Buddhist cosmology this is a name associated with the deity Maheśvara's son or a figure in the divine realms; the epithet is applied here to the figure being praised. The precise referent is somewhat ambiguous in context but the literal rendering is given.

The ten bodhisattva grounds listed follow the standard Mahāyāna enumeration: Supreme Joy (Pramuditā), Stainless (Vimalā), Luminous (Prabhākarī), Radiant (Arciṣmatī), Difficult to Cultivate (Sudurjayā), Manifest (Abhimukhī), Far-Going (Dūraṃgamā), Immovable (Acalā), Good Intellect (Sādhumatī), and Cloud of Dharma (Dharmameghā). The phrase rendered "gods who possess luminosity and sacred commitment" translates a compound combining luminosity (clear light) and samaya (sacred commitment or vow), referring to deities of the tantric maṇḍala who are bearers of samaya. This is a genuine technical usage and the rendering preserves both elements.

The line "the radiant, clear-sounding resonance" renders a cluster of terms meaning clear light, pleasant sound, and purity; the sense is that the practitioner's voice and presence become luminous, melodious, and undefiled — a standard fruit of name-recitation in this genre.
