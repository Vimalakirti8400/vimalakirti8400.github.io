About this text

The Dhāraṇī That Pacifies Anger, in Tibetan Khro pa zhi ba'i gzungs, is a very short dhāraṇī text whose Sanskrit title is not given in the source lines and so cannot be stated with certainty. It presents a brief mantra, invoked with homage to the Three Jewels and the Buddha's ushnisha, whose recitation in front of an angry person is said to quiet that person's anger. The text goes on to describe a number of simple practical applications of the mantra, such as reciting it over gorocana, eye ointment, or water to win people's goodwill, to obtain food when begging, and to be well received when entering an assembly. It is Toh 789 in the Kangyur, belonging to the class of tantra-related texts known as dhāraṇīs.

Translation

If it is recited in front of someone who is angry, that person's anger will certainly subside. Thus concludes the dhāraṇī that pacifies anger.

Homage to the Three Jewels.

Namo bhagavate, uṣṇīṣāya dhare, rante svāhā.

Even without formal practice, all rites will be accomplished.

Recite it one hundred and eight times over a gorocana (the yellow pigment prepared from the bile of cattle) and mark it as a dot on the forehead; whoever looks upon you will become fond of you.

Recite the mantra one hundred and eight times over eye ointment and apply it to the eyes; whoever you meet will become fond of you.

Recite it seven times over any vessel of water and then drink it, and when you go begging you will obtain food.

When you go into an assembly, take the hem of your robe in your left hand, recite it twenty-one times,

Translator's notes

The passage ends mid-sentence, so the final instruction is left unfinished. The mantra is given in the Tibetan with some corruption: "uṣṇīṣāya dhare" and "rante" are rendered as transmitted, and "bhagavate" is a restoration of the Tibetan "bhagabate." The syllables "rante" in particular are uncertain and may reflect a garbled Sanskrit original. The Tibetan "giu wang" is the Tibetan rendering of gorocana, glossed here accordingly. "Eye ointment" translates "mig rtsi," a collyrium. The "robe" is my reading of "gos," which could be any garment, though a monastic robe is likely given the context of begging for food.
