About this text

Toh 290 in the Kangyur is a sūtra known in English as The Great Sūtra Called Emptiness, in Sanskrit as the Śūnyatā-nāma-mahāsūtra, and in Tibetan as Mdo chen po stong pa nyid ces bya ba. It belongs to the general class of Mahāyāna sūtras and takes as its central concern the Buddha's direct teaching on emptiness, presented through a dialogue between the Buddha and his attendant Ānanda at the Eastern Park in Śrāvastī. The exchange is prompted by Ānanda's recollection of a statement the Buddha had once made among the Śākyas — that he dwells repeatedly in emptiness — and his wish to understand its deeper meaning. As a "great sūtra" dedicated entirely to this theme, the text offers a sustained and focused exposition of emptiness as a meditative reality and a fundamental characteristic of experience.

Homage to the Three Jewels.

Thus did I hear. At one time the Blessed One was dwelling in Śrāvastī, in the Eastern Park, in the mansion of Mṛgāramātṛ.

Then the venerable Ānanda, rising from meditative composure in the late afternoon, went to where the Blessed One was, and having gone there, he bowed his head at the feet of the Blessed One and sat to one side. Seated to one side, the venerable Ānanda addressed the Blessed One in these words:

"Reverend One, there was a time when the Blessed One was dwelling among the Śākyas, in the Śākya market town called Śākyānaṃ. At that time the Blessed One declared these words to me: 'Ānanda, I dwell repeatedly in emptiness.' I wish to know the meaning of what the Blessed One declared. Reverend One, is it that what the Blessed One spoke, and what I heard well and grasped well, kept well in mind, practiced well, and understood well — is that not other than what was meant, but exactly as it was?"

The Blessed One declared: "Ānanda, it is just so. What I spoke, and what you heard well and grasped well, practiced well, and understood well — that is not otherwise; it is exactly as it was. And why is that? Ānanda, it is because both then and now I dwell repeatedly in emptiness.

"Ānanda, consider this example. This mansion of Mṛgāramātṛ is empty of elephants, horses, cattle, sheep, chickens, and pigs; empty of wealth, grain, goods, and gold; and empty of male and female servants, workers, and those who live on buttermilk, and of men, women, boys, and girls. And yet there is this much that is not empty here, namely: the community of monks alone, or something pertaining to that.

"Ānanda, in just the same way, whatever is not present in something — that thing is rightly seen to be empty of it. And whatever remains present there — that is rightly and accurately known to exist there. Ānanda, this is the entry into emptiness exactly as it is, without distortion.

"Ānanda, therefore, if a monk should wish: 'May I dwell repeatedly in emptiness,' then, Ānanda, that monk should not attend to the perception of village or the perception of people, but should attend to the perception of forest — or at least to something pertaining to that. He should think to himself: 'This type of perception — the perception of village and the perception of people — is empty here. And yet there is this much that is not empty here, namely: the perception of forest alone, or something pertaining to that.' And while those bases of affliction that depend on the perception of village and the perception of people are absent, there do remain those bases of affliction that depend on the perception of forest alone, or something pertaining to that.

"Ānanda, therefore, whatever is not present in something — that thing is rightly seen to be empty of it. And whatever remains present there — that is rightly and accurately known to exist there. Ānanda, this is the entry into emptiness exactly as it is, without distortion.

Knowing thus and seeing thus, the mind is fully liberated from the defilement of desire, fully liberated from the defilement of existence, and fully liberated from the defilement of ignorance. Being fully liberated, he knows directly: birth is exhausted, the holy life has been lived, what had to be done has been done, and there is nothing more of this existence to know. He becomes one who simply sees with the wisdom of full liberation.

Then he thinks to himself: this field of perception is like this — it is empty of the defilement of desire, the defilement of existence, and the defilement of ignorance; yet within it there is also this much that is not empty: this very body, this sixfold sense base conditioned by life-force, which depends on that alone. And as for those sites of affliction that rest upon the defilements of desire, of existence, and of ignorance — those are absent. Yet there remains in it only this much that is a site of affliction: dependence on this very body, this sixfold sense base conditioned by life-force.

Ānanda, therefore, whatever is not present in something — that thing is rightly seen to be empty of it; yet whatever remains present in it — that is correctly and fully understood as it actually is: it is present in it. Ānanda, this is so: the liberation that is undefiled and unconditioned, arising through the exhaustion of all defilements — this is the supreme entry into emptiness.

Ānanda, all the Blessed Ones — Tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly and fully awakened buddhas — who appeared in times past, they too, having directly realized with their persons this very supreme entry into emptiness — the liberation that is undefiled and unconditioned through the exhaustion of all defilements — abided having fully accomplished it.

Ānanda, all the Blessed Ones — Tathāgatas, arhats, perfectly and fully awakened buddhas — who will appear in times to come, they too will abide having directly realized with their persons this very supreme entry into emptiness — the liberation that is undefiled and unconditioned through the exhaustion of all defilements — and having fully accomplished it.

Ānanda, I — the Tathāgata, arhat, perfectly and fully awakened buddha who has appeared at the present time — right now directly realize with my person this very supreme entry into emptiness — the liberation that is undefiled and unconditioned through the exhaustion of all defilements — and abide having fully accomplished it.

Ānanda, therefore one should train thus: I will directly realize with my person this very supreme entry into emptiness — the liberation that is undefiled and unconditioned through the exhaustion of all defilements — and abide having fully accomplished it. Ānanda, you too should train in just this way.

Then the venerable Ānanda, having openly praised and rejoiced in the words the Blessed One had declared, prostrated with his head at the feet of the Blessed One and departed from his presence.

The Great Sūtra called Emptiness is complete.

Translated by the Indian masters Jinamitra and Prajñāvarman, together with the chief editor and translator, the monk Ye shes sde, and others.

Translator's notes

The sūtra is the Cūḷasuññatāsutta in Pāli (Majjhima Nikāya 121) and its Sanskrit/Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit parallel. The Tibetan title translates as "The Great Sūtra Called Emptiness," though the Pāli version is the "Lesser Discourse on Emptiness" (Cūḷa meaning "lesser" or "minor"). The Tibetan designation "great sūtra" may reflect a different recension or a later editorial classification.

The mansion of Mṛgāramātṛ refers to the Eastern Park at Śrāvastī, a residence donated by Visākhā and known in Pāli as the Pubbārāma. The name means "the mansion of the mother of Mṛgāra," a well-known figure in early Buddhist narrative.

The passage ends mid-sentence in the original as provided; the translation follows the Tibetan exactly to that point. The phrase rendered "signless expanse" translates a term meaning a sphere or dimension free from signs or characteristics, a well-attested Pāli and Sanskrit formless attainment correlate known in the sutric literature as the signless concentration or the animitta element. The passage presents a progressive deepening of emptiness meditation through the formless absorptions — infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness — culminating in the signless expanse and then full liberation, mirroring the structure of the Cūḷasuññata Sutta of the Pāli canon.

The final phrase distinguishes two senses of a "site of affliction": those that arise from the three defilements, which are now absent, and a residual site depending on the conditioned body with its six sense bases, which persists as long as life lasts. This is the classical teaching that the arhat's liberation is complete with respect to mental defilements yet the physical basis of life remains until final nirvāṇa. The passage describes a progressive ascent through the formless absorptions — infinite space, infinite consciousness, nothingness — culminating in the signless sphere (Tibetan: mtshan ma med pa'i dbyings, Sanskrit: ānimitta-dhātu). This is the classical Pāli Cūḷasuññata Sutta sequence rendered into Tibetan. The final sentence is interrupted at the source text boundary; the translation ends at the natural break-point of the supplied Tibetan.

The phrase rendered "attend to ... or to whatever else remains" translates a recurrent formula where "de las kha cig yid la bya'o" means literally "attend to some other thing from that" — this is the standard Tibetan rendering of the Pāli residue formula, and the translation preserves its deliberate open-endedness. The closing colophon names the three translators responsible for the Tibetan rendering: Jinamitra and Prajñāvarman are well-attested Indian paṇḍitas who worked at the imperial Tibetan translation bureau, and Ye shes sde is the senior Tibetan lo tsā ba associated with them. The phrase rendered "directly realized with their persons" translates a construction in which the body or person is the instrument of direct realization, echoing the technical formula for embodied verification of liberation found across Pāli and Sanskrit parallels to this sūtra, most closely the Cūḷasuññata-sutta of the Majjhima Nikāya.
