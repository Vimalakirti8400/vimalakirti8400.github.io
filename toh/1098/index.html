The Jewel Verses
Catalog
Toh 1098 (Tōhoku catalogue; also cited as D 1098 of the Derge edition) · Derge Kangyur · Dhāraṇī collection (gzungs 'dus) · Dhāraṇī

About this text

This short verse text, recorded in the Tibetan canon without a formal title and known by its theme as a discourse on the supreme jewels, is a poem of blessing and protection closely parallel to the Jewel Discourse, the Ratana Sutta of the Pali tradition, which is called the Ratnasūtra in Sanskrit. It opens with homage to the Three Jewels and then praises, verse by verse, the Buddha as the god of gods and most excellent of humans, the Dharma as the deathless and unconditioned peace that Śākyamuni revealed, and the unsurpassed concentration taught by the Teacher. Each stanza declares its subject the supreme jewel and ends with the refrain "By this truth, may there be well-being here," an act of truth meant to bring safety and good fortune to those who hear it. The work is Toh 1098 in the Kangyur, where it belongs among the short protective and auspicious texts that the collection groups with its dhāraṇī literature.

Translation

Homage to the Three Jewels.

Whatever supreme jewels there may be, in this world or beyond,
or in the heavens,
none here is equal to the Tathāgata, who has become the god of gods,
the most excellent of humans.
Therefore this is declared the supreme jewel.
By this truth, may there be well-being here.

Extinction, freedom from passion, the deathless, the unconditioned:
these Śākyamuni revealed through his knowledge.
Nothing at all is equal to that Dharma,
the deathless, peaceful, unconditioned.
Therefore this is declared the supreme jewel.
By this truth, may there be well-being here.

What is supremely sought, rightly and fully taught,
the Teacher's unsurpassed yoga, ever practiced,
unseen even by two eyes like vajras:
there is no concentration equal to that.
Therefore this is declared the supreme jewel.
By this truth, may there be well-being here.

The eight great persons, highly praised,
who are spoken of as four pairs of persons,
great sages, persons without equal,
the Sugata declared worthy recipients of gifts.
What is given to them bears great fruit,
like seed sown in an excellent, well-moistened field.
This is declared the supreme jewel, the Saṅgha.
By this truth, may there be well-being here.

Those who strive diligently with steadfast mind
and enter the teaching of Gautama,
on attaining it, gain entry to the deathless;
dispelling darkness, they attain nirvāṇa.
Therefore the Saṅgha is declared the supreme jewel.
By this truth, may there be well-being here.

As soon as one is joined to this view,
the view of the perishing aggregates, moral discipline and ascetic observances,
and doubt, these three afflictions,
are abandoned at once, and one sees the noble truths.
Therefore the Saṅgha is declared the supreme jewel.
By this truth, may there be well-being here.

With body, with speech, or with mind,
they never commit the three kinds of wrongdoing,
and if they do something on impulse, they do not conceal it.
In this way they do not cling by means of views.
Therefore the Saṅgha is declared the supreme jewel.
By this truth, may there be well-being here.

Just as Indra's pillar, planted in the ground,
is not shaken by the winds from the four directions,
so is the noble Saṅgha of persons
who have seen the sacred noble path.
Therefore the Saṅgha is declared the supreme jewel.
By this truth, may there be well-being here.

Those who meditate on the noble truths
so well taught by profound wisdom,
and who resolve to give even their own bodies,
will not encounter the eight fears.
Therefore the Saṅgha is declared the supreme jewel.
By this truth, may there be well-being here.

Just as a fire scattered by the wind
cannot be taken up in the hand and counted,
so the Buddha's heirs, having wholly cast off the fetters,
pass beyond all designation.
Therefore the Saṅgha is declared the supreme jewel.
By this truth, may there be well-being here.

Whatever beings there are, moving or unmoving,
may they all be happy here.
To the supreme Teacher, worthy of worship by gods and humans,
to the Buddha, homage. May there be well-being here today.

Whatever beings there are, moving or unmoving,
may they all be happy here.
To the peaceful, the passion-free, worthy of worship by gods and humans,
to the Dharma, homage. May there be well-being here today.

Whatever beings there are, moving or unmoving,
may they all be happy here.
To the most excellent of assemblies, worthy of worship by gods and humans,
to the Saṅgha, homage. May there be well-being here today.

Whatever beings there are, moving or unmoving,
may they all be happy here today.
Whatever bhūta spirits have gathered here,
dwelling on the earth or in the sky,
may they always show loving-kindness to all creatures,
and may they practice the Dharma day and night.

Here, by the truth through which the Victor, conqueror of foes,
speaks utter truth without falsehood,
by that truth, may there be well-being here.
May all of them be freed from great fear.

By the splendor of all the buddhas,
the splendor of the pratyekabuddhas,
the powers of the arhats,
and all the holders of secret mantra;
by the wisdom of Śāriputra,
the miraculous power of Maudgalyāyana,
the eye of Aniruddha,
the qualities of Kāśyapa's austerities,
the first attainment of Kauṇḍinya,
the learning of Ānanda,
the loving-kindness of Brahmā,
the dominion of Śakra,
the domains of the world guardians,
the strength of Maheśvara,
the might of the generals,
and the splendor and miraculous power of Hārītī:
by the might and splendor of all these,
may my poison become no poison.

Here ends the aspiration prayer spoken in the Mahāsāhasrapramardanī.

Translator's notes

The line "unseen even by two eyes like vajras" renders the Tibetan literally. In this verse on concentration it corresponds to the "immediate" concentration of the parallel Jewel Discourse, and its sense here is unclear. The final syllable of the colophon appears to be a scribal flourish and has not been rendered. The Mahāsāhasrapramardanī ("Great Thousand Crusher") is one of the five Protection dhāraṇīs. "The generals" renders sde dpon; in this literature these are usually the yakṣa generals, but the Tibetan does not say "yakṣa." "Extinction" renders zad pa (Pali khaya).
