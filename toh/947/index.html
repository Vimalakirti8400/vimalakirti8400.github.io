About this text

The Noble Great Vehicle Sūtra Called Mahābala, known in Sanskrit as the Ārya Mahābala Nāma Mahāyāna Sūtra and in Tibetan as Pakpa Tobpoche Zhejawa Tekpa Chenpö Do, is a Mahāyāna scripture centered on Mahābala, the wrathful protective deity whose name means "Great Strength." The text opens on the great Mount Īṣādhara, where the Buddha is teaching in the presence of the bodhisattva Vajrapāṇi. It then describes the arrangement of a sacred image or maṇḍala, with the Buddha seated on a flaming lotus throne and the four-armed Mahābala at his right, holding a yak-tail whisk, a club, and a noose and making a gesture of homage. Combining sūtra framing with ritual and visualization instructions typical of early tantric practice, the work is concerned with protection and the invocation of Mahābala's power. It is Toh 947 in the Kangyur, where it belongs to the collection of dhāraṇī texts, the incantations and protective formulas gathered among the tantric scriptures.

Homage to all the buddhas and bodhisattvas.

Thus did I hear at one time. The Blessed One was teaching the Dharma on the great Mount Īṣādhara. Then the blessed bodhisattva great being Vajrapāṇi, praised, extolled, honored and delighted by all the buddhas, was also present right there.

Then the Buddha, the Blessed One, comforting beings with his hand, is seated in the east on a lotus seat garlanded with flames blazing all around. To the right of the Blessed One is Mahābala, four-armed. In one hand he holds a yak-tail whisk, and with one hand he makes the gesture of homage. In his right hand he holds a club, and in his left he holds a noose. His tawny hair stands on end and his eyes are red. Below him is the Blessed One Vajradhara with his retinue. Translator's notes

The mountain called རི་བཏང་བཟུང is taken as Īṣādhara, one of the golden mountains around Meru. The identification is probable but not certain. Several names in the heart-mantra list and the svāhā litany are translated by meaning because a Sanskrit form could not be identified with confidence. These are notably "the Soaring One" (possibly Garuḍa), "the hand of cattle-increase," "the bringer of rain," "the son of the god of wealth" and "the goddess who turns back austerities." For "the bringer of rain," the Tibetan reads "rain-relying," likely a slip for "rain-showing/bringing." Agni is supplied as the figure surrounded by the fire sages in the southeast; the Tibetan names only the sages, but the later litany confirms Agni. The phrase "like a tawny moon" for the arrangement of the heart mantras is rendered literally. The opening "Then the Blessed One ... Vajrapāṇi" can also be read as "Then the Blessed One and the bodhisattva Vajrapāṇi"; the draft's reading is kept. The number "bye ba khrag khrig stong phrag brgya" is rendered loosely as "hundreds of thousands of trillions." The exact value of khrag khrig varies between Tibetan numerical lists. The list of harmful beings includes two items, "adepts" and "snake-holders," that do not match any standard class of spirit. They are translated literally, and their intended Sanskrit equivalents are uncertain. "Cutting grahas" renders the Tibetan literally; what the grahas cut is not stated. The epithet "Frenzied Wrathful One" follows the house rendering; the Tibetan word may be a scribal variant. The epithet "Who Overcomes Adversaries with Immeasurable Power" is taken with Mahābala, as in the earlier passage. Grammatically it could also qualify Vajrapāṇi. The wrathful deity ཁྲོ་བོ་ཆོལ་པ is rendered "the Frenzied Wrathful One." Its Sanskrit equivalent is uncertain, though the mantra's "ucchuṣma krodha" may correspond to it. The terms rendered "door bars, contraptions, and bolts" likely refer to magical binding devices destroyed by the vajra. "Sorcerers" (སྒྲུབ་པ) and "snake-holders" (སྦྲུལ་འཛིན) are given by their literal meaning because their Sanskrit equivalents are uncertain. སྲུལ་པོ is rendered pūtana and ལུས་སྲུལ་པོ kaṭapūtana, following the standard equivalences; when the two are not paired, the house list's pūtana for ལུས་སྲུལ་པོ is not applied. The nāga kings ནག་པོ and འཁོར་ལོ are translated literally as "Black One" and "Wheel," since they could not be identified with confidence. ལན་ཁྲིས is read as "ten thousand times." The list of samādhis ends without a main verb in the Tibetan. "Entering" these samādhis is implied. The exchange on the "general fruit" (abiding in the time of the fruit, for the sake of good qualities) is terse and obscure in the Tibetan; it is rendered literally. "Free of disease" renders bro nad, read as a form of "illness". "Collection of the Dharma" renders chos kyi phung po (Skt. dharmaskandha), a body or mass of teachings, not the dharmakāya. "Came forward" renders the unusual phrase དོང་བར་མཛད་ནས; its exact sense (approaching, assembling) is uncertain. "Like a house and like foam" follows the text as written; the "house" is an unusual item in this list of similes, and the reading may be corrupt. The phrase "the sublime attainment" interprets the bare word དམ་པ, "the sublime." "Preceded by the Dharma Realm" renders the title-like phrase "having the dharmadhātu going before." In the verse, "stake" renders a word that in ritual contexts means the kīla dagger. Here it is used figuratively for what pins beings to existence. The Tibetan does not say who sets the dying reader on the path to nirvāṇa. "They" is read as the tathāgatas of the preceding sentence.
