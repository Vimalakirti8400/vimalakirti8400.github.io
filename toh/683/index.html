About this text

Toh 683 in the Kangyur is a dhāraṇī text known in Sanskrit as the Ārya-Amogha-pāśa-hṛdaya-mahāyāna-dhāraṇī and in Tibetan as the Phakpa Dönyö Shakpé Nyingpo, meaning "The Heart of the Noble Infallible Lasso of the Great Vehicle." It belongs to the dhāraṇī section of the Kangyur and centers on the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara, whose emblematic attribute — the infallible lasso — symbolizes his power to rescue beings from suffering without fail. The text opens with a series of reverential homages to buddhas and bodhisattvas of the three times, to key figures such as Amitābha and Maitreya, and above all to Avalokiteśvara himself as the embodiment of great compassion, establishing a devotional frame for the dhāraṇī that follows. As a "heart" dhāraṇī, it distills the essence of a longer associated cycle and would have been used in ritual and contemplative practice within the Mahāyāna tradition.

The ritual application of this dhāraṇī is as follows. By reciting it over the dhāraṇī vase of Amogha-pāśa, one may confer benefit upon those who have been seized by malevolent obstructors, those who have been taken hold of by spirits and demons, those who have fallen under the power of harmful spells, women who miscarry and have no children, and those whose children do not survive, and so forth. In order to perform this ritual, one should take a maṇḍala at the center of a riverbank, the foot of a solitary tree, a crossroads, an empty house, or a similar place. The maṇḍala is square, with four gateways, and one opening — either to the west or to the north. It is drawn with a single white pigment. In the center, draw a white lotus with eight petals. Then take a vase of gold, silver, or copper — one that is uncracked, undamaged, or alternatively a clay vase with the neck sealed with a strip of black leather and having no cracks or flaws — and fill it with scented water. Into it pour five kinds of precious substances, five kinds of medicines, and five kinds of grain. Bind the neck with a strip of white silk whose edge has not been cut, and with a garland of flowers. Insert into its mouth a branch of birch or sandalwood adorned with flowers and fruits, and place the vase upon the lotus. The one who recites the dhāraṇī should visualize himself as the body of the noble Avalokiteśvara. He should then visualize that vase as a jeweled pond filled with nectarous water, and from within that water imagine a white lotus arising. Upon that lotus he should visualize the seed syllable of the noble Avalokiteśvara as a white letter, and from that letter he should visualize the arising of the noble Avalokiteśvara himself. He should then make offerings and praises, and supplicate with the words: "May whatever I am accomplishing now be fulfilled." He should then recite the Amogha-pāśa dhāraṇī over the vase thirteen times without speaking any other words in between. If the water from that vase is then poured over the crown of the person's head, they will be liberated from obstructors, demons, and the like. One should pour it over their head along with their clothing. At the time of pouring, the one who recites the dhāraṇī and the one who receives the recitation both have their outer and inner bodies purified.

One should recite with single-pointed mind, thinking that one will be freed from spirits and demonic afflictions. The wet clothing should be discarded as a means of casting away defilement. One then puts on fresh white clothing, adorns the body with incense, flowers, and the like, and by reciting the dhāraṇī a single time the body is protected. One should also recite the auspicious verses.

Around the circumference of that maṇḍala, the eight World-Protectors are each invoked by name, and offerings are made to them. Then one makes the aspiration: "May whatever I am now accomplishing be fulfilled." One makes offerings with a single fivefold offering, and to the common spirits one gives a generous torma offering.

The self-protection and the setting of boundaries are likewise to be performed properly using this same Amogha-pāśa dhāraṇī. This practice is appropriate to perform on the eighth day of the lunar month.

Not only does this rite liberate those who are afflicted by obstructors and the rest, as described above, but if it is performed for an ordinary person it will also render them thereafter impervious to obstructors and magical attack, prevent them from being struck by contagious illness, and cause their glory to be great and all their wishes to be fulfilled.

Among the five precious substances: gold, coral, pearl, conch, and crystal. Among the five medicines: siṃha, vyāghrī, girikarṇī, saha, and sahadeva. Among the five grains: barley, rice, sesame, wheat, and black lentil.

This concludes the dhāraṇī called the Heart of the Noble Amogha-pāśa.

Translator's notes

"The son of Śāradvatī" refers to the arhat Śāriputra, whose epithet "great wisdom" and patronymic are both invoked here, which is consistent with the standard liturgical homage sections of dhāraṇī texts of this type.

"Mahāpaśupativedhadhara" literally means "bearer of the piercing of the Great Lord of Beasts," an epithet associated with Śiva incorporated into the dhāraṇī's divine retinue invocation. The dhāraṇī addresses and subsumes a wide array of Brahmanical deities — Soma, Āditya, Yama, Varuṇa, Kubera, Brahma, Indra, Vāyu, Agni, Rudra, Sanatkumāra, and Vināyaka — as part of the mantra's protective scope, which is characteristic of this genre of Mahāyāna dhāraṇī literature.

The closing terms "indriya, bala" (sense faculties, power) appear abruptly and likely mark the beginning of a further section of the dhāraṇī that continues beyond the passage provided here. The phrase rendered "Amitābhasutāya" (literally "son of Amitābha") is an unusual epithet applied to Avalokiteśvara in his role as the emanation of Amitābha; the Tibetan reads ཨ་མི་ཏཱ་བྷ་སུ་ཏཱ་ཡ which supports this reading.

The phrase ཁམ་པའི་བུམ་པས་ར་པ་སོ་བཏང་བའི་ནག་པོ is somewhat technical and concerns the sealing of a clay vase's neck with a strip of black leather pressed into the rim; the translation reflects the most plausible ritual reading. The five medicines listed — siṃha, vyāghrī, girikarṇī, saha, and sahadeva — are Sanskrit plant names transliterated into Tibetan. Their exact botanical identifications vary across traditions and are not rendered here beyond transliteration, as any further specification would go beyond what the text warrants.

The colophon formula "Oṃ ye dharmā…" is the celebrated verse summarizing dependent origination, standard in Buddhist colophons. "Oṃ supratiṣṭha vajrāye svāhā" is a standard consecration mantra. "Maṅgalaṃ" means "auspiciousness" and "Śubhaṃ" means "good fortune" — both are conventional closing benedictions.
