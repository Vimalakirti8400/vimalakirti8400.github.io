About this text

The Maṇḍala Rite of the Commentary on the Glorious Hevajra Text, known in Sanskrit as the Śrī Hevajra Sapattati Maṇḍala Vidhi and in Tibetan as Dpal dgyes pa'i rdo rje'i gzhung 'grel gyi dkyil 'khor gyi cho ga, is Toh 1254 in the Tengyur, where it belongs among the commentaries on the tantras. It is a ritual manual for the Hevajra Tantra, opening with homage to Vajraḍāka and a verse invoking the sixteen-armed, eight-faced Hevajra, and it explains that it offers an extremely condensed rite for those who suffer and cannot study many texts. Its instructions guide the practitioner through the maṇḍala ritual, the fire offering, and the consecration of sacred images, bells and rosaries. According to its colophon it was composed by the master Kṛṣṇa, and was translated into Tibetan by the Indian preceptor Kṛṣṇa together with the chief editor-translator Gö Lhetsé.

Translation

In the language of India: śrī he bajra sa patta ti maṇḍa la bi dhi. In the language of Tibet: The Maṇḍala Rite of the Commentary on the Glorious Hevajra Text.

Homage to glorious Vajraḍāka.

Colored like the clouds at the time of the world's destruction, with sixteen arms and eight fierce faces,
his four reddish legs, the right extended in the half-crossed posture, pressing upon the hearts of the māras,
wearing a tiger skin, possessed of the qualities of the nine moods, with a garland of heads and bearing skull cups,
destroyer of the terrors of existence, terrifying and laughing: may Hevajra protect you always.

Since most people, tormented by suffering,
are not able to study many texts,
therefore this extremely condensed maṇḍala rite of the commentary,
which destroys whatever is not excellent,
I shall set out here according to my ability.

First of all, there is the master: one skilled in the way of secret mantra, whose intention is to draw beings out of the suffering of saṃsāra; who, having realized the sphere of reality through the power of hearing, reflection and meditation, has rendered all objects without exception free of poison; who, by increasing the accumulation of wisdom that is put into practice, has removed the cataract of ignorance that arises from mistaken conception; and who possesses the luminosity of intrinsic nature. To such a master the student should make supplication up to three times for the drawing of the maṇḍala.

Then the master should drive away the obstructors that arise from the student's mind by the splendor of the seed syllable at his own heart, protect him by reciting the root mantra or the garland mantra one hundred and eight times, and perform the preliminary approach by reciting the root mantra of the Wheel-bearer and the mantra of Nairātmyā one hundred thousand times each.

After that he goes to the site of the maṇḍala. There the yogin, endowed with the yoga of his own deity, meditates on a sun disc arisen from raṃ, and on it imagines a crossed vajra arisen from hūṃ. From the sun and the vajra he makes the ground into the nature of vajra, and he should sprinkle it and cleanse it with water over which the root mantra has been recited.

Then, with the light rays of the seed syllable at his own heart, he summons the earth goddess Pṛthivī, yellow in color and peaceful in nature, holding a golden vase in her two hands, and makes offerings to her:

Worshipped by Hevajra, the Wrathful One,
you who hold various jewels,
goddess, be the witness:
on this ground I shall draw the maṇḍala.

With these words of the mantra he takes possession of the earth. Having mentally raised the earth goddess into the sky, he digs to a depth of about the knee, removes the pebbles and the like, and should fill the pit with that same earth. So it is said in the Compendium of Secret Reality:

Having dug to about the depth of the knee,
one should fill it with that same earth.
If there turns out to be earth left over,
it shows that accomplishment will come.

At the time of filling the pit one should also make offerings and tormas as far as one is able. This is the rite of filling the pit.

Then, on the ground of the maṇḍala that has been prepared, the master, endowed with the yoga of Hevajra, recites three times rakṣa rakṣa hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā. As taught before, he meditates on the vajra ground arisen from the sun and the crossed vajra, and should consecrate the ground with this: oṃ ma de ni bajrī bha ba bajra bandha hūṃ. This is the rite of purifying the ground.

Then the master enters the maṇḍala house, and the student supplicates him with the verses that come below, beginning "Great Bliss, you are my teacher." The master, endowed with the yoga of Hevajra, adorns himself with garlands of flowers, sandalwood paste, a crown and so forth, and takes up the vajra and bell. By the same rite, four students, visualized as Gaurī and the others, are placed in the four directions, and they should supplicate with four verses:

Peace of all the Tathāgatas,
abode of all the Tathāgatas,
O Lord, please explain the maṇḍala
that arises from Sattvavajra.

Perfectly complete in every characteristic,
free of all that is not a mark,
supreme body of Samantabhadra,
please explain the sacred maṇḍala.

Arisen from the supreme Dharma of peace,
purifying the conduct of wisdom,
supreme speech of Samantabhadra,
please explain the heart of the maṇḍala.

Great mind of all sentient beings,
stainless through natural purity,
supreme mind of Samantabhadra,
please explain the heart of the maṇḍala.

At the end of this supplication, with hūṃ bajrod tiṣṭha he raises the wheel of the maṇḍala into the sky. Then, himself endowed with the yoga of two-armed Hevajra, he meditates on a sun disc arisen from ma in his right eye and imagines a moon disc arisen from ṭa in his left eye, and looks over the whole ground of the maṇḍala with a wrathful gaze. Rising, he meditates on an upraised vajra arisen from hūṃ on the soles of his feet. Beginning from the quarter of Īśāna, he treads everywhere in the manner of a dance, with the vajra gait of the right-extended posture, the left-extended posture and so forth, and should then speak thus to the obstructors dwelling on the ground of the maṇḍala:

hūṃ All you hosts of obstructors
dwelling in body, speech and mind, listen to me!
I am the power of the glorious vajra.
By fully applying the protection wheel,
with this body of blazing vajra,
arisen from the three kāyas, I shall drive you back.
If you transgress my word,
you will be destroyed, without a doubt.

Having said this, he also says: "By the mere action arisen from the wisdom of the great bliss of Vajrasattva, whoever you may be, you great hosts of obstructors, kaṭapūtanas and the rest, do not stay here: depart!" This is taking full possession of the ground.

Then, in order to pacify the obstructors, he stands in the center of the maṇḍala ground. He sends out ten Vighnāntakas, the lower parts of whose bodies have the form of ritual daggers, summons the obstructors of the ten directions, and strikes them with the daggers by the dagger mantra: oṃ gha gha gha ta ya ghā ta ya sarbba duṣṭan phaṭ kī la ya kī la ya sarbba pā paṃ phaṭ hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ bajra kī la ya bajra dha ro ā jñā pa ya ti kā ya bā ka citta bajra kī li kī la ya hūṃ phaṭ. Outside the daggers he imagines a vajra fence, a net and so forth, and should protect it with the mantra rakṣa rakṣa and the rest.

Then, having again worshipped the earth goddess Pṛthivī with flowers and the other articles of offering, one should establish the earth goddess in the preparatory rite with these two verses:

Worshipped by Hevajra, the Wrathful One,
you who hold various jewels,
goddess, be the witness:
on this ground I shall draw the maṇḍala.

and:

Just as Śākyasiṃha, the Protector,
destroyed the power of Māra,
so I too, having triumphed
over Māra, shall draw the maṇḍala.

Then the maṇḍala ground is smeared with a sweet fragrance mixed with the five nectars, and at the respective places of the Blessed One and the yoginīs one makes small maṇḍalas of the five nectars. One mentally summons the wheel dwelling in the sky, in its proper order, and requests it to be seated. Again one performs the sealing of the boundaries and so forth, and having worshipped perfectly with whatever outer and inner offerings are available, one should praise with these verses:

Seeing beings oppressed by delusion,
in order to fully pacify the delusion of beings,
the Vajra Holder took the form of Gaurī;
therefore I praise Gaurī and bow to her.

Seeing beings oppressed by aversion,
in order to pacify the aversion of beings,
the Vajra Holder took the form of Caurī;
therefore I praise Caurī and bow to her.

Seeing beings oppressed by pride,
in order to pacify the pride of beings,
the Lord took the form of Vetālī;
therefore I praise Vetālī and bow to her.

Seeing beings stirred up by attachment,
in order to pacify the attachment of beings,
the Lord took the form of Ghasmarī;
therefore I praise Ghasmarī and bow to her.

To the form nondual with Nairātmyā
I bow, I offer praise, I bow and pay homage.
Self of the five wisdoms and the three kāyas,
Vajraḍāka, I bow to you and praise you.

Then one circumambulates, and holding incense mixed with the five nectars, one sets the right knee firmly on the ground and should speak thus:

hūṃ Heruka, glorious great hero,
king of knowledge, I bow and pay homage.
Protector, I wish to draw
the maṇḍala whose nature is compassion.
Out of love for the disciples
and in order to make offerings to you,
Blessed One, I am devoted;
therefore it is fitting that you grant your kindness.
Buddhas who act for the good of the wheel of beings,
please think of me.
Bodhisattvas who abide in the fruit,
whatever other mantra deities there are,
gods who protect the world,
whatever bhūtas there are of great power,
beings who delight in the teaching,
and all of you who possess the vajra eye:
by your accepting this great vajra,
the maṇḍala from which Heruka arises
I draw, with whatever materials are at hand,
for the sake of beings; therefore,
toward me together with my disciples
showing compassion and care,
all of you, together with the maṇḍala,
considering us with compassion, draw near.

By reciting these verses three times one should cause the deities to abide there. This is the rite of establishing the deities in the preparatory rite.

Then one recites the root mantra and the garland mantra as much as one can, and should purify the inner ground through meditative absorption. At dawn, having again made offerings, praises and so forth, one mentally raises into the sky the wheel of deities abiding in the fragrance maṇḍala and requests them to depart. Then, in this sequence, one should lay the lines of the square maṇḍala. The sequence is as follows:

First, having laid the two Brahmā lines,
lay the two diagonal lines after them.
Then, in keeping with the size of the maṇḍala,
lay it out as a square.
On the two sides, by the Brahmā line,
mark one eighth of the wheel for the door.
Then on one side, measuring upward,
the mental line should be laid with the colored thread.
Then again, at half the door,
with the colored thread at a quarter of the door,
the second thread should be drawn,
and at that measure the wall line is drawn.
Then the door is made three measures.
The thread for the abodes of the deities
is laid at the corners, equal to the door.
The five kinds of lines of the walls
are laid all around at a quarter of the door.
All around, the border is half a door;
the jewel ground is half of that.
The net, the half net and the strip
are laid equal to the ground of the border.
The ground of the parapet
is drawn all around at half of that.
Once it has been drawn thus, the division of the lines,
since it is clearly explained elsewhere,
is not set out here;
each should divide it for themselves.

Then a thread, well dyed in color with five colored strands, the nature of the five wisdoms, is well twisted and cleaned, and made twice the length of the maṇḍala. Again the master meditates on himself as two-armed Hevajra, imagines in his right and left eyes the discs of the sun and moon, arisen from ma and ṭa, and with a blazing gaze summons all the Tathāgatas. He makes them enter the thread, binds them and brings them under control, anoints it with the five nectars, winds it into a bundle, protects it with the root mantra, and should place it in a clean vessel. So it is said in the Compendium of Reality:

The eye urged on very swiftly,
which summons with the eyelashes,
is what is called the blazing gaze;
it summons all beings.

Then, after that, with oṃ a bha ga vā na śa satva sū pra pra ya ccha svā ca kra sū tra na ya hūṃ he takes the wisdom thread from the maṇḍala dwelling in the sky, and the two are understood as following one another, saying:

All phenomena follow one another;
all phenomena, entirely,
follow: so all phenomena are declared.

Having said this, he twists it together with the student, who is endowed with the yoga of Gaurī. Then with jaḥ three times he hands it to the student. Himself saying the same, he holds in his left fist the thread wound around the vajra, and standing in the west, together with the student standing in the east, he lays the first Brahmā line, saying oṃ bajra sū tra sa ma ya mā ti kra ma hūṃ and:

At the time of acting for the good of beings,
the Tathāgatas who dwell in the sky,
in order to make them come here,
are to be urged by the sound of the line thread.

When the line is struck thus in the air, the Tathāgatas are urged by the sound of the line thread and come. Then, going in the clockwise direction, standing in the direction of Yama, he lays the second Brahmā line with the student standing in the north, and after that he lays the others in the same way.

Then he goes to the fire corner, and with the student standing in the corner of Īśāna he lays the line of the eastern side. Again going clockwise, he goes to the wind corner, and with the student standing in the corner of Nairṛti he lays the line of the western side. Staying right there, the student goes to the direction of Īśāna, and he lays the line of the northern side. Again going clockwise to the fire corner, with the student standing in the corner of Nairṛti, he lays the line of the southern side. Again staying right there, with the student standing in the wind corner, he lays the first diagonal line; then going clockwise to the corner of Nairṛti, with the student standing at Īśāna, he lays the second diagonal line.

Then the master stands in the center and doubles the line thread. The student, beginning from the corner of Īśāna, circles the outer wheel in two rounds, and again the master himself, beginning from the corner of Īśāna, should circle the inner maṇḍala all around in two rounds.

Then, filled with pride in himself, looking with the wrathful gaze of eyes wrinkled in anger beneath contracted brows, he opens the doors by saying hi, and with the mantra oṃ āḥ hūṃ bajra muḥ he requests the wisdom thread to depart. This is the rite of laying the wisdom lines.

Likewise, five colored powders in the colors of the five Tathāgatas, made from gold and the like, or if those are not available, from rice flour and the like, are finely ground, perfumed with sweet fragrance and placed in skull cups. As before, he summons all the Tathāgatas with the wrathful gaze, dissolves them into the powders according to their colors, and consecrates them with oṃ bajra ci tra sa ma ye hūṃ. Reciting the mantra oṃ bajra be ga, he mentally raises the wisdom thread of the maṇḍala into the sky, enters inside, and says:

This sphere of reality is utterly pure;
the realm of sentient beings is liberated.
The heart maṇḍala of Hevajra,
the vast, I shall now draw.

Holding each of the colored powders separately in the fist of his left hand, and beginning from the corner of Īśāna, he draws clockwise, without the lines touching one another and without wavering. The order is this: on the inside, black; outside that, white; then yellow; then red; and last, green. Having drawn the five lines a little in this way, he covers them with a clay bowl or the like. Afterwards the students spread the colors as they please, and reciting the words of vajra force, they mentally raise the lines into the sky and cause them to come forth and to enter.

The ground of the heart maṇḍala, encircled by a vajra garland, is made blue. The eastern part is made black, the southern part red, the western part yellow, and the northern part green. For the deities of the corner of Īśāna, one draws a black lion on a multicolored lotus. Likewise in the fire corner a white monk; in the corner of Nairṛti a cloud-colored wheel; in the wind corner a wrathful vajra. At the eastern door a black curved knife; in the south a red hand drum; in the west a yellow tortoise; and in the north a green snake. In the center, on a multicolored lotus, one draws a skull cup of three segments marked with a crossed vajra, and a blue curved knife. In the directions of two-armed Heruka and the rest, one draws blazing vajras. In the maṇḍala of Nairātmyā, one draws blue curved knives at the places of the yoginīs. This is the rite of applying the colors.

Then at dawn, into clean black vases free of blemishes, with wide bellies and with necks, on which their respective emblems have been drawn, one puts five kinds each of grains, jewels and medicines. Abiding in meditative absorption as far as one is able, one should consecrate them with their respective mantras interspersed with the three syllables. Barley, wheat, peas, rice grains and sesame are the five grains. Gold, silver, coral, pearl and lapis lazuli are the five jewels. Bṛhatī, kaṇṭakārī, white aparājitā, hasa and hasadeva are the five kinds of medicine.

The sequence of their consecration is this. The vases are filled completely with water mixed with sweet fragrance and the five nectars and purified with the three syllables. Their necks are tied with cloth of various colors, their mouths are adorned with fresh leaves, and they are decked with garlands of various flowers. Into the victorious vase one places the lord of the maṇḍala, and into the others the deities of the maṇḍala. On top of the victorious vase one places a vajra, and having offered flowers, incense, the welcoming water, water for the feet and so forth, one should consecrate them by reciting the mantras as much as one can, as before. jaḥ hūṃ baṃ hoḥ khaṃ raṃ baṃ oṃ a is the mantra of its welcoming water. bajra puṣpe hūṃ svāhā and so forth are the mantras for the flowers and the rest. Then, having given the torma, one should consecrate the student. This is the rite of establishing the vases in the preparatory rite.

Here, apart from the old, children, kings and one's own sons, a student who keeps his own training and has faith in the Great Vehicle and so forth, having been thoroughly examined, makes a maṇḍala offering, sets his knees on the ground, and is placed at the door of the maṇḍala with palms joined. One should proclaim this verse to the student:

Whoever seeks the accomplishment of mantra
should be brought into this maṇḍala.
Others, apart from these, desiring merit,
seek the good of self and others and the world beyond.
Those endowed with great faith,
the intelligent who aim at the world beyond,
should be brought into the maṇḍala;
they should not seek the fruit of this life.
If one seeks the fruit of this life,
the world beyond will not be accomplished.
If one aims at the world beyond,
the fruit of this life is accomplished without effort.

Then the master mentally brings the student into his own body. Having emerged by the vajra path, the student, by his own light, abides in the heart of the lotus of wisdom; the master meditates that all the Tathāgatas confer empowerment on him, and draws him forth again. In order to enter the maṇḍala, the student should say this:

Great Bliss, embraced at the neck by Nairātmyā,
you are my teacher.
Steadfast in the way of great awakening,
great hero, I make my request to you.

Grant me that very pledge (samaya),
and let me receive the mind of awakening as well.
Of Heruka together with Nairātmyā,
sovereign lady of the heroes and heroines,
please explain the true secret abodes and the rest,
the rite of the sites within the body.

Then the master should say this to the student:

Since you are a vessel of the great way,
come here, my son: the rite,
the way of conduct of secret mantra
of the Great Vehicle, I shall rightly teach you.
The vajra holders of body, speech and mind,
the buddhas who arise in the three times,
through the power of the secret mantra vajra
rightly found incomparable wisdom.
Through the incomparable practice of secret mantra
the supreme ones such as Śākyasiṃha
conquered even the very fierce
and mighty hosts of Māra;
going forth in accord with the world,
they turned the wheel and passed into nirvāṇa.
Therefore, in order to attain omniscience,
my son, form this resolve.

Then the student says:

I go for refuge to the Three Jewels.
I confess each and every misdeed.
I rejoice in the virtue of beings.
I take buddha awakening to heart.

Having said this three times, the student is protected with the root mantra. With the syllables oṃ āḥ hūṃ, one visualizes Bhūcarī, Khecarī and Nairātmyā at the crown of the head, the throat and the heart. Then, holding the vajra in the right fist, the master rises, anoints the heart, the throat and the crown with sandalwood and so forth, recites the three syllables, and touches the three places with the vajra. Then, with the accompanying mantras, he gives a flower at the crown, incense in front, unguent at the heart, and a lamp before the eyes. He gives a tooth stick twelve finger-widths in length, made from a wood such as aśvattha or udumbara, without cracks or breaks and straight, consecrated by reciting the root mantra over it. Having chewed the tooth stick, the student faces east and throws it straight down onto a maṇḍala smeared with cow dung about the size of a cowhide. If its tip points toward himself, the supreme accomplishment will be attained; if it points east, the middling one will be attained. The other directions are said to indicate the attainment of worldly accomplishments.

Then scented water is consecrated with oṃ hrī biśuddha sarbadharmā sarbapāpaṃ niṣcaśodhaya sarbabikalpa apanaya hūṃ, and he is given it to drink three times. Kuśa grass over which the syllable dhī has been recited is given as a pillow, and that over which the syllable ha has been recited is given as a mat. A thread with three knots, over which the syllable hūṃ has been recited, is tied on the upper arm, with oṃ bajrasatva samayamanupālaya bajrasatva tvenopatiṣṭha dṛḍho me bhaba sutoṣyo me bhaba anurakto me bhaba supoṣyo me bhaba śāśvato me bhaba hṛdaya me adhitiṣṭha sarba siddhimme prayaccha sarba karma su ca me cittaṃ śriyaḥ kuru hūṃ ha ha ha ha hoḥ bhagavāna bajra mā me mañca bajri bhaba mahāsamayasatva āḥ.

Then enthusiasm should be roused with these verses:

In the world, the omniscient ones,
like the flower of the udumbara,
only once in a long while
appear, or else they do not appear.
The arising of the way of secret mantra conduct
is rarer even than that,
for by it the incomparable welfare of beings,
the unsurpassed, can be accomplished.
Whatever evil was done before
over many tens of millions of eons,
if all of it is exhausted
as soon as one sees a maṇḍala such as this,
what need is there to speak of abiding
in the ways of mantra conduct, endless in number?
If one recites the secret mantras of the protectors,
one comes to the unsurpassed state.
Whoever has a most stainless mind
toward this supreme conduct
will thereby cut off the lower realms
from which all sufferings arise.
Great beings, today you have obtained
an incomparable gain,
for all the great beings,
the Victors together with their heirs,
have taken all of you into their care,
as though giving you birth, in this teaching.
Therefore you will go
excellently into the Great Vehicle.
This supreme path, from which the Great Vehicle
arises in greatness, is glorious;
since you have entered into it,
you will become tathāgatas.

After that, the student is protected with the root mantra, joins his palms, and, having spoken these words, is made to take the special vow:

Through the empowerment of the irreversible wheel,
the protector has bestowed it upon me,
and has taught the reality of the deities of the wheel
and the whole activity of the master;
he has taught the pledges of all the buddhas
and the unsurpassed vow as well.

Then the master instructs him, "Tell me in the morning whatever dream you see," and sends him, with intense devotion to the deity, to his own quarters. This is the rite of the student's preparation.

Then the master enters the maṇḍala house, and, in order to protect the student, recites the mantra of the Wheel-bearer and offers a torma. In the morning he rises early, summons the student, and determines whether the dreams were auspicious or inauspicious. If inauspicious ones were seen, he protects him by reciting the root mantra.

When the drawing with colored powders is finished, he bathes in fragrant water and puts on clean clothes, requests the remnants of the colored powders to depart with oṃ āḥ hūṃ bajra muḥ, and sets them in a secluded place. The area outside the maṇḍala is smeared with cow dung, sprinkled and purified with fragrant water, and strewn with loose flowers. Then, having stretched out a canopy and arranged banners and so forth in their proper places, he sets the vases in their places. The victory vase is placed inside, in the east. The vase for all activities, for sprinkling and purifying all the people, is marked with a vajra, the three syllables are recited over it, and it is placed to the right of the maṇḍala's door. Further, a little water from each of the vases is poured into the argha vessel, which is placed on top of the victory vase. Then, having laid out whatever offering articles are available, such as butter lamps, the master sits on a soft seat inside the door of the maṇḍala house and should begin the accomplishment of the maṇḍala in the following sequence.

One takes a girl of one's own family, twelve or sixteen years old, or else another, such as the mother and the rest, endowed with the good qualities, and performs the practice; or, if none is available, a consort in meditative absorption will also do. Having generated oneself as set out in the sādhana, up to the empowerment, and preceded by the blessing of vajra and lotus, one enters into union with the wisdom consort, and with the sound of bliss one summons all the tathāgatas and makes them enter one's own body. One emanates them so that they pervade all of space in the manner of the circle of the maṇḍala, draws them back again, and sets them upon the maṇḍala. With the mantra of the Eight-Faced One one dispels the obstructors. Then, with the mantras oṃ bajra gaurī ākarśaya jaḥ, oṃ bajra caurī prabeśaya hūṃ, oṃ bajra betālī bandhaya baṃ and oṃ bajra ghasmarī baśiṃ kuru hoḥ, Gaurī and the others, holding the hook and so forth, summon the wisdom maṇḍala from space into the deities of their respective emblems, cause it to enter, bind it, and bring it under control; and one should make it stable with the hundred-syllable mantra.

After that, one offers argha and worships them properly with flowers and so forth. At the end, with oṃ āḥ bajra vasta hūṃ, one offers the deities a pair of cloths. Having worshiped them again with the inner offering, one rings the vajra and bell and praises them with this:

Akṣobhya, great wisdom,
vajra sphere, great space,
supreme of the three vajras, the three maṇḍalas,
Vajraḍāka, I praise you and bow down.
Vairocana, great purity,
vajra peace, great joy,
by nature the supreme clear light,
Buddhaḍāka, I praise you and bow down.
King of jewels, most profound,
vajra space without stain,
pure by nature and untainted,
Ratnaḍāka, I praise you and bow down.
Vajra deathless one, great king,
nonconceptual space, vajra holder,
who has reached the far shore of desire,
Padmaḍāka, I praise you and bow down.
Amoghavajra, perfect buddha,
who fulfills every wish,
arisen from the very purity of things,
Viśvaḍāka, I praise you and bow down.

Having praised them with this, one should delight oneself and the deities of the maṇḍala by sprinkling them with the nectar that abides in the lotus of the wisdom consort.

Then one stations the ritual assistant in the maṇḍala, goes outside oneself, and offers the extensive torma in the following sequence. First one imagines a blue wind maṇḍala arisen from yaṃ, shaped like a bow and marked with banners, and above it one imagines a triangular fire maṇḍala arisen from raṃ. One imagines that in a skull cup generated from a and set upon it the samaya substances are blessed with oṃ, and in the space above one meditates on a blazing vajra arisen from hūṃ. As the wind below kindles the fire, the substances, together with the vajra above, melt; one imagines that they become of one taste and luminous. With its light one draws forth nectar from the seed syllables abiding in the hearts of all the buddhas without exception who pervade every world, makes it descend into it, and so accomplishes it as supreme nectar.

Then one beholds in space the great seal of the source of phenomena, marked with an eight-petaled lotus. Gaurī and the others, arisen from the seed syllable in one's own heart and holding their emblems, summon all the bhūtas, make them enter there, bind them, and bring them under control. On the lotus one imagines Brahmā and the other gods. On the lotus petals one places the guardians of the respective directions. Below the lotus one imagines humans, pretas, animals and hell beings, down to the eight nāgas. All of these one generates, either as set out in the sādhana or by the instantaneous procedure, into the bodies of deities holding vajra and bell, with the right leg extended. One sees them empowered by Locanā and the others, and seals them. Here Brahmā and the others are white, marked with the body vajra; Indra and the others are black, marked with the mind vajra; the nāgas and the others are red, marked with the speech vajra. Having worshiped them with the outer and inner offerings, one praises them, and, imagining on the lotus petal of the tongue of each of them light rays from a vajra born of hūṃ, one should give the offering with this mantra:

oṃ inda jama jala jakkha bhūta bāhni bāu rakkha canda sujja māda bappa tāla pātālaṃ aṣṭa sabba svāhā idaṃ maliṃ bhuñja jiṃghaṃ phulla dhūbba māṃsa piṃgha aṃbha sappa kajja sādha khanti khuṇi phaḍa gada oṃ akāro mukhaṃ sarbadharmāṇāṃ ādyanutpannatvāta oṃ āḥ hūṃ phaṭ svāhā

Entering inside once more, one mentally receives from the lord of the maṇḍala everything from entry into the maṇḍala up to the authorization. Then one says:

Knowing yourself that this very wheel,
arisen from great passion, is a reflection,
please set it upon the Lord,

supreme one, without beginning or end,
of the nature of great Sattvavajra,

Supreme of all great accomplishments,
Maheśvara, god above the gods,

great passion, great joy,

Utterly pure, supreme of all,
Tathāgata, liberated from the very beginning,
Samantabhadra, supreme of all,
accomplish me as the mind of awakening!
Supreme of all great accomplishments,
Maheśvara, supreme of seals,

vajra-proud one, lord of lords,
pervading the minds of all beings,
dwelling in the hearts of all beings,
the very father of all beings,
supreme pledge of passion's sameness!

Whatever blessing of truth there is
in the maṇḍala whose nature is wisdom and means,

The deity on whom it has fallen is to be known from the drawing of the places, and if it falls between two, he is to be known as belonging to the family of whichever is nearer. If it falls outside the maṇḍala, minor accomplishments are achieved. If it falls on a line, one has him throw again. diligently opens your eyes;
the unsurpassed vajra eye

look, look, great being!

otherwise the accomplishments

and prostrates. hold the vajra, for perfect accomplishment,

Whatever appears, each and every thing,
is glorious Heruka himself, the principal,
because phenomena are completely pure,
the very nature of the wisdom of the buddhas.

gather together the assemblies of students.

In this vehicle of great bliss
there is no death hereafter,
and there is no failing to stand firm;
so be fearless in every way, and rejoice.
Since existence is utterly pure,
you have passed beyond the suffering of existence.
The awakening mind is not to be abandoned:
it is the mudrā called vajra;
by merely generating it
one becomes a buddha, without doubt.
The true Dharma is not to be disparaged;
it is never permissible to cast it aside.
With those who do not understand or who are deluded
one should not share that very reality.
Vajra and bell are not to be abandoned,
and one should never disparage the master.
Do not forsake your own self, equal to all the buddhas,
and torment it with austerities.
If you hold to bliss blissfully, just as you please,
this one is a future perfect buddha.

With this one gives reassurance. The student bows and promises: "All that the Lord has commanded, all of it I shall do." This is the rite of the master empowerment.

Then one should teach these three: the reality of the maṇḍala and the defining characteristics of the purity of the maṇḍala; the reality of the deities and the purity of the deities; and the activities of the master, that is, the acts of the master's entry that precede all accomplishment of the maṇḍala.

After that, one makes the place secluded and screens it off with a curtain. Having offered to the guru a consort who keeps the pledges, as taught in the tantra, the student says:

O Blessed One, great peaceful one,
truly arisen through vajra union,
perfectly accomplished in the indivisible mudrā,
having taken up the one vajra yoga,
great self, just as you are,
Lord, please make me so as well.
I am sunk in the mass of the mire of saṃsāra,
without refuge: protect me!

With this one makes the request for the conferral of the secret empowerment. Then:

The glorious master,
endowed with loving kindness,
having generated compassion for the student,
and to him who acts as Vajradhara,
with pleasing food and drink,
together with liquor and the great flesh,
with garlands of flowers and incense,
with banners and with bells,
with unguents and other offerings,
one worships the guru, the Vajra Holder.

After that one should enter into union according to the rite of the tantra and so on, and one should keep company with the mudrā for as long as she is endowed with the fluid. Having bound the eyes of the mudrā and of the student with blindfolds, one should let fall into the student's mouth whatever has arisen from that union, with this verse:

Just as the buddhas, the victors,
empowered the heirs of awakening,
so with the secret empowerment
please wash me with the stream of the mind.

He in turn says a ho sukha. One has him partake of it completely. The consort, the wisdom, should likewise give it to the student: wisdom and means alike are the secret empowerment.

After that the student again makes the request in the words of request given before, and she is made his sphere of practice. This too has been stated:

To the great-souled student,
free of miserliness and free of anger,
of equal taste in that very reality,
she should be made the student's sphere of practice.

Then the master places her hand in his hand, lays a hand on his head, and says:

This goddess, who abides in bestowing joy,
lovely, of manifold form:
take her, take her, great being!
Having taken her, worship her well.
By no other means does one become a buddha;
she purifies the three worlds.
Therefore you must never
be parted from her.
This is the unsurpassed knowledge discipline
of all the buddhas;
whatever fool abandons it,
for him there is no supreme accomplishment.

With this one gives her. She in turn opens her fragrant lotus, shows it to the student, and speaks thus:

Child, will you delight in eating
excrement and urine,
and likewise blood and semen,
and the great flesh that is shown?
Reverence toward women,
and kissing the lotus of the bhaga:
tell me, child, whether these are to your liking.

He in turn answers, saying:

Goddess, why should I not delight?
I eat semen, blood and the rest,
I revere women,
and I kiss the lotus of the bhaga.

She, delighted with the practitioner, looks at him directly and says:

Ah! This lotus of mine
is endowed with every bliss.
If one keeps company with it according to the rite,
I abide before him,
and the lotus performs, just as they should be,
the acts such as the pride of the buddhas.
The king of great bliss himself
truly abides right here.
bhañja mokṣa ho

After that one visualizes the means as Hevajra and the wisdom as Nairātmyā, blesses the eyes and other faculties of both, and blesses body, speech and mind. The vajra is generated from hūṃ and its jewel from oṃ, and one blesses it with oṃ vajra maha dveṣa catur ananda dāyaka khaga mukha ekaraso nāthā hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ karyaṃ kuruṣva me. One generates the lotus from hrī and blesses it with oṃ padma sukha dhara mahā rāga sukhaṃ dadha catur ānanda dā bhaga viśva hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ karyaṃ kuruṣva me. One explains the divisions of the channels and describes the natures of the moments. Then, with the swing (dola) and with the postures (karana) such as supratisurita, in the manner desired, arousing the lotus with the fingers of the limb of desire, as the Teacher has instructed, one generates bliss with sid sid sid ha ha ha kun du ru vajra dhṛ ka. At the time of release, when the student has found the experience, one should give a sign, and the guru teaches it with:

This wisdom is exceedingly subtle,
the vajra essence, equal to space,
stainless, bestowing liberation, peace itself;
you yourself are your own father.

Then the student takes up the nectar with the tongue, rises, circumambulates the guru, prostrates with the eight limbs, and makes the request:

Through the kindness of your feet,
Lord, I have obtained the three empowerments.
Today, here, Lord,
grant me the kindness of the fourth as well.

For the fourth he also offers whatever fee he can afford. Then the loving guru confers the vows of the pledges: the pledges of guarding, stated in words such as "You should kill living beings," and the pledges of eating, stated in words such as "the first na, the first ga," are the pledges that go with the wisdom-gnosis empowerment. The fourth is to be learned from the guru's own mouth, and so it is not taught here.

Since it has been said, Apart from the maṇḍala and the fire offering, no consecration is taught here, for the benefit of the student I shall explain the rite of the fire offering for whatever one desires. Of these: on white ground one digs a round hearth one cubit across and twelve finger-widths deep. At its center, on an eight-petaled lotus six finger-widths wide and three finger-widths high, is a crossed vajra resting on a skull cup. On the petals one arranges the signs of the goddesses, and one should surround it with a garland of vajras. Outside the hearth one leaves four finger-widths and should surround it with a rim of that same width and height, marked with vajras. The four corners of the platform are marked with three-pronged vajras. One smears it all over with earth suited to the rite and smears it also with scent suited to the rite. One should strew white flowers over it at the three times of day. This is the hearth for pacifying. For increasing, on yellow ground one digs a square two cubits across and half that in depth, and makes the rim twice the former; the rest is as before.

Then one smears it with yellow earth and yellow scent, and should strew it with yellow flowers. For subjugation, on red ground one digs a triangular hearth twenty finger-widths across and ten finger-widths deep, and surrounds it with a rim three finger-widths wide. The rest is as taught before. Then one smears it with red earth and red scent and strews it with red flowers at the three times of day. For summoning and for killing it is likewise. Their distinctive features should now be stated. The site should be black and barren, cracked and saline. One smears it with black earth, with ash from the charnel ground, and with as much donkey and camel dung as one can find, and it should be foul smelling; and one should strew it with scentless black and red flowers and with chaff.

Of these, to the east of the maṇḍala or of the temple one should make the hearths for pacifying and for subjugation; to the south, that for increasing; to the north, that of the fierce rite; and to the west, that for summoning. It is also seen that one may make a hearth suited to the rite with colored powders. This is the rite of the hearth.

Now the filling ladle and the pouring ladle are taught. They may be made of gold or copper, or else of the wood used for sacrifice. At the end of the handle one should make a jeweled vajra six finger-widths long. The shaft is sixteen finger-widths. On the upper part of the shaft is a vajra of four finger-widths. The bowl is four finger-widths long and two finger-widths deep, marked at its center with a five-pronged vajra, and at its tip it has a spout the size of the little finger from which the essence drips. This is the filling ladle. The pouring ladle is shaped like a lotus petal, two finger-widths wide and one finger-width deep, marked at its center with a five-pronged vajra; the rest is as before. The handles of both should be such as can be grasped with the vajra fist. In this way each of them is taught to be one cubit long. If there is no filling ladle and pouring ladle, the fire offering of filling and pouring should be done with one hand with its five fingers spread, and the other with three fingers drawn together.

For this, the fire sticks are made from the tip portions of mango, udumbara, kadamba, palāśa, aśvattha, plakṣa, nyagrodha, vidaṅka, śamī, kasamarga and apāmārga: fresh, about a span in length, with their leaves. Both ends are dipped in milk, honey and butter, and the sticks are sprinkled with water of white scent. Holding a fire stick that has a single tip and two leaves between the thumb and the ring finger, one should burn it. Here one should reject sticks that are rotten, cracked, broken, without bark, crooked, two-tipped, too short or too long, eaten by insects, or with insects in them. Further, barley, parched rice, curd, milk, sesame, fresh kuśa grass, dūrvā grass, mango leaves, sandalwood, cooked rice and white flowers, together with butter: burning whatever of these one can obtain, these are the burnt substances for pacifying.

For increasing, the fire sticks are like those, but taken from the middle portion, a cubit long and broken off to that length, sprinkled with water of yellow scent and dipped in honey. Further, excellent foods, śrīphala fruit, śāli rice, gold, karṇikāra flowers and grains are the substances for increasing.

Fire sticks of five finger-widths with their leaves, from the campaka, aśvattha, bakula and priyaṅgu, along with flowers, sour and sweet fruits, and especially red utpala lotuses, are the substances for subjugation. For summoning, those same substances, and thorns shaped like hooks.

For the fierce rite: fire sticks ten finger-widths long, with both ends cut sharp, made from pungent and bitter trees such as the nimba, the khadira and the bilota, from thorny trees and from charnel-ground trees, smeared with blood, mustard oil and the like; and white mustard seed, black oil, salt, mustard, nimba leaves, charnel-ground ash, dust from the enemy's footprint, and donkey and camel dung, whatever of these there are, or all of them. Mixing them with poison, blood and salt, one makes an effigy of the enemy, and one should cut it up and burn it. This teaches the burnt substances.

The firewood for pacifying is wood for sacrifice free of insects, or else the dung of wild cattle, and it should be kept a little lower than the hearth. For increasing, it should be made from fruit-bearing trees, without cow dung. For subjugation, it should be made from sour trees and from flowering ones. For summoning, from those that have hooklike thorns. For the fierce rite, from pungent trees, from thorny charnel-ground trees, and from donkey and camel dung. This teaches the firewood.

Then, for the rite of pacifying, one bathes and cleanses oneself, perfumes the body with sweet scents, and adorns oneself with white clothes and ornaments. Endowed with loving-kindness, facing west, seated on a soft padded cushion or on a lotus, with a peaceful mind, one should perform it beginning at dawn on the first day of the waxing fortnight. For increasing, one adorns oneself with garlands of yellow flowers and yellow ornaments, sits on a fine seat in the auspicious posture, and with a pleased mind, facing north, one should perform it beginning in the morning. For subjugation, one adorns oneself with red clothes, ornaments and the like, and with a passionate mind, on a high seat, in the vajra posture, one should face east. Summoning is likewise, with the difference that one should perform it facing upward. For the fierce rite, with a wrathful mind, wearing blue clothes and ornaments stained with blood and water, one faces the direction of Yama, the south. One sits on a hard seat, on the heart of the effigy of the target, or else without any seat. Pressing the right foot with the left, gnashing the teeth, with a wrathful frowning face, rolling the eyes, on the second day of the waning fortnight or on another day, one should display wrath itself. In order to turn the target back from evil, at midday or at midnight, having seized the target's protection with the vajra hook arisen from hūṃ, one should perform it. This teaches the practitioner.

The vessel for the arghya water of pacifying may be of gold or silver, of copper, earth or stone, of conch, mother-of-pearl and the like, or of tree leaves. One pours into it water mixed with barley, milk and white scent, and should recite over it seven times the mantra of one's own deity ending with svāhā. For increasing it is likewise, with the difference that the water is of yellow scent, mixed with sesame and curd, and one recites the mantra ending with oṃ.

For subjugation the water is of red scent, and one recites the mantra ending with ho. For the fierce rite, over water that is foul smelling and has black substances in it, mixed with blood, cow's urine and kodrava grain, one recites the mantra ending with phaṭ. This is the rite of the arghya offering.

Here, for pacifying, increasing and subjugation, one recites the syllable hūṃ over the all-purpose water and places the two arghya vessels on the left. Then, taking with the right hand from those two waters over which that same syllable has been recited, one sprinkles in purification the burnt substances, the fire sticks, the firewood and so on, and places them on the right. For the fierce rite one places them the other way around.

For pacifying, one should use fire produced from milky trees, by friction and the like, or the fire of a brahmin's fire offering. For increasing, one should use the fire of the Saṅgha's fire room, or fire from the house of a kṣatriya or a vaiśya. For subjugation, one should use fire from the house of a vaiśya or of a courtesan. For summoning, one should use the fire of dancers or of wandering ascetics, or fire left lying on the road. For the fierce rite, one should use fire from charnel-ground wood, fire struck from stone, or fire from a butcher's house.

Then one takes up with the tip of a kuśa blade water over which hūṃ has been recited and sprinkles it three times. One puts the fire in together with clean, dry grass, kindles it with a fan, and feeds it with butter over which oṃ jvala jvala hūṃ has been recited seven times. One covers the outside of the hearth with fresh kuśa grass over which oṃ vajrasatva a has been recited, its leaves unbroken and not too long, with the tips pointing east. One generates the hearth from the syllable hūṃ, and within it, from raṃ, one generates the fire god, of a color suited to the rite, wearing ornaments and garlands, holding a rosary and a water flask in his two hands, with a hanging belly and a topknot of matted hair. With the mantra oṃ aganye mahā teja sarva kamartha prasadaka karuṇa kritva sarva asmin sanani hito bhava one invites the wisdom fire god, and with jaḥ hūṃ vaṃ hoḥ one draws him into that same one, and so on. With oṃ jaḥ hūṃ vaṃ hoḥ khaṃ raṃ one offers arghya water, and with oṃ niri hūṃ khaṃ one offers water for the feet. One performs everything from the blessing of the eyes and so forth up to the empowerment, worships him with outer and inner offerings, and with oṃ dhvaṃ dhvaṃ offers him the food offering.

Then one visualizes a vajra arisen from hūṃ on his tongue. His mouth slightly open, one inserts the two ladles, held at the two elbows, and with oṃ aganye svāhā one gives the fire offering of the full ladle. For the fierce rite, his mouth wide open, one gives black oil that is pungent and mixed with poison.

Then the wise should examine the characteristics of the fire. A color like a rainbow, like sātika, like a tail, like a reed or like crystal is auspicious for virtuous acts. Further, a fire that is clear, whose crackling has ceased, and that blazes to a single point is auspicious. Then, in particular, for pacifying the color is white; for increasing, a yellow color; for passionate attraction, red; and blue and black for the fierce rite.

Here, a fire beset by obstructors gives much smoke, throws off sparks, burns slowly and feebly, its flames break off, it is lusterless and bluish in color, it takes the shape of a spear point, of a whip, or of a cow's face, it smells of corpses or has no smell, or else it blazes violently. Knowing in this way that it is beset by obstructors, one sprinkles it in purification as before, protects it with the root mantra, and offers three or seven secret fire offerings. Then again one burns the fire sticks and the rest, each with its own mantra, as many as one wishes, offers purifying sprinkling, scattered drink offerings and the like, and should look on the fire god as having become the form of blazing fire.

Then, by the rite that comes from the sādhana, one generates there the deity taught in the tantra suited to the rite, invites the wisdom being, offers arghya and the rest, dissolves it into the pledge being, and blesses body, speech and mind. Visualizing the tongue as a vajra, for pacifying, increasing, subjugation and summoning one opens the mouth only slightly, and for the fierce rite one opens it wide, and one burns the offerings. Here, oṃ aganye svāhā is the mantra for butter. oṃ sarva pāpaṃ dahana vajrāya amukaṃ sarva pāpaṃ daha svāhā is that for sesame. oṃ vajra puṣṭaya svāhā is that for rice. oṃ vajra vijāya svāhā is that for millet. oṃ mahā vegāya svāhā is that for barley. oṃ mahā balāya svāhā is that for peas. oṃ vajra ghasmarīya svāhā is that for wheat. oṃ vajra āyuṣe svāhā is that for dūrvā grass. oṃ apratihata vajra svāhā is that for kuśa grass. oṃ vajra ajñāya svāhā is that for the udumbara. oṃ bodhi vṛkṣāya svāhā is that for the aśvattha. oṃ vajra lahaye svāhā is that for the plakṣa. oṃ vajra kuberaya svāhā is that for the nyagrodha. oṃ vajra sahakaraya svāhā is that for the mango.

Having burned in this way whatever one has, offered purifying sprinkling, scattered drink offerings and the like, and worshipped properly, one asks forbearance with these words:

What was lacking and what was impaired,
and whatever I with deluded mind
have done or caused to be done,
Protector, please forbear all of it.

Again one makes requests to the chosen deity for accomplishments, recites muḥ at the end of the hundred-syllable mantra, and requests the deity to depart. Then again one visualizes the fire god within the hearth, makes the fire offering with what remains of the burnt substances, and, together with the student, makes the final full-ladle fire offering. Then one makes requests for accomplishments:

Fire god, since you have duly done
what accomplishes the aim of your own nature,
keep us in mind in the abode of the pledge,
and please depart happily to your own abode.
Having accomplished the aims of myself and others,
partake of the fire offering and go.
Coming again at the proper time,
grant the accomplishments of my mantra.

This is the rite of the fire offering.

The nature of the Protectors
is nonabiding nirvāṇa;
yet for people endowed with eagerness,
so that mantra practitioners may gain wealth,
and so that, through people's deep faith,
the virtue of the patrons may increase,
the consecration taught in the treatises
is performed as a mere convention.

First, on an auspicious day, one performs the outer and inner bathing, anoints oneself with fragrant scents, adorns oneself with various ornaments, binds up the topknot, and arranges the articles of offering and so forth. Endowed with the yoga of the two-armed Heruka and holding vajra and bell, the master himself sits upon a square platform made two cubits high, and on that same platform he invites to be seated the cast images, books and other objects fit to be consecrated. Above, one stretches a canopy and hangs parasols, victory banners, pennants and the like; one heaps up garlands of flowers and loose blossoms, and adorns the occasion with song, dance and music. Then, in the afternoon, one should perform the bathing in this sequence.

First one clothes the image in a yellow robe. One recites hūṃ over white mustard seed, holds it in the right and left fists, circles it twice, and pours it into the fire in a clay bowl, and one makes the auspicious circling three times. Likewise one should also make the auspicious circling three times with water over which oṃ has been recited. Then one should anoint and smear it in sequence with the five nectars, or else with the five products of the cow. For painted scrolls, images in relief and the like, one anoints their reflection in a mirror.

Then one washes it with fragrant water and anoints it with fragrant black oil over which oṃ sarva tathāgata viśodhane vajra svāhā has been recited; one smears it with a paste made from the bark of the patra, the udumbara and other trees, and so wipes off the black oil. Then one smears it with a wash of emblic myrobalan, and performs the bathing with the auspicious verses explained before. One meditates that the buddhas and the knowledge goddesses, with their words, are performing the bathing as taught before, and one oneself also performs the outer bathing with the victory vase and the rest; then one should take the drops of water from the body of the image with a soft cloth.

Then one anoints it with fragrant scent with oṃ āḥ vajra gandhe hūṃ hūṃ hūṃ, and likewise presents the articles of offering, such as flowers generated from hūṃ. With the right hand holding the vajra one touches its heart, throat, head, shoulders and crown. Holding an incense bowl in the hand, one kneels with the knees on the ground, makes the tongue a vajra with hūṃ, and says:

Blessed One, Vajra So-and-so,
queen of knowledge, I bow to you and praise you.
Protector, whose very nature is compassion,
I ask leave to perform the consecration.
Out of love for the students,
and in order to worship you as well,
Blessed One, it is fitting that you
show kindness to me, who am devoted.
Buddhas, who turn the wheel for beings,
please heed me.
Bodhisattvas and arhats,
whatever other deities of secret mantra there are,
gods who protect the world,
bhūtas, and the Buddha's teaching,
and whatever beings, endowed with vajra eyes,
take delight in the teaching:
I, So-and-so, the Vajra,
with whatever articles I can obtain,
wish to perform, for the peace of beings,
the consecration of this one called the knowledge king.
Turning your compassion closely
toward me and the students,
endowed with compassion,
bless the whole of the consecration.

With this one makes the announcement to the deities. One first meditates that the images, the cast ones and the rest, are selfless, and then generates them as one's own deity, either through the sequence of moon, vajra and so on, or in a single instant. For books, one generates the letters as arisen from the transformation of the vajra of speech. Temples, stūpas and the like one imagines as arisen from the transformation of Vairocana. Then one summons the wisdom beings from the ten directions, offers arghya water, water for the feet and the rest with the mantras taught before, and makes them enter the heart of the image in the manner taught before. Likewise one confers the empowerment, seals it with the mudrā according to the yoga, and worships it properly with outer and inner offerings such as flowers and incense. One recites many times the mantra of whichever deity is being consecrated, says muḥ at the end of the hundred-syllable mantra, and requests the deities to depart again. This is the rite of the preparatory installation.

Then, rising early the next morning, the master, endowed with the yoga of his own deity, gathers all the articles of offering, recites the verses beginning "Blessed One, Vajra So-and-so," saying "I" in place of the word "tomorrow," and urges the deities. One should perform all the rites explained in the section on the preparatory installation, and should make it stable with the hundred-syllable mantra, but without the request to depart. Those who have not performed the preparatory installation should also do it in this way.

Then one performs the fire offering for increasing. At its end, one sprinkles in purification the student, who is placed on the left side, with water over which the mantra of Vighnāntaka has been recited. Holding the right thumb with the left hand, one offers to the deity with the pouring ladle as many oblations of butter as one can, and burns sesame and the rest as well. One ties on the student's hand this garland of flowers over which the mantra of Amṛtakuṇḍalin has been recited, sprinkles him in purification with the water taught before, and visualizes at his heart the mantra oṃ ru ru sphu ru jvala tiṣṭha siddha locani sarva artha sādhani svāhā. With this verse one should request the offering fee:

Since by pleasing the guru fully
one comes to obtain the accomplishment of the rite,
one should please the guru fully,
even if only by showing him honor.

Then, in that same hearth, one cooks a caru porridge with the remaining butter and milk and makes the fire offering. With the mantra oṃ cakṣu samanta cakṣu viśodhana vajra svāhā and a golden probe one opens the eyes of the image, and with the mantra oṃ dibya agane samādhi dhana priṇane svāhā one should offer the first portion of the caru to the deity. Then, for the pacifying and increasing of oneself and the student, one makes the fire offering with oṃ vajra puṣṭaye svāhā, and the guru, saying, To purify your sins, eat the remainder that the tathāgatas have partaken of, has the student eat the remainder of the caru.

Then, reciting the mantra of Amṛtakuṇḍalin, one circles the lower body, the middle and the head of the image three times with the tip of a vajra held in the right fist, and so makes it stable. Then, on a pedestal about one third the height of the image, one invites it to remain with oṃ supratiṣṭha vajre svāhā. The student circumambulates the image, prostrates and makes offerings, and then assembles the articles of offering and presents them to the master. The master, for his part, offers torma to the guardians of the directions, and, so that the image may remain for a long time and so that he himself and the student may be at peace, he makes supplication to the guardians of the directions and asks their forbearance.

If one wishes to perform the consecration of painted scrolls, images and the like in brief, one meditates on oneself as one's own chosen deity, bathes the image or other object, and arranges the articles of offering. Preceded by the meditative concentration on selflessness, one meditates on it instantly as the deity, blesses the eyes, the body and so forth, and dissolves the wisdom being into it. Then one worships it properly with outer and inner offerings and recites that deity's own mantra one hundred and eight times.

As for the consecration of a bell and a rosary, one generates a speech vajra from the syllable, and views it as transformed into the bell. The rosaries and so forth of those of little faith, of kusāli yogins, and of male and female yogins who practice for the sake of a livelihood are blessed simply by reciting the mantra.

Then one should make offerings, recite the mantra one hundred and eight times, ring the vajra and bell, join the palms, and ask the forbearance of the tathāgatas.

By performing the rites of the maṇḍala and so forth,
whatever virtue I have gained,
through it may the sufferings
of all sentient beings be entirely destroyed.

The rite of the maṇḍala, the fire offering and consecration according to the Hevajra treatise commentary, composed by the venerable master Kṛṣṇa, is complete. It was translated, edited and finalized by the Indian preceptor, the venerable Kṛṣṇa, and the chief editor-translator Gö Lhetsé.

Translator's notes

"The Wheel-bearer" renders 'khor lo can, apparently an epithet of Hevajra here; its Sanskrit form (perhaps Cakrin) is not certain. In "he stands in the center of the maṇḍala ground," the Tibetan has the instrumental dkyil 'khor gyis, read as the genitive gyi, an evident slip.
