About this text

Toh 13 in the Kangyur is the Āryaprajñāpāramitāsaṃcayagāthā, known in Tibetan as the Phags pa shes rab kyi pha rol tu phyin pa sdud pa tshigs su bcad pa, and rendered in English as The Noble Perfection of Wisdom: A Verse Condensation. It belongs to the Perfection of Wisdom class of sūtras, one of the most important groupings in the Kangyur, and presents the profound teachings on prajñāpāramitā — the wisdom gone beyond — in metrical verse form rather than the extended prose dialogues characteristic of longer works in this cycle. As its title indicates, the text functions as a poetic summary or condensation of the Perfection of Wisdom teachings, covering themes such as omniscience, the nature of emptiness, and the path of the bodhisattva. It is among the earliest and most studied verse formulations of prajñāpāramitā doctrine and has been widely used across Buddhist traditions as a concise yet comprehensive expression of these teachings.

Translation

In the language of India: Āryaprajñāpāramitāsaṃcayagāthā. In the language of Tibet: The Noble Perfection of Wisdom in Verse — a Condensation. Homage to the noble Mañjuśrī.

Then again the Blessed One (the Buddha), in order to bring perfect joy to those four assemblies, taught this Perfection of Wisdom and at that time spoke these verses:

Establish supreme joy, reverence, and faith close at hand.
Having dispelled the obscurations and afflictions, transcend all defilement.
The wisdom gone beyond, satisfied by penetrative understanding of the benefit of beings —
that in which the courageous ones engage: hear this now.

The first section: teaching omniscience.

However many rivers flow down upon this Jambudvīpa,
producing flowers, fruits, medicines, and forests —
the lord of nāgas (Skt. nāga), the nāga-king, dwelling in the cool lake, is their support:
that is the power and splendor of that lord of nāgas.

However much the śrāvakas (Skt. śrāvaka) of the Victor teach the Dharma,
however much they set forth reasoned exposition and explanation,
however much they bring about supreme noble happiness and its fruit is attained —
all of that too is the power of the Tathāgata.

Why is this? Because whatever manner of Dharma the Victor taught,
the foremost of humans, having become a disciple, trained in it with earnestness,
and having directly realized it, teaches the training just as it is.
It is done through the power of the buddha, not through one's own personal strength.

One in whom the supreme Perfection of Wisdom is without object,
in whom the bodhisattva is without object, and the awakening mind (bodhicitta) without object —
one who upon hearing this is neither confused nor afraid:
that bodhisattva practices the wisdom of the Sugata (the well-gone one, a buddha).

There is no form, no sensation, no perception, no mentation.
In that, there is not even a particle of consciousness that abides.
It does not abide in any phenomenon; it practices without abiding.
Without anything to be grasped, one attains the awakening of the Sugatas.

Just as a skilled wandering magician, knowing the illusion,
produces the apparent destruction of the aggregates without an object —
so too a bodhisattva who knows phenomena in this way
does not touch nirvāṇa, yet abides in wisdom.

Further: what is this wisdom, whose is it?
Contemplating that all these phenomena are empty, from what does it come?
Having examined closely, without shrinking or fear —
that bodhisattva is close to awakening.

If one, while lacking understanding, forms the notion of form,
and while engaging in the aggregates of sensation, mentation, and consciousness,
one thinks "these aggregates are empty" — yet that bodhisattva
practices with signs and does not have faith in the unborn ground.

But one who is neither form nor sensation, not perception nor mentation,
who does not engage in consciousness, who practices without abiding —
that one, seeing that there is no object called "practice," relies on wisdom,
and touches the supreme peaceful samādhi (meditative absorption) of the unborn.

Whatever bodhisattva thus abides here, at peace in themselves —
that one has been prophesied by the Tathāgatas of the past.
There is no pride in thinking "I am in equipoise" or "I have arisen" —
why? Because the intrinsic nature of phenomena is fully known.

If one practices in this way, one practices the wisdom of the Sugatas,
for one fully knows that this practice without practicing is itself the practice.
Whatever phenomenon one practices, it does not become an object —
this is practicing the supreme Perfection of Wisdom.

Whatever does not exist is called "nonexistent."
The childish examine that and make it either existent or nonexistent.
These two — existence and nonexistence — are a phenomenon free of both.
Whatever bodhisattva knows this will certainly emerge.

One who knows that the five aggregates here are like a magical illusion,
and does not make the magical illusion one thing and the aggregates another —

free of manifold perceptions and dwelling in nearness to peace —
this is practicing the supreme Perfection of Wisdom.

One who has a spiritual friend and has gained insight
will not come to be frightened upon hearing the Mother of the Victors.
But one who has evil companions and relies upon others
is like a raw vessel struck by water — it breaks apart.

Why is this one called a bodhisattva?
Because one wishes to sever attachment and to exhaust attachment in all things,
and to touch the Victors' awakening, which is free of attachment.
For this reason, one obtains the name "bodhisattva."

Why is this one called a great being (mahāsattva)?
Because one is supreme among the many multitudes of sentient beings,
and cuts through the great views of the realm of sentient beings.
Therefore one is rightly called a "great being."

Because of great giving, great intelligence, and great power,
because of entering the supreme Great Vehicle of the Victors,
because of donning the great armor and subduing the illusions of Māra —
for these reasons one is rightly called a "great being."

For example, when a magician conjures at a crossroads
and sets about cutting off the heads of a great multitude of people —
those who are killed are of such a nature; likewise the awakening mind
of one who truly knows that all beings are like emanations holds no fear.

Form, perception, feeling, mental formations,
and consciousness are neither bound nor liberated — they do not exist as such.
Thus, to enter into awakening with a mind free of timidity —
this is the supreme armor of noble persons.

Why is this called the Great Vehicle of awakening?
Because riding upon it, one soothes all sentient beings' sorrow.
This vehicle is like the sky — a vast and measureless palace —
the supreme vehicle by which joy and happiness are fully obtained.

One who rides upon it apprehends no direction in which one goes;
one does not apprehend the going that is said to be going to nirvāṇa.
Just as a person who has died has no movement —
for that reason, that is rightly called "nirvāṇa."

The bodhisattva apprehends neither the earlier limit nor the later limit,
nor anything arisen in the present — the three times are purified.
Those things are unconditioned and free of conceptual elaboration.
This is practicing the supreme Perfection of Wisdom.

When the time comes that a bodhisattva is learned and perceptive,
one contemplates non-arising and acts accordingly,
and though one arouses great compassion, one holds no perception of sentient beings —
this is practicing the supreme Perfection of Wisdom.

If one arouses a perception of sentient beings and a perception of suffering,
thinking "I shall act for the benefit of beings and abandon suffering,"
and construes self and sentient beings as truly existent — that is a bodhisattva, indeed,
but this is not practicing the supreme Perfection of Wisdom.

One knows all sentient beings to be just as one knows oneself to be;
one knows all phenomena to be just as one knows all sentient beings to be;
and one does not conceive of either non-arising or arising —
this is practicing the supreme Perfection of Wisdom.

However many names of Dharma are spoken throughout all the worlds —
having transcended arising and truly gone beyond them all —
one attains the noble, deathless gnosis that is other than none of these.
Therefore this is called the Perfection of Wisdom.

Whatever bodhisattva practices thus without doubt,
possessing wisdom and knowing how to abide in evenness —
having fully understood that phenomena have no intrinsic nature —
this is practicing the supreme Perfection of Wisdom.

The second section teaches knowledge of all paths.

One who does not abide in form, does not abide in feeling,
one who does not abide in perception, does not abide in mental formations,
one who does not abide in consciousness — abides in the nature of reality.
This is practicing the supreme Perfection of Wisdom.

Permanence and impermanence, pleasure and suffering, pleasant and unpleasant,
self and selflessness, suchness and the emptiness of suchness,
the attainment of fruits and the grounds of the arhat — without abiding in these —

the grounds of the pratyekabuddha, and likewise the grounds of the buddhas — without abiding in these.
Just as the guide does not abide in the unconditioned element,
so too without abiding in the conditioned, one moves without a home.
Thus the bodhisattva abides in groundlessness as a ground.
This groundless ground is what the Victors have declared as the abode.

Those who wish to become hearers of the Sugata (the well-gone Buddha),
pratyekabuddhas, and likewise sovereigns of the Dharma —
without relying upon this patience, they cannot attain these things.
It is like one who cannot see the near shore or the far shore when crossing over.

The teaching of the Dharma and the hearing of it, and whatever is to be explained,
the attainment of fruits, the condition-awakened ones, and likewise the Protector of the World —
the nirvāṇa attained by the learned and the clear-minded —
all of this the Tathāgata has taught to be like an illusion.

Of these four kinds of persons, none is frightened by this:
the son of the Victors who is skilled in truth and who is irreversible,
the arhat who has cleared away defilements and abandoned doubt,
and the fourth — the one fully held by a virtuous spiritual friend.

A bodhisattva skilled in knowledge who practices in this way
does not learn from the level of the arhat, nor from the level of condition-awakening.
For the sake of omniscience alone, one learns from the Dharma of the buddhas.
One who learns in the mode of neither learning nor not-learning — that is true learning.

Not for the sake of increasing or diminishing form, or grasping it,
not learning in order to grasp the many diverse phenomena —
yet one learns, and even omniscience itself is encompassed in that grasping.
Whatever renunciation there is — this is learning that delights in virtue.

Form is not wisdom, and in form there is no wisdom.
Consciousness, perception, feeling, and volition —
these are not wisdom, and in them too there is no wisdom.
This is like the element of space — undifferentiated, without distinction.

The intrinsic nature of all referential objects is without a further extreme.
The intrinsic nature of sentient beings is also without a further extreme.
The intrinsic nature of the element of space is also without a further extreme.
The wisdom that knows the world is also without a further extreme.

The guide has fully proclaimed that perception belongs to the near shore.
Through shattering perception and relinquishing it, one goes to the far shore.
Those who attain freedom from perception — they have attained the subsequent.
Having gone to the far shore, they abide in the word of the Teacher.

Even if the Teacher were to remain for as many aeons as there are grains of sand in the Ganges
and proclaim the sound "sentient being" throughout all that time —
since beings are primordially pure, how could they ever come to be born?
This is practicing the supreme Perfection of Wisdom.

When I speak in accord with this supreme Perfection of Wisdom,
at that moment the supreme being of former times declared:
"In a future time you shall become a buddha" —
thus did the Victor speak, saying that I received this prophecy.

Whoever takes this Perfection of Wisdom as a protector, enacts it,
and with reverence upholds and comprehends it —
poison, weapons, fire, and water cannot harm that one,
and māra and māra's cohorts will find no opportunity.

Some make stūpas of the seven precious substances for the Sugata who has fully passed into nirvāṇa,
and worship them — stūpas of the Sugata filling worlds
as numerous as ten million times the grains of sand in the Ganges,
fully adorning and grasping those worlds, boundless beyond count —
and all the sentient beings dwelling therein, as many as there may be,
doing nothing else but making offerings with divine flowers, supreme incense,
and perfumed ointments, worshipping for three times an aeon or even more than that.

But whoever, from this source from which the ten powers of the supreme guide arise —
this mother of the Sugata — writes it in a book
and upholds it, and with flowers and ointments makes homage —
the merit of building stūpas and making offerings cannot compare with that.

This Perfection of Wisdom of the Victors is a great knowledge-mantra:
it pacifies the Dharma of suffering and sorrow for the many classes of sentient beings.

Those who have passed beyond, and those who are the guardians of the world in all ten directions —
they trained in this knowledge-mantra and became the unsurpassed king of healers.
Those who act with benefit and compassion for beings —
training in this knowledge-mantra, the wise touch awakening.

Know that all happiness, whether conditioned or unconditioned,
arises from this very source.
As a seed planted in the ground gives rise to what grows from it,
and gathering together, many forms come into being —

so the five perfections, and whatever qualities of awakening there may be,
all of them arise from the Perfection of Wisdom.

The road along which the wheel-turning king always travels
is that very road on which all seven precious things and all his armies move.
Likewise, the path from which the Victor's Perfection of Wisdom proceeds
is the path from which all virtuous qualities and phenomena proceed.

The Victor, having answered fully all the questions of Śakra (Indra, the king of the gods), replied:
"Even if all the buddha-fields equal in number to the grains of sand of the Ganges
were filled with the relics of the Victors, piled up in heaps —
still I would take up this very Perfection of Wisdom.

Why? Not because I am disrespectful toward the bodily relics,
but I honor them through complete cultivation of wisdom.
Just as a king obtains offerings and reverence when a support is present,
so the relics of the Buddhas depend upon the Perfection of Wisdom.

A precious jewel endowed with every quality, of priceless worth —
wherever a treasure is bestowed, that place is worthy of veneration.
Even when the jewel has been removed, one still rejoices in the treasure-casket,
for those qualities belonged to that precious jewel.

In the same way, through the supreme qualities of the Perfection of Wisdom,
even after the Victors have passed into nirvāṇa, their relics receive homage.
Therefore, whoever wishes to uphold the qualities of the Victors
should take up this Perfection of Wisdom — this is liberation.

Wisdom is that which precedes the giving of gifts;
and so it is with moral discipline, patience, diligence, and meditative concentration.
It retains virtuous qualities so that they are not wasted.
This reveals all phenomena under a single principle.

Consider, for example, the thousands of millions of trees in Jambudvīpa,
with their many varied forms and their multitude of shapes —
apart from their shadows being enumerated in number,
the shadows themselves are neither many nor separately distinct.

In the same way, these five perfections of the Victors
all come to bear the very name of the Perfection of Wisdom.
When all is fully dedicated for the sake of omniscience,
these six all become of one flavor, called awakening.

If a bodhisattva, while not yet fully understanding,
takes to teaching the impermanence of form, perception, feeling, mental formations, and consciousness,
and acts upon what is compounded —
the wise never bring about the destruction of phenomena.

One who does not apprehend form, does not apprehend feeling, does not perceive perception,
who does not apprehend consciousness, and does not perceive mental formations,
who thoroughly understands all phenomena as unborn and empty —
this is the conduct of the supreme Perfection of Wisdom.

As for one who leads all the sentient beings dwelling in fields equal in number
to the grains of sand of the Ganges to the state of arhathood —
whoever writes out this Perfection of Wisdom
and gives the book to the most excellent of sentient beings, their merit is far superior.

Why? Because those supreme in speech, having trained in this,
teach all phenomena here as emptiness.
Hearing that, a śrāvaka swiftly touches liberation;
one touches the awakening of a pratyekabuddha, and one touches the awakening of a buddha.

If a world tree were to arise without a sprout, it would not come to be;
and where would its branches, flowers, and fruit come from?
If buddhas were to arise in the world without bodhicitta, it would not come to be;
how then could Śakra, Brahmā, and the śrāvakas with their hundredfold fruits arise?

When the orb of the sun spreads its web of light,
the beings who engage in activity do so likewise.
When, for the sake of the wise, bodhicitta arises in the world —

sentient beings are endowed with the qualities and phenomena of wisdom. Consider: if the lord of nāgas were absent from the unheated lake, how could rivers flow down into this land of Jambudvīpa? Without rivers, flowers and fruit could not arise, and the ocean too would be without its multitude of precious forms. In just the same way, if bodhicitta is absent, how could the gnosis of the Sugata arise throughout all these worlds? Without gnosis, qualities cannot increase, there is no awakening, and the Dharma of the buddhas — vast as the ocean — would likewise cease to exist.

In this world, all the living creatures that give light pour forth their rays so that illumination may reach everyone, yet a single ray from the orb of the sun is supremely excellent, and all those gathered rays of the light-givers together cannot match it. However great the accumulations of merit that the gathered assemblies of śrāvakas produce through generosity, moral discipline, meditation, and all they are richly endowed with — a single thought of rejoicing in a bodhisattva's wake surpasses the entire mass of merit belonging to the śrāvaka assembly.

Of all the buddhas of the past — inconceivable hundreds of millions of them — dwelling in the immeasurable billions of countless buddha-fields, and of all those protectors of the world who have already passed into nirvāṇa and who teach the precious Dharma so that suffering may be exhausted: from the first moment they aroused the supreme resolve for awakening to the final hour when the Dharma of those great guides comes to an end — whatever merit belongs to those Victors, the Dharma of the buddhas together with the perfections, whatever virtue belongs to the children of the buddhas, the śrāvakas, those in training and those beyond training, defiled virtue and undefiled — gathering all of this together and rejoicing in the bodhisattvas, one dedicates it all to awakening for the sake and benefit of beings.

If at the moment of total dedication a perception of dedicating should arise in the mind, and if the perception is of awakening, of dedicating, and of sentient beings, then because of that perception the mind clings to three objects — the viewed position, the abiding, and the mind itself — and because there is something apprehended, the dedication does not become complete.

But if one understands that this phenomenon ceases and is exhausted, that to wherever one dedicates is likewise exhausted, that phenomenon never dedicates to phenomenon — if one comes to know this clearly, then the dedication is truly complete.

If one makes a sign of it, that is not dedication. If there is no sign, then it is a dedication to awakening. Just as fine food mixed with poison becomes harmful, the Victors have declared that dwelling on virtuous phenomena is like that as well. Therefore the training in dedicating in this way is as follows: rejoice in the manner in which those Victors know that virtue — whatever its aspect, whatever its arising, whatever its defining mark — and dedicate in just that way.

When one thus dedicates merit to awakening, the Victors have declared: speak of the buddha who is without defilement and does not perish. The bodhisattva who thus dedicates with heroism while remaining object-referenced regarding the world surpasses and overwhelms every bodhisattva who is object-referenced.

Blind people without a guide, though they number in the hundreds of millions, do not know the road and cannot enter a city. Without wisdom, these five perfections are like the blind — without a guide they cannot touch awakening. But when one is fully held by wisdom, one has found the eye and receives this very name. Just as a painted work that is otherwise complete but lacks eyes — as long as the eye has not been painted in, the reward is not obtained — so too when wisdom thoroughly dissolves conditioned and unconditioned phenomena, white and dark alike, and does not apprehend even a particle, then in the world the counting of the Perfection of Wisdom proceeds — like the sky, resting nowhere at all.

If I am to practice the wisdom of the Victors
and liberate from contact with suffering the many hundreds of millions of sentient beings —

one who perceives sentient beings as real is a bodhisattva in name only — this is not the practice of the supreme Perfection of Wisdom. Whatever bodhisattva practiced in former times, when practicing this Perfection there is no doubt that he is skilled. As soon as he hears it, he gives rise to the perception of the Teacher; through that, he swiftly comes to realize the peace of awakening. Even if in former times of practice one honored hundreds of millions of buddhas, if one has no faith in the Perfection of Wisdom of the Victors, then upon hearing it that faint-hearted one rejects it; having rejected it and become without refuge, he goes to the Avīci hell. Therefore, if one wishes to touch the supreme gnosis of a buddha, have faith in this, the Mother of the Victors. For example, it would be wrong to be like a merchant who has gone to the isle of jewels and returns empty-handed. Know that the purity of form is the purity of the fruit; when the fruit and form are pure, omniscience itself becomes pure. Omniscience, the pure fruit, and the purity of form are like the element of space — indivisible, without partition.

The third section teaches the knowledge of all foundations. The hero who practices the Perfection of Wisdom: he is not liberated by passing wholly beyond the three realms, yet though afflictions are cleared away, he manifests birth; though aging, sickness, and death are absent, he displays passing away. These beings, clinging to the swamp of name and form, wander on the wheel of cyclic existence like a wheel driven by the wind. Knowing that wandering beings are like deer that have entered a snare, one endowed with wisdom ranges freely, like a bird in the sky. Conduct that is wholly pure neither engages with form, nor engages with consciousness, perception, feeling, or thought. Practicing thus, one abandons all attachment, and, freed from attachment, one practices the wisdom of the Sugatas. The bodhisattva, luminously skilled in such practice, cuts through attachment and moves among beings without attachment — like the sun standing clear and bright, freed from Rāhu the eclipse-demon, or like a fire that burns grass, trees, and forest. When a bodhisattva views the Perfection of Wisdom, seeing that all phenomena are by nature pure, wholly pure, he does not apprehend an agent and does not apprehend any phenomenon at all — this is the practice of the supreme Perfection of Wisdom.

The fourth section teaches the realization of full manifest awakening in all aspects. The inquiry put to the Victor by Śakra, the king of the gods: "How does a bodhisattva strive in the practice of wisdom?" One does not strive even a particle toward the aggregates or the elements; not striving toward the aggregates — that is the bodhisattva's striving. Whoever hears that these phenomena are like an emanation and like an illusion, and without doubt turns back to the teaching, applying himself again and again — know that such a sentient being has long since entered the Great Vehicle; he has served more than ten billion hundreds of millions of buddhas. As a person who has traveled many leagues along the road through a wilderness, upon seeing a fine forest at the edge of a black cattle-herding district, thinks "These are signs that a village or town is near," and gains relief and loses fear of bandits — so too, when one who earnestly seeks supreme awakening receives the opportunity to hear this Perfection of Wisdom of the Victors, know that he has gained relief and is without fear: he is not on the ground of an arhat, nor on the ground of one who has realized conditions alone. As a person who goes to see the waters of the ocean — if he still sees trees, forest, and mountains, it is still far off; but if those signs are not seen, one knows the great ocean is near, and concerning this there is no doubt — so too, one who has genuinely entered upon supreme awakening should know that by hearing this Perfection of Wisdom of the Victors, even though a guide has not yet directly prophesied it, awakening as a buddha is not far away; one will surely attain it. In the good season of spring, when a tree has burst into leaf —

the branches do not take long to put forth leaves, fruit, and flowers — so too, whoever obtains this Perfection of Wisdom of the Victors in hand will not take long to attain the awakening of those who guide beings.

For example, when a pregnant woman is beset by the pains of labor, one says that her time of giving birth has come. Likewise, a bodhisattva who hears the wisdom of the Victors, who gives rise to joy and aspiration, will swiftly touch awakening.

When a practitioner of yoga engages in this supreme Perfection of Wisdom, he does not perceive the increase or decrease of form. He who does not perceive phenomena or what is not phenomena within the dharmadhātu, and who does not touch upon nirvāṇa — he abides in wisdom.

He who, practicing here, does not conceptualize the qualities of buddhas, does not conceptualize powers, miraculous feet, awakening, or peace — he practices free from all conceptuality and conceptual elaboration, moved by blessing. This is the practice of the supreme Perfection of Wisdom.

Subhūti, the moon of speakers, then asked the Buddha: "What is it that cuts off the qualities and the joy of those who possess them?" The Teacher declared: "What cuts them off is many and various. Let me for now recount just a small portion. When one is copying this Perfection of Wisdom of the Victors, if a different kind of boldness arises and one abandons the welfare of beings and, swift as a lightning flash, falls into complete deterioration — this is the work of Māra.

"When this is being taught, some will be seized by doubt and think: 'The guide has not spoken my own name here, nor has he proclaimed my lineage, land, or clan' — and at that thought they will not listen and will abandon it. This is the work of Māra.

"Not knowing in this way, such people cast away the root and, in their confusion, search for branches and leaves. Just as one who has found an elephant then searches for the elephant's tracks — seeking sūtras after having heard this Perfection of Wisdom is exactly like that.

"For example, it is as if someone who has found food with a hundred flavors, having found the finest of foods, then seeks inferior food. In just the same way, a bodhisattva who has found this Perfection of Wisdom but then seeks awakening through the ground of an arhat is exactly like that.

"One may wish for honor and wish for gain; one who is intimate with households through the minds of gods and men, who abandons the Dharma and engages in what is not Dharma, who having found the path then strays from it — this is the work of Māra.

"Those who at that time went with aspiration and faith to listen to this supreme Dharma, yet upon recognizing that one who speaks the Dharma is present, depart without joy and with a troubled mind — at that time this work of Māra will arise. And at that time, those who stir up and disturb the many monks so that they will not take hold of this Perfection of Wisdom — many other obstacles too will appear.

"Those who obtain something rare and priceless — because it is rare, they are always much harmed. In just the same way, this supreme Perfection of Wisdom of the Victors is a precious Dharma; it too is rare, and there is always much harm done against it. Māra rejoices in creating obstacles for the newly entered, small-minded sentient being who has not found this rare jewel. Apply yourself to being held by the buddhas of the ten directions.

"A mother who has fallen ill and has many children — all of them are distressed and care for her. Likewise, the buddhas of the world-realms of the ten directions attend with care to this supreme wisdom that has become their mother."

All the protectors of the world in the ten directions, of past times and of the ten directions, and those who will appear in times to come — all of them arise from this. This wisdom is the mother who gives birth to the Victors, the teachers of the world. She reveals the mental conduct of other sentient beings. She reveals the suchness of the world, the suchness of the arhat, the suchness of the pratyekabuddha, and the suchness of the sons of the Victors.

Suchness — that oneness free from substantiality, not other —
the Perfection of Wisdom: the Tathāgata knows it.
Whether the wise one abides in the world or passes beyond sorrow,
this emptiness of phenomena, the nature of reality free from fault, remains.
The bodhisattva realizes this suchness in accordance with it,
and therefore the buddha is given the title "Tathāgata."

The Perfection of Wisdom, which rests in the garden of joy,
is the domain of those who lead beings — the holders of the ten powers.
Though they fully draw sentient beings forth from suffering and the three lower destinies,
they never conceive of those beings as sentient beings.

Just as a lion, fearlessly dwelling on a mountain slope,
causes the many small deer to tremble and roars its cry —
so the lion among humans, relying on the Perfection of Wisdom,
causes the many non-Buddhists to tremble and roars throughout the world.

Just as the rays of the sun abiding in the sky
dry up this earth and clearly reveal all forms —
so the king of Dharma, relying on the Perfection of Wisdom,
dries up the river of craving and fully reveals the Dharma.

Forms are not seen; feelings too are not seen;
perception is not seen, and intention is not seen;
and where consciousness, mind, and mentation are not seen —
this the Tathāgata has taught to be the seeing of phenomena.

Sentient beings speak the words "I see the sky" —
yet examine how exactly one sees the sky and what it means.
In just that way has the Tathāgata taught the seeing of phenomena.
That seeing cannot be expressed through any other analogy.
Whoever sees in that way, sees all phenomena.

Just as a king sits in equanimity while his ministers do all the work,
so all the phenomena of the buddhas and the śrāvakas, however many they are,
are all accomplished by the Perfection of Wisdom.

Just as a king does not go into the town, does not go around the district,
yet gathers all the assemblies from his own realm —
so the bodhisattva's nature of reality does not go anywhere,
yet gathers all the qualities of the ground of buddhahood.

Whoever has stable faith in the Sugata and the bodhisattvas,
and applies their mind to this supreme Perfection of Wisdom,
having utterly transcended the two grounds of the śrāvaka and the pratyekabuddha,
swiftly attains the awakening of the Victors without being overpowered.

Just as when a ship setting out onto the ocean is wrecked,
and a person does not grasp at a corpse, or grass, or a plank —
they will perish in the midst of the water and will not reach the shore.
But whoever grasps hold of something will reach the far side of the ocean.

Likewise, if one who has attained faith and purity
does not follow the mother, the Perfection of Wisdom,
yet if they are entirely embraced by the qualities of skillful means and wisdom,
they will swiftly touch the awakening of the Sugata — most wondrous.

Just as when someone fires unfired clay and fills it with water,
because it is fragile it will quickly be understood to break.
But a fired vessel filled with water and carried along the road
will not break, and will arrive home safely and with ease.

Likewise, though a bodhisattva may be thoroughly steeped in faith,
if wisdom is lacking, they will quickly become unsuitable for awakening.
But if faith is fully embraced by wisdom,
they will utterly transcend the two grounds and attain the supreme awakening.

Just as a well-made ship, if poorly repaired, will sink in the midst of the ocean
together with its cargo and merchants —
but if that ship is well repaired and well made,
it will not sink and will reach the shore with its cargo intact.

So too, even though a bodhisattva is thoroughly permeated by faith,
without wisdom they will quickly fall away from awakening.
But if that very one possesses the supreme Perfection of Wisdom,
without being harmed or diminished, they will touch the awakening of the Victors.

Having reached a hundred and twenty years, old and afflicted —

though they rise, they cannot walk on their own.
But if they are supported by people on both left and right,
there is no fear of falling and they walk along with ease.

So too, a bodhisattva of weak wisdom,
though they have entered the path, will quickly decline.
But if that very one is fully held by supreme skillful means and wisdom,
they will touch the awakening of the Tathāgatas without decline.

Those who abide on the ground of a bodhisattva of initial deeds,
who with elevated aspiration have entered the supreme awakening of the buddhas —
those who are good disciples and who revere their teacher
should always rely upon learned and wise teachers.
Why? Because the qualities of the learned arise from that.
These teachers point the way along the Perfection of Wisdom.
"The qualities of the buddhas and the Dharma depend upon a spiritual friend" —
so spoke the Victor, who possesses the supreme of all qualities.

Generosity, moral discipline, patience, and likewise diligence,
meditative concentration and wisdom — dedicate all of these to awakening.
Do not cling to the heap of awakening and grasp it as supreme —
this is the instruction to be given to one of initial deeds.

Those who practice thus, the moons who speak of the ocean of virtue,
are refuge, ally, and dwelling place for beings.
They are support, intelligence, island, and guide for those who seek meaning,
illuminating lamp, speaker of the supreme Dharma, and undisturbed.

Those of great renown don the armor that is hard to don.
It is not the armor of the aggregates (skandhas), the elements (dhātus), or the sense bases (āyatanas).
It is free of the perception of the three vehicles, and there is nothing to be grasped.
It is non-regressing, unshaken, and undisturbed in its nature.

Those who are endowed with such qualities, free of conceptual elaboration,
rid of doubt and uncertainty, free of indecision, and rich in meaning —
one should know that upon hearing the Perfection of Wisdom, they do not lament,
they do not rely upon others, they do not regress.

This Dharma of the guides is profound and difficult to see.
None whatsoever can realize or attain it.
Therefore, having attained awakening with love and for the benefit of others,
the compassionate ones thought: "Who among the hosts of sentient beings could understand?" —
and put their minds at little anxiety.

Sentient beings delight in their abode and desire their objects,
they dwell in grasping, the unintelligent, the foolish and dull, as though in darkness.
The Dharma to be attained has no fixed place and no object of grasping.
That is why conflict with the worlds arises.

The expanse of space — in the eastern direction and the southern direction,
likewise in the western direction and the northern direction, boundless to the farthest edge,
in above, below, and however much exists in the ten directions —
there is nothing become different, nothing become distinct.

The suchness of the past, the suchness of what is yet to come,
the suchness of the present, the suchness of an arhat —
the suchness of all phenomena, the suchness of the Victors —
this suchness of all phenomena in the Dharma is undifferentiated.

What has arisen free from the qualities of difference between the awakening of the Sugatas:
this is what a bodhisattva who wishes to attain it
should, endowed with skillful means, apply themselves to the Perfection of Wisdom.
Without the wisdom of the guides, it cannot be attained.

Consider a bird whose body has grown to a size of a hundred and fifty leagues,
but whose wings have wasted away and whose strength is gone —
if it leaps from the Heaven of the Thirty-Three down to this Jambudvīpa on its own,
it will come to harm and injury.

These five perfections of the Victors as well,
though cultivated through countless tens of millions of eons,
and though vast, limitless aspirations are maintained in the world always —
without skillful means, lacking wisdom, one falls into the śrāvaka state.

Whoever wishes to emerge with certainty into the vehicle of the buddhas
should, with an equanimous mind toward all beings, with the perception of mother and father,
with a mind of benefiting and a spirit of loving-kindness, train oneself,
and with impartiality and honesty speak gentle words.

Sthavira Subhūti asked the Protector of the World:
"O ocean of qualities, please show the signs of being free from afflictions.
Just as the mighty do not turn back again,
so please, Victor, prophesy even a fraction of those qualities."

Free from the perception of separation, speaking words endowed with reason,
one does not take refuge in other ascetics (śramaṇas) and brahmins.
The wise constantly abandon the three lower realms,
and apply themselves diligently to the path of the ten virtuous actions.

Without material expectation, they teach Dharma to beings by following after them.
They delight in Dharma alone and always speak gentle words.
Whether walking, moving about, lying down, or sitting, they are thoroughly endowed with vigilance.
They look only as far as a yoke's length ahead, and their mind is not confused as they move.

Keeping their body clean and wearing clean robes, the three — body, speech, and mind — are in solitude and purity.
They do not seek gain; foremost among the herd, they always desire Dharma.
They have passed beyond the realm of Māra and do not rely on others.
They cultivate the four meditative concentrations, yet do not remain attached to those states of concentration.
They do not seek fame; their mind is not bound by anger.

Though they become householders, they are never attached to any possessions whatsoever.
For the sake of livelihood, they do not seek wealth through dishonest means.
They do not practice violent spells or mantras for binding women,
and they do not teach that one who performs acts of desire will be reborn as a man or woman.

They strive earnestly in the supreme perfection of wisdom through thorough seclusion.
Free from fighting and quarreling, they are also steady in a mind of loving-kindness.
Desiring omniscience and steady, they are possessed of a mind ever inclined toward it.
They completely abandon the borderlands inhabited by barbarian peoples.

In their own domain they are without suspicion, always like Mount Meru.
For the sake of Dharma they even cast aside their lives, and strive earnestly in yoga.
These are to be known as the signs of non-return.

Form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness
are profound — without inherent nature, without characteristics, utterly pacified.
Just as one who seeks the bottom of the ocean with an arrow does not reach it,
so, when examined with wisdom, the depths of the aggregates are not found.

Whatever bodhisattva has become thus profound,
who has realized this Dharma of the ultimate vehicle, without attachment,
in which aggregates, elements, and sense bases do not exist —
what merit truly accomplished could there be greater than that?

For example, take a man engaged in the Dharma of desire
who has arranged a tryst with a woman yet does not meet her.
As many mental thoughts and activities arise in his mind throughout one day,
so many eons will a bodhisattva take to attain awakening through that path.

Yet a bodhisattva who, through countless tens of millions of eons,
gives to arhats and pratyekabuddhas and maintains moral discipline —
the good of speaking even a word of Dharma possessing the supreme Perfection of Wisdom
surpasses that giving and that moral discipline.

A bodhisattva who has cultivated the supreme wisdom,
who then rises from it and teaches the unstained Dharma,
and who, for the sake of beings' welfare, dedicates it as a cause for awakening —
there is nothing in the three worlds equal to that virtue.

That merit, too, should be known as accumulated, yet equally empty,
as a husk, as a skeleton, as marrow, and as without essence.
When one thus practices and sustains the wisdom of the Sugatas,
one fully grasps immeasurable merit in the act of practicing.

Knowing that all these Dharmas, which the buddhas have taught, practiced, and demonstrated,
are exhausted in mere words —
though one speaks them throughout countless tens of millions upon billions of eons,
in the dharmadhātu there is neither exhaustion nor increase.

Whatever is called "the perfections of the Victors" —
those dharmas too are thoroughly proclaimed as names only.
A bodhisattva who fully dedicates without the conceit of dedication
will be without decline and will attain the supreme awakening of a buddha.

Like sesame oil and its essence: upon first contact
the essence is not burned by a word, nor does it vanish without a word.
The final tongue of flame does not burn the essence,
yet without the final flame the essence is also not burned.
So too, the first moment of mind does not reach supreme awakening,

yet without that final moment it cannot be reached.
The last moment of mind does not attain the peaceful awakening,
yet without it that attainment is also impossible.

From a seed arise a sprout, a flower, and fruit,
and yet when the seed has ceased, the tree is not found there —
just so, the first moment of mind is itself a cause of awakening,
and yet when it has ceased, awakening is not in that moment either.

As drops of water fill a jar from the first drop to the last,
little by little, gradually filling it to fullness —
just so, the first moment of mind is a cause of supreme awakening,
and gradually, through the completion of white qualities, one becomes a buddha.

Practicing the Dharma of emptiness, signlessness, and wishlessness,
not touching nirvāṇa, not engaging in signs —
like a skilled archer shooting his arrows, moving back and forth,
yet dwelling at neither extreme and not remaining in the ocean —
a bodhisattva who practices in this way has no thought of "I
shall be prophesied by the ten powers and shall touch awakening."
Nor does the thought "there is nothing here" frighten him away from awakening.
Such practice is the practice of the wisdom of the Sugata (the Well-Gone One, the Buddha).

Seeing the world as a desolate path beset with famine and disease,
one is without fear; from that point one dons the armor,
strives without cease to the very end of the future,
and does not produce even a trace of a despairing mind.

A bodhisattva who practices the wisdom of the Victors,
knowing that these aggregates have been empty and unborn from the very beginning,
enters into compassion for the realm of sentient beings even outside of meditative equipoise,
and in that intermediate period does not fall away at all from the Dharma of the buddhas.

Consider a skilled person endowed with all qualities,
possessed of strength and tireless effort, knowing arts and crafts and rituals,
who has gone to the far shore in many trades and crafts,
knows how to perform illusions, and has a deep wish to benefit beings.
Having gathered together his father, his mother, and his wife,
he travels along a desolate road beset with many enemies.
He, being courageous and bold, conjures up many illusory persons,
goes along safely and returns home again.

Just so, the bodhisattva who is skilled and clear-sighted,
generating great compassion for all the realms of sentient beings,
thoroughly passing beyond the four māras and the two grounds as well,
abides in supreme meditative absorption and yet does not touch awakening.

Wind rests upon space; upon wind rests a mass of water;
upon that the great earth rests; upon that beings rest.
The cause that underlies the activities and actions of sentient beings is of just such a nature —
contemplate what meaning it is that space contains.

Likewise, a bodhisattva abiding in emptiness,
supported by aspirational prayers and the awareness of sentient beings as beings,
manifests many and various activities,
does not touch nirvāṇa, and does not abide in emptiness as though fixed there.

When a wise and luminous bodhisattva
practices this supreme meditative absorption of peaceful emptiness,
in that intermediate period he does not meditate upon signs at all;
he dwells without signs, at peace, practicing in utter stillness.

A bird flying through open space does not dwell there —
not dwelling there, it does not fall to the ground.
Likewise, a bodhisattva who practices through the doors of liberation
does not touch nirvāṇa and does not practice in signs.

Consider a person trained in archery who shoots an arrow into the sky:
he strings other arrows in succession so that each follows the one before,
so that the earlier arrow never finds an occasion to fall to earth —
yet if that person wished it, the arrow would indeed fall to the ground.

In the same way, one who practices the supreme Perfection of Wisdom,
conducting oneself through wisdom, skillful means, powers, and miraculous ability —
for as long as the roots of virtue are not complete,
for so long does one not attain that supreme emptiness.

Consider a monk endowed with the highest powers of miraculous ability:
dwelling in the sky, he performs magical displays all at once,
manifesting going, walking, lying down, and sitting —
and yet he is without weariness and without fatigue in all of this.

Likewise, a wise bodhisattva who abides in emptiness —

displaying limitless activities for all beings,
without weariness and without fatigue across ten million aeons.

Consider a person who stands at the edge of a great precipice,
holding an umbrella in each hand, who leaps into the sky
and, having cast their body over the great abyss,
does not fall for as long as they continue moving through the air.

Likewise, a wise bodhisattva who abides in compassion,
grasping the two umbrellas of skillful means and wisdom,
contemplates all phenomena as empty, signless, and wishless,
does not touch nirvāṇa, and yet perceives the Dharma.

Consider one who desires jewels and sails to a jewel-isle,
finds those jewels, and then returns home again.
There the merchant alone lives at ease,
and does not place his friends and companions in discomfort.

Likewise, a bodhisattva who has gone to the jewel-isle of emptiness,
who has attained the powers of meditative concentration,
takes delight only in touching nirvāṇa
and does not lay to heart the sufferings of sentient beings.

Consider a merchant who travels through whatever towns, villages,
and settlements lie along the way, seeking profit and wishing to learn the route.
He does not stay there, nor does he stay at the jewel-isle,
nor does he rest at home — he becomes expert in the path.

Likewise, a bodhisattva who is clear-sighted becomes expert in all the forms of liberation
of śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas.
He does not dwell there; the wisdom of the buddhas is not his dwelling-place.
He does not dwell in the unconditioned, and yet he knows the manner of the path.

When one has connected beings to loving-kindness
and practices meditative absorption that is empty, signless, and wishless —
can it be said that such a one has attained nirvāṇa,
or can it be designated as conditioned? That one is without a fixed abode.

Consider a magically created human body: it is not invisible,
and yet it can be designated by name.
Likewise, a bodhisattva who moves through the gates of liberation
can still be designated by name.

If, when one's conduct and faculties are fully examined,
a bodhisattva does not fully teach the Dharma of emptiness and signlessness,
and the Dharma of the irreversible ground is not interrupted —
that is to be understood as neither affirmed nor denied by scripture.

The fifth section teaches the full and perfect realization of the summit.

The wisdom of arhats and of those buddhas who awaken through conditions,
who even in dreams have no desire for the three realms,
who behold the buddhas and teach the Dharma to beings —
that is to be understood as a prophecy of irreversibility.

One who in a dream sees sentient beings dwelling in the three lower realms,
and in that very instant makes aspiration prayers to sever the stream of the lower realms,
and through the power of truth's blessing quenches a mass of fire —
that is to be understood as a prophecy of irreversibility.

One who, in a human world filled with spirits and many diseases,
pacifies them through the power of truth with benefit and heartfelt compassion,
and yet is without conceit and without the arising of pride —
that is to be understood as a prophecy of irreversibility.

If, however, the thought of conceit should arise —
"Because I have gained these various powers of truth's blessing, I have been prophesied" —
or if a bodhisattva should take pride in being prophesied by another,
the one in whom such conceit abides is to be known as of small intelligence.

A māra approaches from the ground of names and addresses one thus:
"This name is yours, and it was the name of your father and mother,
and of all who came before you down to seven generations.
It is the very name by which you will become a buddha.
What kind of training, vows, and yoga will arise?
And your former qualities and manner of conduct were just such as this."

A bodhisattva who hears such words and becomes conceited
is to be known as fully stirred up by māra and of small intelligence.

One who frequents deeply secluded villages and towns, mountain ravines,
hermitages, wilderness places, and forest groves,
yet praises oneself while disparaging others —
that bodhisattva is to be known as fully stirred up by māra and of small intelligence.

One who perpetually dwells in towns, villages, and hamlets
for the purpose of ripening sentient beings and striving for awakening,
and in whom no wish other than that of an arhat or pratyekabuddha arises —
such solitude, the Sugatas' sons have said, is real solitude.

A bodhisattva who dwells for many thousands of years
in a mountain ravine five hundred leagues across,
filled with snakes,
yet does not understand this solitude
has fallen into the company of spirits (pretas) by seizing upon an inflated pride.

A bodhisattva who strives with diligence for the benefit of beings,
who has attained meditative concentration, the powers, the faculties, the liberations, and samādhi —
one who looks down on such a person thinking, "This is no life of forest seclusion,"
dwells, the Victor has said, within the domain of māra.

Whether one dwells in a village or in a hermitage,
if one is free from the mind of both vehicles and resolved upon supreme awakening,
that is the solitude of those who have entered the service of beings.
A bodhisattva who turns lukewarm will suffer the ruin of his own realization.

Therefore, one who seeks supreme awakening,
possessed of resolute intention and wisdom, having decisively subdued conceit,
should, just as an assembly of sick people relies on a physician to be healed,
rely upon a spiritual friend without slackening.

Bodhisattvas who have entered upon buddha-awakening, the supreme awakening,
should rely upon them along with the perfections as their spiritual friend.
Those who guide others in the footsteps of this practice —
this is the ground of accomplishment.
By means of these two causes, buddha-awakening is swiftly realized.

All the Victors of the past, future, and those dwelling in the ten directions —
for all of them the path was none other than these perfections.
These perfections, it is said, are the light, the lamp, the radiance, and the supreme guide
for those who have entered upon supreme awakening.

Just as the Perfection of Wisdom is characterized by emptiness,
one should know that all these phenomena share that same characteristic.
And if one thoroughly understands that phenomena are empty and without marks,
to conduct oneself accordingly is to practice the wisdom of the Sugata.

Sentient beings, utterly famished, crave food,
and those whose minds are attached to saṃsāra circle without end.
Self and what belongs to self — both are empty, not truly existent.
In their childishness, ordinary beings cast knots into the sky.

For example, when one is made to ingest poison through the perception of suspicion,
the poison, though it has not actually entered, causes one to faint.
Likewise, ordinary beings who affirm self and what belongs to self —
the perception of that very self, impermanent and subject to arising and perishing, arises truly for them.
As one clings, so does affliction fully manifest throughout.
Not perceiving self and what belongs to self is proclaimed as purification.

In regard to this, a bodhisattva who realizes the Perfection of Wisdom
understands that no affliction can be purified while remaining unchanging.

However many sentient beings there are in Jambudvīpa without exception —
if all of them were to arouse the mind set on supreme awakening
and were to practice generosity for many tens of millions of years,
dedicating all of it as a cause for awakening for the benefit of beings —
yet if someone devotes earnest effort to practicing the supreme Perfection of Wisdom
and accords with it even for a single day,
the mass of merit from that generosity cannot match it.
Therefore, without slackening, one should always engage in wisdom.

A yogin who practices the supreme Perfection of Wisdom,
though arousing great compassion, holds no perception of sentient beings as such.
At that time the wise one becomes a field of generosity for all beings
and always, going about among villages and districts on alms-round, practices meaningfully and completely.

A bodhisattva who has long been connected to this place,
wishing to teach the vast path of the perfections to the realm of sentient beings —
gods, humans, and those of the three lower destinies — in order to liberate them,

Day and night one should strive in the Perfection of Wisdom.

A supremely precious jewel that a person had never before found,
found at last on some other occasion, and overjoyed,
if through carelessness one loses it the very moment it is found,
then, having lost it, one forever aches with longing for that jewel.

Likewise, the yogin must not abandon the Perfection of Wisdom,
which is like a precious jewel entered upon in supreme awakening.
Just as one who has found a jewel, having taken it up and bound it securely with diligent effort,
moves swiftly onward until reaching peace —

so too the sun, its rays spread wide, free from clouds,
rising to vanquish all dense and heavy darkness,
outshines the firefly, every living creature and spirit,
all the hosts of stars, and the full light of the moon.

Likewise, the bodhisattva who practices the Perfection of Wisdom,
dwelling in supreme conduct, practicing emptiness and the signless,
through wisdom shatters the dense thicket of [wrong] views and, going forth,
utterly outshines all beings, all arhats, all pratyekabuddhas, and the multitude of [ordinary] awakening minds.

Take the example of a prince who, wishing to distribute wealth, is fit to stand at the head of all
and to set out openly in that role —
yet even in that lesser station he gladdens multitudes of beings;
what need is there to speak of his position once he has obtained the means and sits on the royal throne?

Likewise, the bodhisattva who is skilled in the practice of wisdom
bestows nectar and gladdens gods and humans alike.
Even in that lesser station he strives to benefit multitudes of beings;
what need is there to speak of his position once he is established as the Dharma king?

At that time Māra becomes afflicted with a thorn of anguish,
wretched with distress, suffering, mental unease, and dejection,
wondering how this bodhisattva has come to be gladdened,
and so, to inspire terror, he sets the directions ablaze and hurls meteor arrows.

But whenever those wise ones are possessed of strong resolve
and day and night attend to the supreme meaning of the Perfection of Wisdom,
at that time body and mind move through the sky like birds in flight —
how could the dark forces with all their stratagems find any opening?

Yet when bodhisattvas fall into fighting and disputing,
harboring minds of mutual hostility and anger,
at that time Māra rejoices and takes supreme delight,
thinking that these two have grown far from the wisdom of the Victors.

Those two grow distant and become like cannibals toward each other,
both breaking their own pledges and commitments.
Where there is hatred, where patience is absent, how can awakening be found?
At that time Māra and all his hosts rejoice together.

When a bodhisattva who has not received a prophecy
begins to quarrel in anger with one who has received a prophecy,
for as many moments as that faulty and defective mind persists,
for that many eons the armor of merit is stripped away from the ground up.

A buddha touches awakening through the Perfection of Patience.
Yet if one produces the thought that this mind is not good,
and clings to things separately, and then restrains oneself afterward,
without rejoicing — that is what one studies in the Dharma of the buddhas.

When one studies and yet does not accept any training as such,
not apprehending the student, not apprehending the dharmas of training,
not conceptualizing these two — training and what is not training —
whoever studies in that way, that one studies in the Dharma of the buddhas.

Whatever bodhisattva knows this kind of training,
that one will not, even elsewhere, fall into broken conduct or impaired moral discipline.
That one studies this in order to please the buddhas in the Dharma.
That one is skilled in training that transcends training, without any apprehension of an object.

It is in just this way that the wisdom of the wise illuminators is trained.
They do not give rise to even a single moment of non-virtuous mind.
Just as in the sky before the sun, whose rays course through the heavens and overwhelm all,
no darkness remains in the expanse of space ahead —

so too, for those who have trained in the Perfection of Wisdom,
all the perfections come to be gathered here in this one.
Just as the sixty-two views are said to be subsumed within the view of the transitory collection,
likewise it is taught that all these perfections are subsumed in this.

For example, if the faculty of the life-force is cut off, all other faculties —

all that exist, however many, cease.
Likewise, for the supreme wise ones who train in the Perfection of Wisdom,
all the perfections are taught to be gathered here in this one.

Those skilled in the awakening mind (bodhicitta) train in whatever qualities the śrāvakas possess,
and likewise train in all the qualities of the solitary buddhas (pratyekabuddhas),
yet they do not abide in those, nor do they give rise to any desire for them.
They train because they think: "This is what I must train in."

Toward the arising of the mind directed toward supreme, irreversible awakening,
they rejoice with deep and sincere intention.
The merit of that rejoicing cannot be measured even by weighing Mount Meru
on a scale against the triple-thousand world-system —
and yet the merit of such rejoicing is not even like that.

Toward however many sentient beings there are who seek virtue and wish for benefit,
one has rejoiced in every mass of their accumulated merit.
Therefore, having thus obtained the qualities of the Victors,
in order to bring suffering to exhaustion, one gives the Dharma to the world.

A bodhisattva who does not conceptualize any phenomena,
who thoroughly knows emptiness, signlessness, and freedom from conceptual elaboration —
that one does not seek awakening by means of two kinds of wisdom,
and that yogin (Skt. yogin) strives in the supreme Perfection of Wisdom.

Just as there can be no contradiction between the element of space and space itself,
and no one whatsoever has attained it —
so too the bodhisattva skilled in the Perfection of Wisdom
is like space and dwells in perfect peace.

In the midst of ordinary people, like a conjured illusory man,
one does not think "I will please these people" — and yet one does so.
Even while appearing to display all manner of miraculous powers,
that conjured man has no body, no mind, not even a name.
So too, one who trains in the Perfection of Wisdom never thinks:
"Having realized awakening, I will liberate all beings" —
yet appearing like an illusion with various births and manifold deeds,
one acts without conceptual thought.

For example, a magical emanation of a buddha performs the deeds of a buddha,
yet in the doing of those deeds, not even a trace of pride arises.
So too, the bodhisattva skilled in the Perfection of Wisdom
displays all deeds like an emanation, like an illusion.

Just as a master carpenter skilled in his craft fashions a figure resembling a woman —
that figure too performs all the actions —
so too, the bodhisattva skilled in the Perfection of Wisdom
performs all deeds through non-conceptual wisdom (jñāna).
