About this text

The Means of Accomplishment of Glorious Saṃvarodaya, known in Sanskrit as the Śrī Saṃvarodaya Sādhana and in Tibetan as Pal Dompa Jungwé Druptap, is a concise ritual manual for the meditative practice of the deity Cakrasaṃvara according to the Saṃvarodaya Tantra. After a verse of homage and a statement that it condenses the stages of practice taught in that tantra, it guides the practitioner through dissolving ordinary pride into emptiness, setting up the protective circle, generating oneself as six-armed Cakrasaṃvara in union with his consort amid a maṇḍala of ḍākinīs, and then through empowerment, mantra recitation, the offering of torma, and the dissolution and closing of the practice. It is Toh 1515 in the Tengyur, where it belongs among the commentaries and practice texts of the tantra section, and its colophon records that it was translated into Tibetan in Nepal by the monk Shong Lodrö Ten under the Nepalese master Kṣāntiśrī.

Translation

In the language of India: śrī sambarodaya sādhanaṃ. In the language of Tibet: The Means of Accomplishment of Glorious Saṃvarodaya.

Homage to glorious Saṃvarodaya.

Pure and peaceful, pervading equal to space,
hard to realize, the domain of yogins,
unequalled, nondual, and without cause:
may glorious Saṃvarodaya protect you all.

The stages of the means of accomplishment
taught in the Saṃvarodaya Tantra I have rightly condensed;
I compose this so that, by meditating,
people may be freed from the harm of delusion.

Here, first of all, with regard to meditation: facing south, with hair unbound, having given the torma, and being firm in one's commitments, in order that all the aggregates of sentient beings may attain the body of Cakrasaṃvara, one thinks, "I am glorious Cakrasaṃvara." Thus, in an instant, through emptiness, ordinary pride is abandoned.

Then, with the mantra of one possessed of the yoga of two-armed Cakrasaṃvara together with his consort, wisdom, one recites three times oṃ, the letters beginning with ā and ending with kṣa, followed by hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. One meditates that this garland of letters abides circling to the left, and that hosts of deities radiating five-colored light rays destroy the hosts of obstructors. Then one should intensely resolve that Vairocana and the others are the complete purity of the aggregate of form and the rest.

Then, reciting the four mantras that follow, oṃ sumbha ni sumbha hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ, oṃ gṛhṇa gṛhṇa hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ, oṃ gṛhṇa pa ya gṛhṇā pa ya hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ, oṃ ān ya ho bha ga bān biṅyā rā ja hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ, one should place in order, in the east, the north, the west, and the south, great figures, black, green, red, and yellow, blazing and pervading up to the egg of Brahmā and down to the underworld.

By their light rays arise four walls of their colors; and at the very same time, born from hūṃ and blessed by it, four grounds of the nature of crossed vajras, reaching to the very bottom of the underworld. By the light rays of that vajra arise a fence of five-pronged vajras, and above it a net of arrows. Beneath that is a vajra tent, and beneath that one should also imagine a vajra canopy.

Then, reciting the four mantras of Sumbha and the others and snapping the fingers toward the directions, one drives away the seven malevolent ones, and one should intensely resolve that beings are in every respect free of obstructors.

Then one looks upon a blue syllable hūṃ at one's own heart, resting on a moon born from oṃ. With hooks of light rays emanating from it and radiating throughout the ten directions, one properly summons into the sky in front the wheel of the maṇḍala that will be described, and one should make offerings to it with the sixteen goddesses who issue from the seed at one's own heart: the Goddess of Flowers, the Goddess of Incense, the Goddess of Lamps, the Goddess of Perfume, the Goddess of the Lute, the Goddess of the Flute, the Goddess of the Round Drum, the Goddess of the Clay Drum, the Goddess of Laughter, the Goddess of Dalliance, the Goddess of Song, the Goddess of Dance, the Goddess of the Mirror, the Goddess of Taste, the Goddess of Touch, and the Goddess of Dharma.

After that, one praises with the mantras of eight lines, and having performed the confession of misdeeds and the rest, up to equanimity, one should make manifest the meaning of svabhāba śuddhaḥ sarba dharmmāḥ svabhāba śuddho'haṃ. Viewing the three realms as having the nature of emptiness, one meditates on the nature of the three syllables oṃ āḥ hūṃ, dissolving in sequence into the ū, the ha, the head stroke, the crescent moon, and the drop, up to the end in the nāda. One makes clear the meaning of oṃ śūnyatā jñāna bajra svabhāba ātmako'haṃ by reciting it inwardly, and one should then completely abandon even the conceptual thought of the mere nāda.

Through the power of former aspirations and the like, one's own mind, in which one's own seeds remain present, is set in space in the form of nāda. From yaṃ arises a blue wind, shaped like a bow, with banners. From raṃ arises a red fire, triangular, with seven-tongued flames. From baṃ arises white water, round, with a vase. From laṃ arises a yellow, square earth maṇḍala with half-vajras at its corners. Upon it, from suṃ, arises Mount Meru, made of various jewels. Upon that, from baṃ, arises a multicolored lotus. Resting on its anthers, from hūṃ, arises a crossed vajra. On its hub, from the doubled vowels and consonants circling to the left and to the right, arises a single moon maṇḍala like a reflection. On it, from the nāda that was set there before, one should meditate on the syllable hūṃ, gradually completed, which, by emanating and gathering light rays, is the nature of Vajrasattva, the wheel of deities.

From the complete transformation of that moon and seed arise the eight charnel grounds, the celestial palace, and, together with the deities of the maṇḍala, the Blessed One. In the stance with the right leg extended, with his two feet he tramples a multicolored lotus, a sun, Bhairava, and Kālarātri. His main, right, and other faces are black, white, and red, and each face has three eyes. He wears a skirt of tiger skin. He has six arms: with the pair of hands holding vajra and bell he embraces Vajravairocanī; with the second pair of hands he holds a white elephant hide, still bloody, stretched out behind him; and with the third right and left hands he holds a ḍamaru, and also a khaṭvāṅga and a skull cup.

A garland of fifty moist human heads hangs at his neck. He wears the six mudrās: necklace, earrings, and bracelets, the wheel on the crown of his head, the girdle, and ash that is of the nature of human bone. He wears a sacred thread of human sinew, and his forehead is adorned with a garland of five skulls. To the left of the jewel is a crescent moon, and his crest of matted hair is marked with a crossed vajra. His wrathful face is slightly open, with fangs bared. One should meditate on oneself as possessed of the nine moods of dance: grace, heroism, disgust, ferocity, mirth, terror, compassion, wonder, and peace.

The Blessed Lady Vairocanī is red, three-eyed, and one-faced, with her hair hanging loose. She is naked and adorned with a garland of pieces of skull. With her left hand, holding a skull cup to the side, she embraces him, offering the Lord the skull filled with menstrual blood. With her right hand, extended upward, holding a vajra with the threatening gesture, she threatens all the malevolent ones. Being bloodless, she wears a garland of dry human heads. She has the radiance of a sun disk and bears the five mudrās. Her hair bristling with joy, she sits embracing the Blessed One, her two shanks wound around his two thighs, joined with him in union.

Then, on the petals of the cardinal directions are Ḍākinī, Lāmā, Khaṇḍarohā, and Rūpiṇī, black, green, red, and yellow in color. Each has three eyes and one face, with hair unbound, and two arms: with the left she holds a khaṭvāṅga and a skull cup, and with the right a vajra curved knife. They are naked and stand in the posture with the right leg extended. On the petals of the intermediate directions are lotus vessels filled with the five nectars. In the gates are Kākāsyā, Ulūkāsyā, Śvānāsyā, and Śūkarāsyā, the crow-faced, owl-faced, dog-faced, and boar-faced goddesses, who are like Ḍākinī and the others, except that their faces follow their names. In the corners are Yamadāḍhī, Yamadūtī, Yamadaṃṣṭriṇī, and Yamamathanī, who have human faces and are of half-and-half form. These eight stand upon corpse seats.

All the goddesses, Ḍākinī and the rest, have garlands of strung human heads hanging at the neck, and their foreheads are marked with a garland of vajras. They bear the five mudrās, and they are beautifully adorned with small tinkling bells on their girdles. Their foreheads, throats, and hearts are blessed by the three syllables.

At the heart of the Blessed One, resting on a sun, is the blue syllable hūṃ, the samādhi being. At its heart one should imagine a drop with nāda, of the nature of the nectar of great bliss. One should recite oṃ āḥ hūṃ oṃ sarba bīra yo gi nī naṃ kā ya bāk citta sva bhā va ātma ko'haṃ and oṃ bajra śuddhaḥ sarba ddharmmā bajra śuddho'haṃ.

After that, with oṃ ha na ma hi svā hā hu bauṣaṭ he hūṃ hūṃ ho phaṭ haṃ, as the essence of the six tathāgatas, one should make protection at the heart, the head, the crown, the two shoulders, and all the limbs. With oṃ baṃ haṃ yoṃ hriṃ moṃ hreṃ hriṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ phaṭ, the heart mantras of the six heroines, one should also put on armor at the navel, the heart, the mouth, the crown, and all the limbs.

After that, having purified the vajra and the lotus, one places the heart and near-heart mantras of the goddess at the navel and the heart, and one's own heart and near-heart mantras at the throat and the forehead; that is, for the Blessed One and the Blessed Lady, the heart and near-heart mantras of the Blessed One are placed at the throat and the forehead. Then, with the three syllables, one should begin the joy of union.

Then, with the light rays of the seed at one's own heart, with the blazing mudrā, and also with the syllable phaiṃ, one summons the wisdom wheel. Having made offerings to it with the sixteen goddesses, one causes it to enter fully into one's own deities, each as is fitting, reciting oṃ yo ga śuddhaḥ sarba dharmā yo ga śuddho'haṃ, and one also performs the lotus-turning gesture and the gesture of embrace. One should bless with the three mantras beginning oṃ āḥ hūṃ.

Then the Vajrayoginīs who have come together with the wisdom wheel, holding skull cups filled with the nectar of wisdom and ḍamarus, and the offering goddesses emanated from the seed at the heart, the Goddess of the Flute and the others, recite:

Just as at the very moment of his birth
all the tathāgatas performed the bathing,
so likewise with pure divine water
I perform the bathing.

So reciting, and holding in their left hands skull cups filled with the nectar of wisdom, slightly tilted, they confer empowerment upon oneself, and one should meditate on oneself as having the nature of great bliss. The tathāgatas formed from the remaining water are also on the crowns of the heads of oneself and the deities. One should recite oṃ sarba ta thā ga ta a bhi ṣe ka sa ma ya śri ye hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. Among these, for the Blessed One it is Akṣobhya, resting on a crossed vajra; for the Blessed Lady, Vairocana; for Ḍākinī and the others, Ratneśa; and for Kākāsyā and the others, Amoghasiddhi.

Then, having made offerings to the lord of the maṇḍala, together with the deities of the maṇḍala, with the samaya offerings and the rest presented by the goddesses emanated from the seed at one's own heart, one draws up the nectar with the tongue by reciting, and tastes it. One should then praise the Blessed One and the Blessed Lady with the mantras of eight lines and through the emanated goddesses.

In order to realize fully that all that has been taught here, and the entire three realms as well, are of one taste with emptiness and compassion, one enters fully into it. Beings, drawn in by the light rays of the breath, the charnel grounds, Kākāsyā and the others, Ḍākinī and the others of the wheel of great bliss, the Blessed Lady, the Blessed One, the sun, the hūṃ, the ū, the ha, the head stroke, the crescent moon, the drop, and the nāda: these one should cause to enter fully into luminosity, through a sequence of dissolution like the vapor of breath on a mirror. Then, rising in an instant, one should view everything as the wheel of deities, whose nature is the nonduality of ultimate emptiness and compassion.

Then one should perform the mantra recitation. At the lotus of the navel, on a moon, is the white and red nāda, together with the wheels of deities. To recite oṃ āḥ hūṃ inwardly with the entering, abiding, and rising of the wind is the vajra recitation. In just the same way, reciting from that very nāda, one should recite the heart and near-heart mantras, preceded by the emanation and gathering of the host of deities; when the recitation of each individual deity's mantra is complete, one emanates and gathers each separately. One should cultivate this, the samaya recitation.

With the conviction that all phenomena are equal, the garland of mantra enters one's own mouth, goes by the path of the vajra into the lotus, rises by the path of the avadhūtī, comes out of the mouth of the Blessed Lady, and enters fully through the door of one's own mouth. Meditating on it circling in just this way, one should recite the garland mantra: this is the swing recitation.

Here the heart mantra is oṃ śrī bajra he he ru ru kaṃ hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ ḍā ki nī jā la samba ra svā hā, and the near-heart mantra is oṃ hrīḥ ha ha hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ; these are the Blessed One's. Those of the Blessed Lady are: the heart mantra, oṃ bajra bai ro ca nī ye hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ svā hā; and the near-heart mantra, oṃ sarba buddha ḍā ki nī ye bajra barṇṇa nī ye hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ.

The garland mantras of the Blessed One and the Blessed Lady are to be taken from elsewhere.

oṃ ḍā ki nī ye hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. oṃ lā me hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. oṃ khaṇḍa ro he hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. oṃ rū pi ṇī ye hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. oṃ kā kā sya hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. oṃ u lū kā sya hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. oṃ śva nā sya hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. oṃ śū ka rā sya hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. oṃ ya ma dā dhī hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. oṃ ya ma du tī hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. oṃ ya ma daṃṣṭi ṇī ye hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. oṃ ya ma ma thā nī ye hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ. These are the mantras of the heroines.

Then, in front, upon a wind born from yaṃ, and upon a fire born from raṃ, resting on a hearth of three human heads, is a lotus vessel born from a white syllable a. In its center one generates food and the like, the syllables oṃ āḥ traṃ khaṃ hūṃ and lāṃ māṃ pāṃ tāṃ bāṃ, and the nectars and the five lamps blessed by them. Then, as the wind stirs, the fire is kindled, and by its heat the seed syllables and the rest melt; one should view them as having the color of the rising sun and the form of liquid. Above that, one should behold a khaṭvāṅga arisen from a syllable hūṃ like quicksilver, of the nature of nectar, turned face downward and melting into the vessel, and one should meditate that everything has become of equal taste.

One should visualize the syllables oṃ āḥ hūṃ, born from the vowels and consonants, set one above another. From them one emanates the deities of the wheel of the maṇḍala; having accomplished the benefit of beings, and having melted through first entering into union, they are dissolved, just as they are, into those three syllables, and one views these too as gradually melting into nectar. With them one should bless the torma as much as one wishes.

After that, pressing down the right foot with the left foot, and gazing to the left upon the torma, one turns the hands face downward. The tips of the thumbs touch each other; above them the tips of the two index fingers are linked like a chain; the middle fingers are brought together into a point; and the ring fingers and little fingers are extended. One oneself should circle it above the forehead, to the left and the other way. This is the blazing mudrā.

Then one views the heroes and yoginīs dwelling in the ten directions as invited by the syllable phaiṃ. Toward those deities, with the left and the other hand, reciting sa ma ya stvaṃ sa ma ya hoḥ, and circling to the left and the other way, one should, by that circling, please them with the torma, preceded by the firm conviction that the object of offering, the one who offers, and the offering are inseparable.

indra ya ma ba ru ṇa ku be ra ī śa agni rākṣa sa pā yu candra sūrya brahma ā di be ma ci tra pra thi bi de ba tāḥ sa pa ri vā ra ā karṣa ya jaḥ hūṃ baṃ hoḥ saṃ tṛ pa taḥ sarba de ba tāḥ sā dha ka śīntaṃ ku ru sarba ke ryaṃ ku ru siddhir bha ba tu ya thā su khaṃ oṃ ka ra ka ra ku ru ku ru bandha bandha trā sa ya trā sa ya kṣo bha ya kṣo bha ya hrauṃ hrauṃ hraḥ hraḥ pheṃ pheṃ phaṭ phaṭ da ha da ha pa ca pa ca bhakṣa bhakṣa ba sa ru dhi ra antra mā lā ba la mbi ni gṛhṇa gṛhṇa sapta pā tā la ga ta bhu jaṃ ga sarbambā tarja ya ta rja ā kaḍḍhya hriṃ hriṃ jṇauṃ jṇauṃ haṃ haṃ hiṃ hiṃ hūṃ hūṃ ki li ki li si li si li hi li hi li dhi li dhi li hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ phaṭ svā hā

Reciting this once for each, one should circle the torma, as is fitting, for the Blessed One together with his consort, wisdom, and for Ḍākinī and the others, ending with Yamamathanī. One should offer it reciting oṃ bajra a ralli hoḥ jaḥ hūṃ baṃ hoḥ bajra ḍā ki nī sa ma ya staṃ dṛ śya hoḥ once, twice, three, four, and five times.

Then one should circle it, as before, for the eight charnel grounds, the guardians of the directions, and the others. One should offer it with oṃ kha kha khā hi khā hi sarba yakṣa rākṣa sa bhū ta pre ta pi śā ca un maṃ da a basma ra ḍā ka ḍā ki nya da ya i maṃ ba liṃ gṛhṇantu sa ma yaṃ rakṣa ntu sarba siddhi mme pra ya cchantu ya thai baṃ ya the ṣṭaṃ bhuñja tha pi ba tha ji ghra tha mā ti kra ma tha ma ma sarba kā rta ya satsu khaṃ pṛddha ye sa hā yi ka bha bantu hūṃ hūṃ phaṭ phaṭ svā hā.

Then, having praised them properly with the mantra of eight lines and requested the accomplishments one desires, one recites the hundred-syllable mantra and gives a signal with a snap of the fingers. Reciting oṃ yo ga śuddhaḥ sarba dharmā yo ga śuddho'haṃ, one should please them with the lotus-turning mudrā. When withdrawing the mudrā, too, preceded by the gesture of embrace, one should snap the ring and little fingers.

oṃ You have accomplished every aim of sentient beings;
grant the accomplishments that accord with each.
Depart to the buddha realm,
and abide in bliss as you please.

Reciting this and oṃ bajra muḥ, one should send them off, and cause that entire wheel to enter fully into oneself.

When one wishes to rise, one dissolves the four, Ḍākinī and the others, into the anthers of the lotuses at the heart, the throat, the navel, and the forehead; the four, Kākāsyā and the others, into the mouth, the left nostril, the anus, and the right nostril; and the four, Yamadāḍhī and the others, into the right and left ears and the eyes. Having understood that they abide in the form of the doors of speech and the rest, one should learn from the oral instructions the following: here the Blessed One, the vajra of the awakened mind, dwelling below the crown of the head, fully embraces all the deities, and from his full embrace with Vajravārāhī, born from the secret lotus, the joy that is the great bliss of the mind arises as one's own nature.

Again and again one firmly resolves that the body maṇḍala is utterly stable, and by firmly resolving that oneself is like the body whose nature is great bliss, in which emptiness, the very self of wisdom, and compassion are of one taste, one should conduct oneself gracefully, in whatever way one wishes. So it is said.

By the stainless merit that has arisen
from this condensed means of accomplishment of Cakrasaṃvara,
may these limitless beings
swiftly attain the state of the Vajra Lord.

So it is said. The Means of Accomplishment of Glorious Saṃvarodaya is complete. It was translated by the Tibetan translator, the monk Shong Lodrö Ten, under the great Nepalese paṇḍita Kṣāntiśrī, at Gautamabhihari in Nepal.

Translator's notes

The sixteen offering goddesses are rendered by the meaning of their Tibetan names (flower, incense, lamp, and so on), since the exact Sanskrit forms used in this text are not certain. In the description of the protection wheel, the tent and canopy are each said to be "beneath" the preceding element, which is translated as it stands. The phrase rendered "holding a skull cup to the side" (thod pa yon por) is somewhat uncertain; it may mean holding the skull cup tilted. "Of half-and-half form" renders phyed gzugs ma, probably referring to the two-colored bodies of the four corner goddesses; the exact sense is not certain. The phrase rendered "draws up the nectar with the tongue" uses ro ma (Skt. rasanā), which here, in the context of tasting, is taken in its sense of "tongue" rather than the right channel. The term introducing the recitation section reads skyon sngags ("fault mantra"), which does not make clear sense; it is translated simply as "the mantra recitation." The sentence on placing the heart and near-heart mantras at the navel, heart, throat, and forehead is compressed in the Tibetan and is rendered as closely as its syntax allows. "Gazing to the left upon the torma" renders a terse phrase (gyon pa gtor ma'i steng du blta) whose subject, perhaps the left eye, is not stated. "Conduct oneself gracefully, in whatever way one wishes" takes rnam par dpyad pa ("examine") as a slip for the similar-sounding rnam par spyad pa ("conduct oneself"), which fits the close of the practice. The finger snap renders tsho ma'i lag pa, taken from Sanskrit choṭikā, "snapping of the fingers." The place name Gautamabhihari is given as spelled; it is probably a monastery, a Gautama Vihāra. In the list of dissolutions the Tibetan writes kha gdong ma, evidently a slip for khwa gdong ma, Kākāsyā, as in the earlier lists.
