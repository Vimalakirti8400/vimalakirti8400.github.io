About this text

Toh 568 in the Kangyur is a dhāraṇī text known in Sanskrit as the Ārya Jayavatī Nāma Dhāraṇī and in Tibetan as Pakpa Gyalwa Chen Zhejawa'i Zung, meaning "The Noble Dhāraṇī Called 'She Who Possesses Victory.'" It belongs to the class of dhāraṇī and vidyā mantra literature, presenting a sacred incantation taught by the Buddha for the benefit, welfare, and happiness of all sentient beings and for the protection of worldly rulers. The text is set on Vulture Peak Mountain near Rājagṛha, where Vajrapāṇi, the great lord of the yakṣa host, approaches the Buddha and requests the teaching of this vidyā mantra queen known as Jayavatī. As a dhāraṇī sūtra, it sits within the broader collection of such protective and meritorious scripture texts preserved in the Kangyur.

In the language of India: Ārya Jayavatī Nāma Dhāraṇī. In the language of Tibet: The Noble Dhāraṇī Called "She Who Possesses Victory."

Homage to all buddhas and bodhisattvas.

Thus did I hear. At one time the Blessed One was dwelling together with a great Saṅgha of monks — fully a thousand monks — on Vulture Peak Mountain near Rājagṛha. Then Vajrapāṇi, the great lord of the yakṣa host, went to where the Blessed One was and, having arrived, prostrated with his head at the feet of the Blessed One and addressed him with these words:

"For the benefit, welfare, and happiness of all sentient beings, and for the welfare of human kings, may the Blessed One, the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly and completely awakened buddha, please teach this great queen of vidyā mantras called Jayavatī."

Then the Blessed One, looking with affection upon Vajrapāṇi, the great lord of the yakṣa host, proclaimed the great queen of vidyā mantras called Jayavatī:

"Vajrapāṇi, this queen of vidyā mantras called Jayavatī — spoken by many tens of millions of buddhas, blessed by them, and proclaimed for the benefit, happiness, and loving care of all sentient beings — you, together with me, receive this as I now expound it as a deity:

Tadyathā: Jaye jaye mahājayavatīye buddhādhiṣṭhitiye dharma-avabhāsani buddhadhigamajñānāvabhāse svāhā.

Vajrapāṇi, these are the root mantra syllables of the secret mantra, consecrated upon the crown by the Tathāgata. Vajrapāṇi, whoever wears upon their body or writes in a book these words — consecrated upon the crown by the Tathāgata — for that person, no hostile harm will arise within a range of a thousand leagues from their body. Weapons, poison, and snake venom cannot affect them. In the realm of that king, no harm from adversaries will arise. Enemies will not be able to cause harm to that region by their power. Vajrapāṇi, whatever son of good family or daughter of good family writes this queen of vidyā mantras, Jayavatī, in letters and bears it, for as long as they live, weapons, poison, water, plague, and other poisons compounded by others will not be able to harm their body. Fear of enemies will not arise for them, nor harm, nor ambush. Fear of kings will not arise. Fear of thieves will not arise. Even if they enter into the midst of battle, weapons will not strike their body. Everywhere that person will speak victoriously. Without injury and without having uttered a word of slander, through the power, majesty, strength, and blessing of this queen of vidyā mantras, Jayavatī, they will be victorious.

At that time, the great king Vaiśravaṇa, together with his retinue of attendants and with tens of millions, hundreds of thousands of multitudes of yakṣas, will completely pacify those contagious diseases and those calamities among people.

This great queen of all vidyā mantras, the supreme among all vidyā mantras, Jayavatī — in whatever land and wherever it comes to be employed, there and in those very places the deeds of a buddha will be performed."

Then the great king Vaiśravaṇa went to where the Blessed One was, arrived there, prostrated with his head at the feet of the Blessed One, and spoke these words to the Blessed One: "Blessed One, I too will bestow protection upon this enumeration of Dharma teachings — complete shelter and complete holding, warding off injury, warding off weapons, destroying poison, repelling enemies, preventing untimely death, and repelling all fighting, quarreling, dispute, dissension, warfare, bloodshed, and every fear. Whoever transgresses this victorious great queen of mantras, Sulalā, shall be split into seven pieces like the fruit of the śirīṣa tree.

Whoever carries, reads, venerates, recites, writes out, or inscribes in a book this victorious great queen of mantras — the Great Victorious One — and places it in a village, a city, a market town, a province, a royal palace and its grounds, or a temple: I, together with my retinue of attendants, accompanied by many hundreds of thousands of millions of yakṣas, shall follow in that mantra-holder's footsteps and protect them. "Vajrapāṇi, take hold of this victorious great queen of mantras, embrace it, and make it fully complete."

"Vajrapāṇi, in a future time — in the final period of five hundred years, when the sacred Dharma is in utter decline — fierce sentient beings will arise, unbearable and color-stealing yakṣas and rākṣasas, and kings who act contrary to Dharma. The Blessed One declared: "Vajrapāṇi, therefore hold it as the Tathāgata-essence and Mudrā. Hold it also as the Victorious Destroyer of Every Māra-Maṇḍala."

When the Blessed One had spoken these words, Vajrapāṇi, the Lord of Secrets, and

This concludes the noble dhāraṇī called the Victorious One.

Translator's notes

The transliterated mantra syllables in this text are Sanskrit rendered phonetically into Tibetan script. They have been restored to plausible Sanskrit phonetic form as closely as the Tibetan orthography permits. Readers should note that several syllables in the longer mantra sequence are garbled or metrically irregular in the Tibetan transcription, which is common in canonical dhāraṇī texts; the restorations offered here follow the Tibetan faithfully without emendation.

The phrase rendered "you, together with me, receive this as I now expound it as a deity" reflects the Tibetan "ང་ཡང་ང་ལྷར་འཆད་པ་འདི་ཟུང་ཤིག" which is syntactically ambiguous and possibly corrupt; a literal reading yields something like "receive this which I, too, expound as a god" or "receive this which I expound as both myself and as a deity." The translation adopts the most coherent available reading. The list of spirit-types includes several terms that reward brief comment. "Gongma-spirit" renders གོང་མའི་གདོན, a category of possessing spirit associated with those who have died without proper funerary rites, sometimes rendered "upper-realm spirit" or left transliterated; the rendering here keeps it descriptive. "Dri za" (gandharva) is rendered "gandharva-spirit" as these are smell-eating celestials also functioning as spirit-entities in dhāraṇī literature. The phrase མི་ངས་མི་འབྱུང is rendered "no epidemic will arise," reading mi-ngas as epidemic or pestilence, though it could also mean "no human plague" in a more general sense — either reading is doctrinal defensible and the difference is not consequential. The text ends mid-sentence as supplied; the translation preserves this break faithfully. The mantra syllables are transliterated as closely as the Tibetan phonetic rendering permits; some syllables represent degraded Sanskrit forms standard in dhāraṇī literature, and exact Sanskrit reconstruction is not always possible. The phrase rendered "the deeds of a buddha will be performed" translates a standard formulation indicating that the text or practice functions with the full soteriological efficacy of a buddha's activity. The final line breaks off mid-mantra as the Tibetan passage ends there; this is a continuation passage and the mantra of Vaiśravaṇa continues in the following section. The epithet "color-stealing" for the yakṣas renders a term suggesting beings who drain vitality or luster from their victims, a standard description in the literature of malevolent supernatural forces. The phrase is rendered literally here; some editions gloss it as beings who steal the healthy complexion and life-force of humans.

The name "Sulalā" in the curse formula is a dhāraṇī-deity name embedded in the mantra string; it is left as a proper name consistent with the preceding passage.

The phrase rendered "nine hundred and ninety million buddhas" represents the Tibetan "bye ba phrag dgu bcu rtsa dgu," literally ninety-nine ten-millions, which equals nine hundred and ninety million.
