About this text

Toh 571 in the Kangyur is a short canonical sūtra known in Sanskrit as the Ārya Jiguli Nāma Vidyā and in Tibetan as Pakpa Duksel Zhejawa Rikpa (འཕགས་པ་དུག་སེལ་ཞེས་བྱ་བ་རིག་པ་), meaning "The Noble Wisdom-Mantra Called 'Poison Remover.'" It belongs to the class of dhāraṇī or vidyā texts — scripture centered on a protective mantra — and its content concerns the Buddha recounting, in the form of a past-life narrative, the origin and virtues of a powerful mantra capable of removing poison. The text is set in the familiar sūtra frame of the Buddha addressing his monks at Śrāvastī, lending it the authority of direct Buddhavacana. It was translated into Tibetan from Sanskrit by the Indian masters Jinamitra and Dānaśīla together with the great Tibetan editor-translator Yeshé Dé, and was subsequently revised in accordance with the standardized new terminology of the imperial translation period.

Translation

Translated by the Indian masters Jinamitra and Dānaśīla, and the great editor-translator the monk Yeshé Dé; revised and finalized according to the new style of terminology.

In the language of India: Ārya Jiguli Nāma Vidyā.
In the language of Tibet: The Noble Wisdom-Mantra Called "Poison Remover."

Homage to all the buddhas and bodhisattvas.

Thus did I hear. At one time the Blessed One was dwelling at Śrāvastī in the Jeta Grove, the garden of Anāthapiṇḍada, in the company of Prince Jeta. Then the Blessed One addressed the monks:

"Monks, long ago in a past age, when I was still a bodhisattva, on the northern face of the Himalayas, on a fragrant mountain, dwelling in a pass of the mountain-king, there came a youth bearing the marks of a hundred merits, wearing a garment of antelope hide, adorned with a belt ornamented with snakes, his head crowned with a dreadful serpent, wearing fearsome earrings to behold, eating poisonous flowers, drinking the sap of the malūta plant. He called out to me:

'Come here, child. Come close and hear me. I have a noble wisdom-mantra called "Poison Remover," a sacred spell that destroys poison. Whatever poisons there may be, every one of them shall be destroyed by my name.'

Tadyathā: oṃ ili mitte, tili mitte, ili tili mitte, dumbe, dumbāli ye, dusme, dusmaliye, tarke, takaraṇe, marme, marmeraṇe, kaśmiri, kaśmīra, mugte, aghe, aghaṇe, aghanaghaṇe, ilī alī e, akhā, ī e, abyā, i e, śvete, śata, duṇḍe, anuragtesvāhā.

Monks, this wisdom-mantra of mine that removes poison is the giving of all that is white and pure. Whoever hears this wisdom-mantra even once: for seven years no snake shall eat him; poison shall not take hold in his body. If any snake seizes him, that snake's head shall split into seven pieces like an arjaka tree's vessel. These words of the secret mantra should not be recited before a snake, and why is that? Because the snake would die.

Tadyathā: ilalā, cililī, cakrabko, kauḍa, koḍeḍi, kuruta, kuruḍati, poḍāti, burudā, purudeti, moḍeti, nikuruḍe, nikuruḍe, nimamuruta, nimamruḍete, purude, phuṭṭareha, phuṭṭaphuṭṭaṭṭaṃḍarahe, nāgarāhe, nagadatadataṇḍarahe, sarvarahe, sarvatattadaraha, acchi acchi, puruṭata, calabiṣe, śīte, śītā, bandhale, mātāle, halehale, halahala, halalele, elalale, kepacale, milale, taṇḍihaṇḍi, taṭataṭa, sphuṭasphuṭa, phaṭṭaphaṭṭa, haraviśvasvāhā.

Monks, thus it is that whatever wisdom-mantras are recited and proclaimed in this Poison Remover for the assembly of all the spirits gathered there — every one of them is thus, is not otherwise, is correct, is true, is unerring, is unfailing. May this poison be rendered harmless. Go to the one who gives! Go to the one who holds! Go into fire! Go into water! Go to the plain! Go into a pillar! Go into a wall! Go into the sky! Go into the earth! Be pacified! Svāhā."

When the Blessed One had spoken these words, those monks were gladdened and praised what the Blessed One had taught.

The Noble Wisdom-Mantra Called "Poison Remover" is complete.

Translator's notes

The youth described in the bodhisattva's past-life narrative bears unmistakable iconographic attributes of the Great God (Śiva): antelope hide, snake ornaments, serpent crown, and fearsome earrings. The text does not name him explicitly as Śiva, but the description is a standard Śaiva depiction found across Buddhist literature of this type. The passage presents the mantra as originating from this figure and being transmitted to the bodhisattva.

The phrase rendered "all that is white and pure" reflects the Tibetan dkar po thams cad, a standard idiom meaning all virtuous and meritorious giving; it characterizes the mantra as encompassing the entirety of pure generosity.

The phrase rendered "the assembly of all the spirits" translates 'byung po 'dus pa thams cad, which refers to assembled elemental spirits or demon-beings; the declaration that the mantra-words are "thus, not otherwise, true, and unfailing" is a standard truth-declaration formula invoking the power of truth to neutralize poison.

The mantra syllables are transcribed from the Tibetan phonetic rendering and cannot be reconstructed with full certainty into a normalized Sanskrit form; they are given here as they appear.
