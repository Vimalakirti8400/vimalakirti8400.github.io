About this text

The Secret Tantra of the Ritual Manual of Krodhavijaya, known in Sanskrit as the Krodhavijayakalpaguhyatantra and in Tibetan as Khro bo rnam par rgyal ba'i rtog pa gsang ba'i rgyud, is a tantric scripture centered on Yamāntaka, the wrathful form of the bodhisattva Mañjuśrī, here called Krodhavijaya, the Victorious Wrathful One. It opens with the terrifying appearance of this wrathful deity, before whom the other wrathful ones drop their weapons and the worldly gods bow down in fear and offer praise, and it goes on to teach his mantras, mudrās and ritual procedures. Much of the text consists of ritual instructions in verse: rites of protection, healing and pacification, rites for bringing rain or stopping hail, rites of subjugation and destruction directed at enemies, and the construction of the secret wheel diagram called the Hela wheel together with the twelve postures of the wrathful practitioner. Its chapters are presented as sections of a secret ritual manual belonging to the root rite of Mañjuśrī. It is Toh 604 in the Kangyur, where it belongs to the Tantra section, among the action-oriented ritual tantras associated with Mañjuśrī and Yamāntaka.

Homage to the Blessed One Mañjughoṣa.

Then the wrathful one of Mañjughoṣa,
this wrathful one named Yamāntaka,
turbulent with weapons and blazing tongues of flame,
fierce and endowed with fierce power and might:
when they saw him, all the other
wrathful ones let their weapons fall.
Likewise the worldly
gods bowed down with their bodies,
and moreover, joining their palms,
in terror they offered many praises.
All the many gods, however many there were,
as though tormented by the splendor
of Mañjuśrī's wrathful one, declared:
"Blessed One, we have the Buddha,
the Dharma and the Saṅgha as our refuge,
and we have seen your utterly unbearable, terrifying form,
pervading the directions with blazing splendor,
throwing the three realms into turmoil."
When the gods and the asuras,
terrified, had gone for refuge,
then the wrathful one of Mañjughoṣa
became peaceful once again.
When the great wrathful one
called Yamāntaka was at peace,
all the gods who had gone for refuge
then asked him:
"We who seek your supreme diligence,
how are we to please you?
In what way will you be gracious?
We have great doubt."
The wrathful one heard the words of the gods,
and Yamāntaka declared:
"All you gods, listen!
I arise before the three times;
I arose before all the buddhas.
I arise after the three times as well;
I arise in the present of the three times.
At the time of the last five hundred years,
I cause my teaching to arise.
When all the teachings have declined,
I cause my teaching to arise.
When evil deeds of immediate retribution are done,
then my teaching arises.
When people eat foul and heavy food,
then my teaching arises.
When they wear poor clothes and tattered rags,
then my teaching arises.
I am the body that arises in the three times;
when all the teachings have declined,
then my teaching arises.
In whatever way things become peaceful,
I am the body of all peace;
in whatever way they become wrathful,
all wrath is my body.
All of you of great splendor,
since you have gone for refuge to the Three,
keep my unbearable
wrathful one abiding in your minds.
Because the Three Jewels abide,
and because their command is upheld,
and out of loving compassion for all sentient beings,
my mind has become peaceful.
You who have gone for refuge,
endowed with the mind of awakening:
the supreme accomplisher of every aim,
these seventeen syllables,
my heart essence, arisen from my mind,
all you gods must hold!"
When Yamāntaka himself
had proclaimed the heart essence,
the earth then quaked,
the ocean too was violently churned,
and the yakṣas, the rākṣasas,
the piśācas and the rest were destroyed.
All the gods bowed down,
the hair on all their limbs standing on end,
and took up that wrathful heart essence
of the wrathful one Yamāntaka.
Then, to protect the sacred Dharma,
Yamāntaka taught them at length
this ritual manual
of Krodhavijaya.
Then the ritual manual of Krodhavijaya
was spoken by the wrathful one himself.
The gods endowed with compassion and the mind of awakening
heard it and rejoiced,
and to protect the Three Jewels
each explained a ritual of his own.
As for this root tantra
proclaimed by the wrathful one himself,
called Krodhavijaya:
the transmundane and the worldly ones
who had bowed to him before
heard it just as it came from the wrathful one's mouth;
and this tantra spoken by the wrathful one himself
the bodhisattvas,
with compassion grown vast in the same way,
blessed in many ways.

This was the first chapter, called "The Emergence of the Tantra from Krodhavijaya, the Root Ritual Manual of Mañjuśrī."

Then I shall now concisely explain
the supreme and marvelous ritual
of Krodhavijaya,
taught by Mañjuśrī's wrathful one.
Here, its maṇḍala,
which bestows all accomplishments
and throws the three worlds into turmoil,

In what place is this maṇḍala
accomplished in every respect?
The mantra practitioner, first of all,
should examine its site:
a solitary tree, or a charnel ground,
an empty house, a crossroads,
a stūpa, a place where two borders meet,
an empty thicket, an old road,
a pleasure garden, a mountain peak,
a riverbank, a forest,
or else the shore of a lake;
a shrine of the Great God (Śiva) is suitable as well,
or a cattle pen not trampled down.
Above all, in a clean place,
wherever the mind is also glad,
there one should make the maṇḍala.
Then the vidyādhara should diligently
purify the ground properly:
free of salt, uncracked,
level and free of thorns,
high toward the east and north,
its color rich and especially good.
Smear it with cow dung
mixed with the five products of the cow.
Having smeared it as is fitting,
the master, having bathed,
having kept a fast and settled in meditative equipoise,
should bless it with the wrathful one's protection.
At night, draw the supreme secret maṇḍala
called the Great Host of Pretas.
With a beautiful line of three twisted strands
made of linen or cotton cloth,
the wise one should lay out the lines of the maṇḍala,
with four corners and four doors:
first from the east and the north
to the south and the west.
That line, from the intermediate directions,
should then be drawn straight.
At the southeast corner
and at the northwest corner,
measure it well with the line,
and lay out the lines as a square.

Beautiful with a single tier,
it should be adorned with four doors.
The southern door is left open,
and the rest should be closed.
In the center, draw a lotus
proportioned to the maṇḍala.
The lotus and the doors of the four directions

The ornament should be made half the door;
its sides are equal in measure to the petals.
The secondary ornament as well
should be made like the ornament.
Having laid out the lines of the maṇḍala

afterwards the vidyādhara, in the proper way,
draws with ash from the charnel ground.
Always within the corners,
the northeast and the rest,
the wise one draws the cudgel mudrā.
At the doors of the directions, place pretas,
and a fifth preta besides
should be visualized upon the lotus.
Likewise, inside the outer doors
and in all of those directions,
draw headless human corpses, pretas,
starkly, with ash from the charnel ground.
That is why it is the Great Host of Pretas.
Its size runs in steps from one cubit
up to eight cubits;
never draw it larger than that.
For one's own constant worship,
one of a single cubit should be drawn.
For pacifying and for increase,
and for rites concerning kings, two cubits are taught.
For driving away all beings,
and likewise for destroying, three cubits.
Four cubits is declared the best
for the acts of subjugation and summoning.
Five cubits is best for striving to eliminate
disease, pestilence among people, and enemies.
Six cubits surely turns back
all obstructors and grahas.
Seven cubits performs
the acts of subjugating and summoning the gods.
Eight cubits should be known as supreme
for all accomplishments and for fire offerings.
Having drawn it in whatever way is fitting,
one who is pure should then touch it.
For the water, a water vessel
should be filled to the brim with water.
Likewise, for the flowers,
another fine vessel should be filled well
with lovely, fragrant flowers and garlands of flowers,
and made beautiful.
For the scent,
take white and red sandalwood,
and likewise guggulu incense
mixed with aloeswood.
For lamps, butter lamps,
and the wise one uses a soft wick;
for fierce rites,
it is taught that human fat is used.
As torma, offer fish, meat and so on,
and especially mixed porridge and the like.
Having gathered the requisites there in this way,
the holder of the vidyā-mantra
bows to all the buddhas
and focuses on the mind of awakening.
If one is unable
to carry out the work of drawing,
then upon the lotus drawn earlier
in the center of the Great Host of Pretas,
the wise one should imagine
the preta with the mind.
Having made this visualization
with the supreme mantra of the preta,
the four cudgels of the corners as well
should be visualized with their own mantras.
If one is able to draw them,
the mantra itself should be placed upon them.
If one is unable to draw
the four pretas of the doors as well,
it is taught that they are likewise to be blessed
with the mantra that seizes pretas.
In order to bless them within
with their own mantra,
imagine legs, arms, heads and shoulders,
and likewise further shoulders,
and likewise garlands of hands,
skulls, skeletons, fresh human skins,
and garlands of corpses.
Within it, cause them to be blessed
with their own mantra.
Having visualized in this way according to the rite,
at the end, invite the wrathful Yamāntaka
by reciting his mantra,
and request him to be seated with his own heart mantra.
The wrathful one, turbulent and terrifying,
his body like blazing flames,
noble in splendor, is to be visualized,
his complexion radiant as if crushing all pride.
He has six faces, his hair is tawny,
his brows are tawny and his eyes are yellow;
he has three eyes, and his eyes are round;
his form has twelve arms.
He sits likewise upon a preta,
beautified by six legs.
A trident, a sword, a staff, an axe,
a hand outstretched in giving,
and the weapon of the hook:
these he holds likewise in his right hands.
Likewise the six on the left:
a skull cup, a short spear, a great arrow,
the forefinger raised in threat, a noose held,
and a club in hand; so he is to be visualized.
He is adorned with fresh and dried human heads
and with garlands of pretas,
and wears a garment of tiger skin.
His body is huge and his belly is huge;
his faces have many shapes,
his hair is like a turbulent ocean.
One face laughs, one face is peaceful,
one face glares terrifyingly with wide eyes,
one face has its mouth gaping open
and moves its tongue about,
and one face bares its fangs.
Thus, having invited him well with the heart mantra,
one should offer him the welcoming water.
Then, having offered the welcoming water according to the rite
with water mixed with scent,
place a red flower
on the crown of the preta as well.
Then the one of great intelligence
should offer red sandalwood to the wrathful one.
After the scent come the flowers;
offer a torma of sandalwood.
Flowers and garlands of flowers
should be offered with the flower mantra.
Having offered the flowers in due order,
according to the rite, with the flower mantra,
then, with his own heart mantra as well,
on the crown of the wrathful one
and at the heart of the chief wrathful one
the heart mantra should be set in place.
At his fontanelle place the fontanelle mantra,
and on his topknot the crest mantra;
on his body the armor,

These, as here taught,
should be set in place with flowers.
Having worshiped in whatever way is fitting,

Then blazing lamps
should be set out in the eight directions as well.
Having offered the bhūta torma to the wrathful one,
outside the Great Host of Pretas
the wise one
should give the outer tormas.
Facing toward the chief wrathful one,
then once again offer incense.
Outside it, a vessel with water
filled with flowers is set down.
Having summoned the disciples,
who have bathed and fasted according to the rite,
one should kindle splendor in them with all the mantras,
and with the heart mantra of the wrathful one
give flowers into their two hands
and cover their faces with a cloth.
Having had them recollect the Three Jewels,
the master should lead them in.
With his own heart mantra, have them
cast the flower itself onto the maṇḍala.
Then the mantra practitioner shows them
the mudrā called the Cudgel.
The secret pledge of the Three Jewels
should be given to them.
Then, for the sake of accomplishment,
the mantra practitioner, having empowered them
with water and flowers, gives them
the secret mantra of the chief wrathful one.
Then, having empowered them according to the rite
with the heart mantra of the wrathful one,

with flowers, incense and garlands
the heart mantra should be given to them.
When they in turn have thus received
the supreme heart mantra of the wrathful one,
they should then bow to the guru
and honor him with gifts as best they can.
If the mantra practitioner honors the guru,
accomplishment will be gained; not otherwise.
Having given the disciples the heart mantra,
and having worshiped with garlands, sandalwood and so on,
after that, take up the vessel
placed outside earlier,
filled with scented water and the like,
and, having offered the welcoming water,
request the unbearable wrathful one of Mañjughoṣa,
fierce in his might, to depart.
Once he has departed, that offering water
should be offered to the maṇḍala as well.
In the maṇḍala on the vast ground,
the holder of vidyā-mantras
who desires unequaled accomplishment
should always perform the worship well in this way.
Then he will obtain accomplishment.

Next, further, I shall explain the mudrā of Mañjughoṣa's wrathful one, Ekavīra, the Sole Hero, the great being of boundless splendor. First, having displayed the pledge of the Three Jewels, one should bind the right hand well and firmly, according to the rite. The vidyādharas, always, with all the fingers of the hand hide the thumb well and bind it tightly and well. This is the cudgel mudrā, as the rite calls it. Next, accomplish all that has been taught

has been fully explained.

food of any kind whatsoever,

fill that skull cup.

to any sentient beings at all.
After doing this properly,

For this practice of the skull cup that has been explained,
it is to be held in the left hand.
A sharp, well-made short spear

Having made the fire offering of incense,

Covering the index finger of the left hand

If the finger of the left hand

The summits of all the mountains,

The supreme accomplishment of the finger

Taking up that blazing staff,

becomes, as before, the chief

Through the smoke, the heat, and the rest,

The practice of the supreme weapons
has thus been explained in summary.
Next, the other accomplishments
that come from the rites are to be explained,
just as the Supreme Wrathful One
himself explained them in summary.
Having first drawn the maṇḍala
called Many Pretas,
and having obtained an unwounded corpse,
the wise one washes it and adorns it well.
Having worshiped with flowers and incense,
one gives a torma together with meat.
The rite is to be done in charnel grounds;
it is never to be done anywhere else.
On a bed strewn with flowers,
one lays it with its head toward the western door.
Taking a fine skull cup
filled to the brim with excellent food,
one should also pour human fat
into that excellent food.
At that time, the wise one,
if with a ladle he pours into the man's mouth
a full one hundred oblations
empowered with the Wrathful One's mantra,
then its tongue will come forth.
Then it is to be cut with a blade.
From the tongue cut at its root,
through the power of the Wrathful One, swiftly,
that very thing turns into the form
of thirty-two fine swords.
Then, taking up the swords,
one gives one to each of one's companions.
When the practitioner takes one up,
he is accomplished on the instant.
He becomes a king honored
in every way by all those vidyādharas,
and together with those companions
he will live for seven eons.
Or else, the wise one, in a charnel ground,
having then drawn the maṇḍala
called Many Pretas,
should make the offerings as they have been taught.
Having obtained the unwounded corpse
of a man or woman who has died,
washed it well, and adorned it with ornaments,
one places it at the door of that maṇḍala,
and, sitting on its heart,
one should recite one thousand and eight times.
The mantra of the Supreme Wrathful One,
of seventeen syllables, one thousand and eight times:
having recited it, one attains accomplishment.
The corpse then rises up.
One should offer it water for the guest well.
If one then at once touches
its head with the left hand,
realgar, like the color of fire,
will then swiftly come forth
from the mouth of that being.
The practitioner takes it,
and, having smeared all his limbs with it,
becomes the color of refined gold.
He gives it likewise to his companions,
and at once all of them
become the color of refined gold.
They take whatever forms they wish,
and they become the foremost
of all the vidyādharas.
When all the substances have been accomplished,
then, once they are accomplished,
one should offer one portion to the Three Jewels.
Then the wise one takes his own share,
and, having given to all his companions,
flies from here into the sky.
When a single substance has been accomplished,
yet another rite is to be practiced:
if a single substance has been accomplished,
first having made prostrations,
one should fully offer oneself
to the Three Jewels according to the rite.
After that, taking the substance,
and having given that substance
to the companions in due order, one will fly.
Afterward one practices these rites.
If one does not do it in this way, then even if it is accomplished,
that substance will be stolen.
Therefore, for the supreme accomplishments,
always act just as has been explained.
Next, further, the practice of eye ointment (añjana)
and of edibles, just as it is,
in order to accomplish every need:
this sublime practice I shall explain.
Having kept a fast for three days,
one should draw the maṇḍala in a charnel ground.
Then, following the earlier rite,
one should perform the torma rite there.

Then one should protect oneself
with the ashes of a cremated corpse.
Having smeared oneself with them,
that joyful one, with excellent companions,
having smeared himself with them,
should place the essence of the eye ointment
on the head of one executed on a stake.
Having covered it with a dead person's cloth,
one holds it in the hand and recites;
until the three kinds of accomplishment appear,
for that long the intelligent one recites.
Heat or else smoke arises,
or else it blazes like fire.
If one anoints one's eyes with it,
then through the blaze one will travel through the sky.
Through the smoke, the body, it is said,
without doubt becomes invisible.
If it becomes hot, then the beings
one looks at will be killed,
and one will revive them again, fully,
before long and without doubt.
Even if one has not accomplished this,
if one anoints one's eyes well with it,
the beings one looks at come under one's power;
they kneel down and bow their heads.
Following the rite of the eye ointment,
the wise one should practice with bezoar.
Its accomplishments are taught likewise;
if it is not accomplished, beings come under one's power.
Red lead, orpiment,
and realgar likewise
the wise should practice
following the procedure for the eye ointment.
The dead person's cloth is likewise practiced according to the rite,
and the other practices in the same way.
The mantra spoken by the Wrathful One himself
I shall fully explain in summary.
One should draw the maṇḍala in a charnel ground,
and make offerings and tormas.
Having put on that dead person's cloth,
the one who knows the rite recites at night.
Then, if together with that cloth
one comes to blaze brightly at night,
blazing and surrounded by blazing light,
one soars well into the sky.
One gives forth the sound of loud laughter
that terrifies the world.
Always endowed with whatever form one desires,
one moves about like the Wrathful One.
Likewise, if smoke arises,
without doubt one becomes supreme
in invisibility, it is fully taught.
If that practitioner's cloth
likewise becomes hot,
one becomes swift of foot and of great strength,
travels everywhere across the earth,
and lives a hundred years without illness.
Even if one has not been accomplished,
all beings without exception come under one's power;
whomever one sees, likewise,
those one sees become like servants.
If a man wishes to accomplish
these yakṣiṇīs,
then, at the foot of a delightful banyan tree,
having drawn the maṇḍala,
with hair unbound and without any clothing,
having filled a fine alms bowl completely
with excellent, well-cooked food,
one practices at night during the waning fortnight.
Then, taking out seven handfuls,
and having charged them seven times with the mantra,
one says, "Fair Goddess, accept this!"
and throws them behind one's back.
Then, taking another seven handfuls,
charged with the mantra, one should eat them.
The one called the Fair Goddess,
accomplished, sovereign of all the yakṣas,
surrounded on all sides by a great retinue,
then appears, her light blazing.
Then, having rinsed one's mouth,
one should offer her water for the guest
together with incense and fine flowers,
and also offer flowers of welcome.
Then, if one offers her a fine and delightful seat
furnished with flowers,
she says to him, "Now I am accomplished for you;
I will do as you command, and in just that way,
never otherwise, I will obey your command.
Give me your command!"
She becomes a mother, or else a sister,
a beloved, or else a wife.
If one addresses her as mother, then without fail
she gives him realgar and the like,
many substances of accomplishment,
without doubt.
As a sister, she gives flowers and fruits,
supreme nectar and gold-making elixir
to that practitioner always,
without doubt.
When one says, "Become my beloved!",
at night she shows him excellent pavilions,
and other pleasing things as well,
and shows herself delighted.
Swiftly she brings lovely women,
leading them from a hundred leagues away.
Moreover, to that practitioner
she, being gentle, gives what he desires and delights.
If he addresses her as wife,
taking whatever form he desires, utterly beautiful,
with every power and every enjoyment
she delights that accomplished one.
Being gentle, she gives what he desires and delights;
smiling, respectful, granting every wish,
she provides every power and every enjoyment,
which the practitioner enjoys for as long as he wishes.
If one wishes to accomplish
the womb-born piśācīs,

I shall explain here.
At the foot of a śleṣmātaka tree
one should draw the maṇḍala in a clean place.
Having made a great offering,
one should perform the torma rite as before.

having kindled a fire with wood from a charnel ground,

then, into that blazing fire,
if with the mantra he burns
one thousand and eight fire offerings of fragments,
then, at that time, without doubt,
the piśācī with a blazing face
will come. At that time,
having made one's mind courageous,
and unafraid, one offers her water for the guest before her.
After that, to him
she shows herself in a beautiful form.
Her name is Kaṅraṅgi:
one should make fire offerings with the mantra of her own name.
Whatever wealth one thinks of,
she comes and, before long,
without doubt she offers it fully.
The one called Kaṅraṅgi
is supreme among all piśācīs.
For a practitioner who is courageous
and of very great splendor,
all grahas are fully accomplished.
If it is not so, then swiftly
she kills even the practitioner.
If the glorious vidyādhara
wishes to accomplish
the daughters of the nāgas,
this is taught as their rite.

following the former rite,

the maṇḍala called Many Pretas.

the daughters of the nāgas

are accomplished by all these rites.
Having drawn the maṇḍala as before,

Having kindled a fire in the hearth

Whatever the practitioner wishes

rākṣasas without commitment

Urging them by the practitioner's name,
without doubt
the one to be accomplished swiftly comes.
What was taught on accomplishment with red ochre
has thus been explained in brief.

Furthermore, the most wondrous
application for accomplishing white mustard seed,
just as the Wrathful One himself taught it,
I shall explain.

eight finger-widths in size.

to anything whatsoever,

and dries up rivers;
many kinds of high mountains
one crushes into powder.

Thunderbolts and hail
one makes fall suddenly.

suddenly too there is no obstruction.
Shaking lakes and oceans,
bending trees right over,

likewise threatening the nāgas,

The practice of the mustard seeds
that grant all accomplishments is complete.

Then, in the later rite,
how it may be done even without the discipline:
for that reason I shall state that rite
as the Wrathful One himself taught it.
The mantra of seventeen syllables
is what is called "the Wrathful One."
For it, first, as shown,
draw a painting, and before it
make offerings in the proper way.
Or else the wise one
may have a statue made; after that,
the wise should recite eight hundred thousand times.
At the end of that,
make a great offering to the Three Jewels.
After that, to the Supreme Wrathful One
make offerings just as was taught.
Then it is accomplished, and at that time,
bhūta grahas and poisons,
and whatever one desires in mind:
he is able to do the rites he intends.
It should never
be applied to trivial accomplishments.
Poison and the rest, whatever is spoken of,
these rites are done by words.
Even with words spoken while distracted elsewhere,
it is just as he himself wishes.
Likewise, toward hostile beings,
he is able to subdue them or to benefit them.
For one seized by a black one,
he can also perform the rites:
by sprinkling water upon him,
or with a whip or with the foot,
just as the Wrathful One taught,
he will recover without doubt.
Thus in all cases one should urge it on
by mind or else by word.
Even without being wrathful,
minor rites are accomplished;
by mere recollection alone
the doer's rite is accomplished.

Then one should properly perform
this other rite, of bringing down into a vessel.
A youth endowed with auspicious marks,
free of faults and defects, with complete limbs,
is thoroughly washed and adorned with ornaments,
and is also given the five products of the cow to drink.
After that he keeps a fast,
and at dawn he is washed,
anointed with fragrant scents,
and adorned with clothes and ornaments.
Having honored him in this way,
facing toward the east,
seat him, according to the rite,
on a seat of kuśa grass; then
into both his hands
give sandalwood, torma, and flowers.
Offer incense of black aloeswood.
Then one swiftly recites the mantra,
and by this rite of practice
one performs the rite of bringing down into a vessel.

When it has descended into the vessel,
one should ask through him about good and bad.
Then whatever has happened, what will happen,
and what is happening, he tells truly.
"Great being, do this!" he says,
and turns one back, saying, "Not this."
Vidyā-mantras that are deficient or excessive,
and mantras too, he here makes complete.
Then to that Supreme Wrathful One
present a water offering with flowers.
Then, by the teaching of the Sugata,
the commitments should also be shown.
When the vessel has heard them, then,
being released, he falls to the ground.
The vidyā, the Wrathful One Yamāntaka,
having been accomplished, then departs.
Then take up the vessel;
afterward wash him thoroughly,
give him food and let him eat,
honor him, and send him back.
Having performed the bringing down into a vessel
with the rites as they were taught,
then perform the rites
taught in the rite of the later rite.

Then, if the mantrin
wishes to bring a king under control,
at that time, in front of the painting,
draw his form with charnel-ground ash,
sit upon its heart,
and with one-pointed mind recite ten thousand times:
"May so-and-so come under my power."
Adding his name, as before,
at the end of the wrathful mantra as taught,
say svāhā at the close.
This is the application always taught
for bringing under control through the Wrathful One.
Then the king, come under control,
forsaking his dear ones,
with his own person and his wealth
will surely serve well, like a servant.
Likewise, if one does ten thousand recitations,
brahmins too come under control;
vaiśyas and śūdras, all without exception,
surely do so through six thousand.
For women and for girls
one should recite ten thousand times.

Then, if one wishes to separate
two people who are friends with each other,
draw an effigy of one of them
in the same way, place one foot
on its heart, and squat.
Adding his name at the end,
recite the Wrathful One Yamāntaka:
"May so-and-so swiftly
be separated from this one, phaṭ!"
For this separation, thus performed,
one should recite ten thousand times.

Then, knowing that a being is very vicious
and is to be tamed properly,
toward one who harms the Three Jewels
one should act with the Wrathful One, and not otherwise.
Uttering the enemy's name,
draw his effigy with charnel-ground ash.
Then recite one hundred and eight times
over a peg of khadira wood,
drive it into the effigy's heart,
and after that recite ten thousand times.
Then, putting the name at the end
of the mantra of the Wrathful One Yamāntaka:
"Kill so-and-so!",

that is explained as Binding All Around.

Spreading the left leg,

brandishing the club with outstretched arm,
with a single threat one deludes.
The one called the Swift posture

assuming a stance of great might,

raising the hand and threatening,

fiercely clap both hands,

explained likewise as being with pride,
this posture of the great being
is called Terrifier,
constantly meditating on the Wrathful One.
One sits with the feet placed evenly,
the right raised a little;
the glorious one lowers the left,
threatens with a single finger of the left,
lifts the arm and brandishes the club;
with the feet, the face is turned:
constantly meditating on the Wrathful One,
this is called Kīlikīla.
Great Kīlikīla
is that same one, looking backward,
the Wrathful One closing one eye,
and swiftly assuming the stance of worship.
This is the application of the postures,
and how, in brief, they are set out
from the sequence of the spokes of the Hela wheel:
this is the good explanation.
By the power of the mantra of the Hela wheel
and by the power and delight of the right posture,
rites that are utterly marvelous,
the rites just as they were taught, are to be done.
If one knows those accomplishments, the secret tantra,
and this Hela wheel,
one becomes one with the Wrathful One in reality,
and so will act in the language of the Wrathful One.
This ritual manual, together with its secrets,
is explained as the supreme secret essence.
Than this ritual manual of the King of Wrathful Ones
there is nothing else more supreme.
Having heard, from Krodhavijaya,
this latest of the later ritual manuals,
which is called the Utterly Secret,
and meditating on the secret meaning,
all those gods rejoiced,
bowed to the Supreme Wrathful One,

Translator's notes

The maṇḍala is named first ཡི་དགས་མང་པོ ("Many Pretas") and then ཡི་དགས་ཕལ་ཆེན ("the Great Host of Pretas"); both are translated literally, as no Sanskrit form can be confirmed. In the list of sites, the line བ་ལང་ལྷས་ས་མ་གཡུལ་གྱིས is obscure; "a cattle pen not trampled down" is a tentative reading. "Cudgel" renders བེ་ཅོན, the club-like implement drawn in the corners and also the name of a mudrā. The adverb དངར་ཏེ describing how the corpses are drawn is obscure; "starkly" is a tentative rendering. མི་རྒྱལ་སྒྲུབ་པ is taken as rites concerning kings (mi'i rgyal po), which is uncertain. སྨེ་གཏོར is an unclear term, tentatively rendered "outer tormas" from its placement outside the maṇḍala. མཚོག་མ is taken as the fontanelle, one of the body places where parts of the mantra are installed alongside heart, crown, crest, armor and weapon. "Cudgel" renders བེ་ཅོན, a club or staff, used both for the implement drawn in the maṇḍala and for the mudrā. སྨེ་གཏོར is rendered "outer tormas," following the previous passage, where these tormas are given outside the maṇḍala; the term is uncertain. The line ཡི་དགས་མང་པོ་རྟག་ཏུ་བྲི could also mean "always draw many pretas"; it is taken here as naming the Many Pretas maṇḍala, as in the torma mantra's label just before. དཔའ་བོ་གཅིག་པུ is rendered Ekavīra, matching ekabīra in the mantras. The line བེ་ཅོན་ཁྲོ་བོའི་བར་བཅུག་བྲི is unclear; it is taken as the cudgels drawn with the wrathful one set between them. ཕྱུགས་ཆེན, "great beasts," may be cattle, or may echo Sanskrit mahāpaśu, a term that in some tantric contexts denotes a human victim; it is rendered literally. The adverb དངར, applied to the many pretas drawn in the maṇḍala, is obscure and is tentatively rendered "starkly," as earlier. སྨེ་གཏོར is rendered "outer tormas," as in the preceding passages. Many of the short urging formulas here are given piecemeal within the verse; each Sanskrit element is transcribed as written. ཅེས་བདག་གིས་བྱ after the ha ha hā hā baitāli hūṃ phaṭ formula is taken as "so one should do it oneself"; the phrase is terse. འགྲོ་བདག, "the lords of beings," stands in a line one syllable short and may be defective. "The great flesh" (ཤ་ཆེན, Skt. mahāmāṃsa) conventionally denotes human flesh. The "Ha Ha" maṇḍala (ཧ་ཧར་གྱུར་པ, literally "become 'ha ha'") may allude to laughter (cf. Aṭṭahāsa), but the name is kept as written. ཡི་དགས་མང་པོ་སྦྱར་བ could also mean simply "the arrangement of many pretas." སྨེ་བ (in "incense, all of it impure") is taken in its sense of ritual impurity; this may also bear on the term སྨེ་གཏོར rendered "outer tormas" in earlier passages. ཚིལ་ཆེན, "great fat," conventionally means human fat. The line རོ་ལངས་ཕྱག་རྒྱ་སྒྲུབ་པ་བྲི reads བྲི ("draw"), which does not fit; since the next line announces "a third practice," the evident reading is གཉིས ("two"), and that is translated. The mantra's hanamtha and bundha are transcribed as written. The line listing "subjugating and compelling" reads literally "subjugating and doing/making"; the second term is taken as making others act, but its exact technical sense is uncertain. "Without pennants" renders 'phan med pa literally; the sense of this qualifier of the handle is unclear. In "warmth, smoke, and blazing" the text reads dud 'thul, taken as an evident slip for dud pa, "smoke," in light of the following verses. The line rendered "with the splendor of a great being" (sems can chen po de yi gzis) is obscure. Ganaka (ga na ka) and śarapuṅkhā (sha ra bu ka) are tentative identifications of ingredient names given in transliteration; mahānala and mohanī are likewise reconstructed from the transliterated forms. "Great fat" is the usual tantric code for human fat. "The top should be closed up well" renders rtse mo rab tu sbyar bar bya, whose exact sense is uncertain. The "leaves of a tree tossed by a whirlwind, those that float, together with what remains" is an uncertain rendering of an obscure ingredient line. In "whoever is touched by this powder," the text reads rig pa, taken as an evident slip for reg pa, "touched." The line "take earth from his footprint" is a syllable short in the Tibetan, and "earth" is supplied from context. Kanaka is probably datura; mālatī is jasmine; ariṣṭa is the soapnut tree. The text frames these destructive rites as permissible only against those who harm the Three Jewels and only for one with the mind of awakening, which it states explicitly. The plant names barahe, ricira, patra, keśinī, sukari, and balakata are given in transliteration because they cannot be identified with confidence; the line describing them as found "where perfect awakening abides" is literal and obscure. "Houses of bell metal" renders khang pa li ba, whose sense is uncertain. "The very essence of that vetāla" is taken to mean its heart mantra. "Brought low by sickness and by masters" renders nad dang gtso bos sma phab pa; the sense of gtso bo here is uncertain. "Without attachment" renders brtse ldan ma yin, literally "not affectionate." The closing summary lines on the noose rites are elliptical in the Tibetan and are rendered closely. "For the purpose of the rite" renders bu don cho ga, literally "the rite for the sake of a son"; the sense is uncertain. "Fontanelle" takes mtshog ma in that sense. The implement called se ral kha could not be identified with confidence and is left in Tibetan form. "A fresh one" reads sar pa as gsar pa. "Pigments containing no glue" reads སྦྱིན་མེད as སྤྱིན་མེད (without glue), the evident sense; as written སྦྱིན would mean "giving." The "threefold rite" (ཆོ་ག་ལས་གསུམ) is not further specified in this piece; it probably refers to the three stages just described (approach recitation, mountaintop recitation, and the observance). The final line is a volume marker of the Kangyur edition rather than part of the tantra. "Urmo iron" (ཨུར་མོའི་ལྕགས) is left as a name since the exact kind of iron or steel meant is uncertain. "Incense of śata wood" reads ཤ་ཏ་ཤིང་གི་བདག་པོ as a slip for བདུག་པ (incense); as written བདག་པོ ("lord") makes no sense here, and the identity of śata wood is itself uncertain. "Reciting eight times" (བརྒྱད་བརྗོད) translates the text as written; a reading "a hundred" is possible but not attested here. "Eight hundred leagues" reads དཔག་ཚད་བརྒྱད་བརྒྱན as བརྒྱད་བརྒྱ, the evident sense; as written བརྒྱན ("adorned") does not fit. "Surrounded by their own mounts" takes གཞོན་པ as a spelling of བཞོན་པ (mount, vehicle); the line is obscure. In "undefeated" ཕམ་པ has its ordinary sense of defeat in battle, not the monastic downfall. "Intensely hot" reads ཚར་གྱུར as ཚ་བར་གྱུར, the evident sense. "Human fat" renders ཚིལ་ཆེན (literally "great fat," Skt. mahāvasā), the standard tantric term for human fat. "The great arrow" renders མདའ་བོ་ཆེ, probably the iron nārāca arrow. "During the waning fortnight" reads the obscure མར་གྱི་ང་ཡི as མར་ངོའི, the evident sense in this ritual context; this is uncertain. "The Fair Goddess" translates ལྷ་ཡི་བཟང་མོ, a yakṣiṇī whose Sanskrit name could not be identified with confidence; in the quoted address the text has བཟང་པོ, taken as the same name. "Fragments" renders གཟེགས་མ, whose precise substance here is unclear. Kaṅraṅgi is given as the Tibetan transliterates the name (ཀང་རང་གི); its Sanskrit form is uncertain. "One executed on a stake" renders གསལ་ཤིང་གིས་བསད, a person killed by impalement. "The great flesh" (mahāmāṃsa) is the tantric term for human flesh. In "they give to him, and not to others," the Tibetan reads byad kyis, taken as a slip for byed kyi. In the red ochre rite, dge bdag ni is read as dge ba ni ("wholesome"), and 'dri bar bya ("ask") is read as 'bri bar bya ("write"), since the rite has the target's name written with the ochre cloth. The sense of the closing phrase kha ru is unclear and has been left out of the rendering. "Sita wood" renders si ta'i shing, a wood not identified with confidence. "Earthquakes and the gathering of clouds" reads the bdag of sa g.yo bdag sprin as a slip for dang. "One seized by a black one" most likely means a victim of a black snake, in keeping with the reference to poison just before. "Bringing down into a vessel" is the rite of making a deity descend into a medium, here a youth, for divination. "So-and-so" renders the Tibetan placeholder for the target's name, to be replaced by the actual name in the mantra. The short line zhi bdag gi cho ga yi is read as zhi ba'i cho ga yi ("by the rite of pacifying"). "Very vicious" reads rab 'dug pa as rab gdug pa. "Except to stir suffering in his heart and so purify him" is a tentative rendering of a terse line. "Lac-tree wood" renders rgya skyegs shing, the tree that yields lac, probably the palāśa. In the passage on the finest food, the Tibetan repeats the line "from him one obtains the supreme of desires and wealth as well," which has been kept as written. "Paints that contain no glue" reads sbyin med as spyin med. In "When he is wrathful, the earth splits," the line is a syllable short and "the earth" is supplied from the parallel passage earlier in the text. The line on the tiger-skin garment (man bas par dgyes) is obscure; it is rendered by its evident sense. "Then on a fine patch of ground" reads the evident དེ་ཚེ་ས་གཞི for the written དེ་ཚེས་གཞི. The phrase ར་པ་བཀྲུས་པ describing the cloth is obscure; "washed along its border" is a tentative rendering. The single syllable of Yamāntaka referred to throughout is hūṃ, as the preceding piece states. "Mantra jewel" renders སྔགས་ཀྱི་ནོར་བུ literally; in context it most likely denotes the recitation rosary or its bead. The line འདི་དག་སྦྱིན་ནི་བསྒྲུབ་བསྟན་ཡིན is terse and its rendering "these are taught as the practices that grant them" is tentative. In the closing verses of chapter thirteen, དེ་ཡི་དབང་པོ could also be read as "his lords" rather than "his senses." In the series of syllables added after hūṃ, ཚིག is transcribed as a syllable to fit the pattern, though it could also be read as the ordinary word "word," giving "the word hūṃ." The substances listed as ba bla and btsag are taken as orpiment and red ochre. In "even cloth will swiftly split," the word རས (cloth) is kept as written although the parallel lines suggest a hard material. In the list of the effects of the six maṇḍalas, the text reads རི་མོ་མང་པོ ("Many Images") where the list of names has མི་རོ་མང ("Many Corpses"); the transposed syllables are taken as a slip and rendered "Many Corpses." The ornament སེ་རལ་ཁ is rendered as an ornament worn across the body; its exact form is uncertain. The title of the rite, དོན་གྲུབ, is rendered "the Accomplishment of Aims." "Placing his mind upon the meaning" reads the written བུ་དོན as the evident དེ་དོན or a similar phrase; the written form makes no clear sense. "The lesser ones he obtains with a hundred" reads རྒྱ as བརྒྱ ("a hundred"). The couplet beginning "even what is held fast by the dark one" is obscure, and its rendering is tentative. "Grateful" renders གཟོ་བ as written; since an artisan (བཟོ་བོ) is paid later in the rite, it may be a slip for བཟོ་བ, "an artisan."
