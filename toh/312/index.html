About this text

Toh 312 in the Kangyur is a large sūtra known in English as The Great Sūtra of Entering the Noble City of Vaiśālī, in Sanskrit as the Ārya Vaiśālī Praveśa Mahāsūtra, and in Tibetan as Phags pa yangs pa'i grong khyer du 'jug pa'i mdo chen po. It belongs to the general class of Mahāyāna sūtras and recounts teachings given by the Buddha as he journeyed to and dwelt in the celebrated city of Vaiśālī, a place of great significance in early Buddhist history, arriving at the mango grove of Ambapālī in the company of his attendant Ānanda. The text opens with the Buddha setting out from a resting place among the reeds of a marsh, suggesting a narrative connected to the final period of his life and perhaps to events associated with his last testament. As part of the Kangyur, it preserves canonical teachings attributed directly to the Buddha and transmitted through the traditional formula of dependent witnessing.

Translator's notes

The passage opens with what appears to be the closing colophon of a preceding text before launching into the sūtra proper. The phrase rendered "last testament" translates ཞལ་ཆེམས, which literally means "testament from the mouth," a standard term for the final words or testament of a great master; here it refers to the Blessed One's last instructions.

The epithet "Great God of great gods, the god above all gods" applied to the buddha is a deliberate Buddhist appropriation and supersession of Śiva's titles; the Tibetan ལྷ་ཆེན་པོ་ལྷའི་ཡང་ལྷ literally means "the Great God, the god even above the gods." This should be understood as a polemical formula asserting the buddha's supremacy over all divine beings, not as an identification of the buddha with Śiva.

The passage containing muñcata and nirgacchata preserves Sanskrit imperative forms transliterated into Tibetan: muñcata means "release" or "let go," here rendered "desist"; nirgacchata means "go forth" or "depart." These are standard elements of epidemic-averting dhāraṇī literature, addressed to disease-causing spirits.

The section beginning with sumu through hisimiri is pure dhāraṇī syllabary and is untranslatable by design; these syllables are reproduced phonetically as they appear in the Tibetan transliteration. The passage is a dhāraṇī text and contains extended mantra syllables in the opening section. These are transliterated rather than translated, as is standard for such material. The Tibetan spellings have been rendered into their closest Sanskrit phonetic equivalents following the conventions established in the preceding passage.

The phrase rendered "seat of awakening" in the verse about the six grounds shaking translates the implied reference to the Vajrāsana at Bodh Gayā; the Tibetan here reads simply "at the seat of awakening" (byang chub snying por), which is the standard designation. The passage contains an unusual phrase rendered as "the Buddha, the Great God among gods and the god above gods, the teacher of gods." The Tibetan reads "sangs rgyas lha chen po lha'i yang lha / lha'i bla ma," which is a standard Buddhist epithet sequence praising the Buddha as supreme over all divine beings, including Mahādeva (Śiva). Here "lha chen po" functions as a superlative title applied to the Buddha himself, not as a reference to Śiva as a separate deity; the gloss "(Śiva)" has therefore been omitted in this instance to avoid misidentifying the Buddha with Śiva. The sequence means "the Buddha, the great god, the god even above gods, the teacher of gods," a well-attested Indic honorific formula.

The mantra syllables throughout this passage are transliterated directly from the Tibetan phonetic rendering. The underlying Sanskrit forms of many syllables, particularly in the long dhāraṇī sequence, are uncertain or corrupt in the transmitted Tibetan text, and no attempt has been made to normalize them beyond faithful phonetic rendering. The text breaks off mid-sentence at the final verse, as the passage ends partway through. This is a textual boundary, not a translation lacuna.

The phrase rendered "swift-moving teachers" for སྟོན་པ་རྒྱུག་པ་རྣམས་ཀྱི་རྟེན is somewhat unusual; རྒྱུག་པ could mean "running" in the sense of those who actively travel to teach, or possibly refers to a class of teacher-figures. The sense of "support and basis for teachers who go forth" is preserved in the rendering.

The stanza about "the great hero" whose birth brings prosperity connects to traditional accounts of the Buddha's birth and the accompanying cosmic events, as does the stanza about the shaking of the earth at the seat of awakening.
