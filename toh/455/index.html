About this text

The Analysis of the Secret Tantras, Called the Taming of the Three Fierce Ones, known in Sanskrit as the Vibhaṅgaguhyatantrānāṃ Trirudradamanaṃ and in Tibetan as Gsang ba'i rgyud rnams kyi rnam par 'byed pa drag po gsum 'dul, is a tantric text belonging to the Kangyur, where it is catalogued as Toh 455. The text concerns the analysis and classification of secret tantras, framed around the taming of three fierce or wrathful beings, and opens with Vajrapāṇi inquiring into the secret meaning of practice, abode, and characteristics as declared by the awakened buddha. Its verses describe a fearsome, multi-faced and multi-armed deity set amid dramatic landscapes including iron-peak mountains, dense forests, and stūpas, situating the work firmly within the tradition of wrathful tantric practice. The text was translated from Sanskrit into Tibetan by an Indian master together with the monk-translator Dharma Sangpo.

The bodhisattva Vajrapāṇi, acting in accordance with the Blessed One's command, made this request to the Blessed One: "When one has transgressed the sacred commitments, the activities are not accomplished — they are without purity and without purpose. Where then will the result arise?" The Blessed One replied: it becomes an inexhaustible fruit of suffering in the vajra hell.

Vajrapāṇi then asked the Blessed One: "For how great a measure of time will one experience suffering there?" The Blessed One replied: "Excellent! Excellent, O Vajrapāṇi, great bodhisattva, great being! Do not be afraid. This has distressed the hearts of all the Tathāgatas, and by acting contrary to their minds, one will be liberated at the very moment Vajrāsana, the seat of awakening, is shaken apart."

Vajrapāṇi asked: "Blessed One, when will Vajrāsana be destroyed?" The Blessed One replied: "When all sentient beings throughout the realm of space have attained the fruit of buddhahood, Vajrāsana will be destroyed and liberation will come. The intermediate state and the places of birth will also be destroyed."

Translator's notes

The opening line is a colophon fragment indicating a prior Indian paṇḍita collaborated with the Tibetan translator Dharma Sangpo; the paṇḍita's name appears to be incomplete or damaged in the source.

The term rendered "scent-eaters" for "dri za" refers to gandharvas, celestial musicians in Indian cosmology who are said to subsist on fragrances.

The mantra passage at the close is a transliteration of Sanskrit preserved phonetically in Tibetan; the translation renders it back into reconstructed Sanskrit rather than attempting a semantic translation, as is standard for dhāraṇī and mantra material.

The iconographic description of the central deity with three faces, six arms, and associated attributes strongly evokes Vajrapāṇi in a fierce Trailokyavijaya-related form, consistent with the text's title referencing the taming of the three fierce ones, which in this tantric context likely refers to the subjugation of Śiva, Brahmā, and Indra or related groups of powerful beings. The phrase rendered "born of the ocean" translates a term for one who arises from or belongs to the ocean — in context a Ratnasambhava-family epithet, possibly referring to Vajrapāṇi's jewel-ocean aspect.

The five stanzas of praise correspond structurally to the five buddha families and their associated wisdoms: dharmadhātu wisdom (Vairocana), mirror-like wisdom (Akṣobhya), discriminating wisdom (Amitābha), wisdom of equality (Ratnasambhava), and all-accomplishing wisdom (Amoghasiddhi). The three-faced, six-armed form throughout is Vajrapāṇi in his wrathful aspect.
